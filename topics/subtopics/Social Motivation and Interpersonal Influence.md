# Social Motivation and Interpersonal Influence

## Scope

This topic gathers the parts of the vault where behavior is shaped by fairness, altruism, reciprocity, trust, cooperation, coordination, convention, self-serving interpretation, and interpersonal influence rather than by narrow self-interest alone. In the current vault, the key pages are [[Social preferences]], [[Fairness]], [[Trust]], [[Cooperation]], [[Coordination games]], [[Convention]], [[Reciprocity]], [[Self-serving bias]], and [[Motivated reasoning]], with [[Rabin 1998]] serving as the broad psychology-and-economics source for this social-motivation branch.

## Current focus

The topic now has a game-theory entry path. [[Experimental games]] is the methodological hub. [[Ultimatum game]], [[Dictator game]], and [[Trust game]] introduce the experimental settings through which fairness, giving, trust, reciprocity, and betrayal became economically visible. [[Public goods game]] adds the collective-action side: [[Free riding]] creates the standard problem, [[Conditional cooperation]] explains why cooperation can decay without universal selfishness, and [[Costly punishment]] shows how norm enforcement can stabilize contribution. [[Coordination games]] adds the Schelling branch: even when interests are aligned, agents may still need focal clues, conventions, labels, and mutually consistent expectations to make their actions fit together. [[Lewis 1969]] then turns that into the [[Convention]] branch by showing how recurrent coordination solutions become stable social regularities through [[Common knowledge]]. [[Cultural variation in social preferences]] then adds the anthropological correction: these motives and coordination cues are real, but their expression varies across societies and local social worlds. [[Inequity aversion]] and [[Reciprocity]] show two ways social-preference patterns are formalized: as concern with unequal outcomes or as response to perceived kindness and unkindness.

A later drafting pass should connect this branch more fully to field settings: labor markets, bargaining, compliance, institutional legitimacy, and public policy.
