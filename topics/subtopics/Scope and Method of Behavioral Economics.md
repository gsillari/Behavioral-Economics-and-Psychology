# Scope and Method of Behavioral Economics

## Scope

This subtopic gathers the field-level and methodological questions that determine what behavioral economics studies, what counts as evidence, and how deep its challenge to standard economics is. The main concept pages are [[Field Evidence in Behavioral Economics]], the preference-side mechanisms [[Time inconsistency]], [[Reference dependence]], and [[Social preferences]], plus [[Nonstandard beliefs]], [[Nonstandard decision-making]], [[Strategic response to behavioral anomalies]], and [[Regression to the mean]].

The first methodological question is about scope. [[Rabin 1998]] gives the branch its clearest early architecture by separating richer preferences, biased beliefs, and challenges to stable maximization. [[Camerer and Loewenstein 2004]] then presents behavioral economics as a research program that keeps economics' formal discipline while making the psychological assumptions more realistic.

The second question is about evidence. Behavioral economics moves between laboratory anomalies, field evidence, experiments, surveys, and institutional cases. [[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]] is useful here because it distinguishes judgment, preference, choice, heterogeneity, and intervention rather than treating the field as one undifferentiated list of biases.

The third question is about method. [[Friedman 1953]] supplies the older predictive and assumption-tolerant benchmark. [[Berg and Gigerenzer 2010]] argues that behavioral economics may preserve more of this `as-if` methodology than it admits, even when it uses psychologically richer labels. [[Smith 1991]] adds a different warning: rationality may be mediated by institutions, learning, and interaction, not located only in isolated conscious calculation.

The fourth question is historical and sociological. [[Sent 2004]] reconstructs the venues, actors, and professional conditions that made a particular version of behavioral economics successful. [[Hands 2010]] complicates the standard "psychology was expelled and then returned" story by linking [[Ordinal utility theory]], [[Revealed preference theory]], and [[Behaviorism]]. [[Bruni and Sugden 2007]] widens that story through the [[Paretian turn]], where economics defined itself as a separate science of rational choice. [[Angner 2019]] and [[Angner and Loewenstein 2012 - Behavioral Economics (in Maki 2012, ch. 22)]] then raise the later question of whether behavioral economics was assimilated by the mainstream or whether the mainstream itself became behaviorally methodological.

## Current focus

The current focus is to keep four questions visible for students:

1. What departures count as behavioral economics: preferences, beliefs, decision processes, welfare mistakes, or institutional failures?
2. What evidence travels from laboratory anomaly to field relevance?
3. How much of the standard optimization architecture is revised, and how much is retained with more realistic parameters?
4. Which version of behavioral economics became professionally successful, and what alternatives were marginalized by that success?

This subtopic therefore sits between [[Foundations and Rationality]] and the rest of the vault. It asks not only what behavioral economics says, but what kind of research program it is.
