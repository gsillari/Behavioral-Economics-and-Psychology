# Scope and Method of Behavioral Economics

## Scope

This topic will gather the field-level and methodological questions that determine what behavioral economics studies and how strongly its claims should be taken. In the current vault, the main anchor pages are [[Field Evidence in Behavioral Economics]], [[Nonstandard preferences]], [[Nonstandard beliefs]], [[Nonstandard decision-making]], [[Strategic response to behavioral anomalies]], and [[Regression to the mean]]. The strongest source-level orientation pages for this branch are now [[Camerer and Loewenstein 2004]], [[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]], [[Berg and Gigerenzer 2010]], [[Sent 2004]], [[Angner 2019]], and [[Angner and Loewenstein 2012 - Behavioral Economics (in Maki 2012, ch. 22)]]: the first maps behavioral economics as a research program, the second maps mature judgment-and-decision research around judgment, preference, choice, heterogeneity, and intervention, the third asks whether behavioral economics changed economics' method as much as it often claims, the fourth reconstructs the profession-level story of how the field became institutionally successful through journals, conferences, prizes, foundations, and a strategically limited accommodation with the mainstream, the fifth argues that the supposed synthesis with neoclassical economics is already behaviorally framed, and the sixth gives a philosophy-of-economics overview of the field from inside the behavioral camp itself. [[Friedman 1953]] now adds a useful earlier benchmark from outside the behavioral tradition, because [[Essays in Positive Economics - Essay - The Methodology of Positive Economics]] makes explicit the predictive, assumption-tolerant methodology that later behavioral economics often pushes against. Berg and Gigerenzer sharpen that point by arguing that much of behavioral economics still relies on the same `as-if` logic even when it adds psychologically richer labels and parameters.

This branch matters because behavioral economics is not only a set of theories. It is also a research program with empirical scope conditions, domain variation, benchmark choices, methodological constraints, and a professional history. One of the key questions now visible in the vault is whether behavioral economics mainly replaced unrealistic assumptions with better psychology, or whether large parts of it preserved the old optimization architecture while repairing it with behaviorally named adjustments. Sent adds a second question that should stay visible here: what kind of behavioral economics could become mainstream, and what kinds were left behind? Angner adds a third, later question: if the mainstream now freely integrates behavioral factors whenever they improve explanation or policy, should that be described as assimilation of behavioral economics or as its victory? [[Hands 2010]] adds a fourth background question that should now stay visible as well: what exactly does it mean to say that economics once drove psychology out? Hands' answer is that the history of consumer theory is better read as a selective shift among different psychologies, with [[Ordinal utility theory]] and especially [[Revealed preference theory]] marking different stages of that sorting process and with [[Behaviorism]] playing a larger role than the standard textbook story suggests. [[Bruni and Sugden 2007]] pushes the background wider still by arguing that the problem is not only Samuelson or consumer theory, but the larger [[Paretian turn]] through which economics tried to define itself as a separate science of rational choice independent of psychology. On that reading, behavioral economics is not merely adding new experiments to an unchanged discipline. It is reopening a much older methodological settlement.

## Current focus

The topic page is still relatively light, but it no longer needs to be only a placeholder. A later pass should turn it into a course-facing guide to what the field studies, what kinds of evidence it uses, how it distinguishes judgment from preference from choice, how it moves from lab results to institutional relevance, how it couples analytical and behavioral research when evaluating real decisions, how methodological benchmarks such as [[Friedman 1953]] shaped the background that behavioral economists later revised, why sources such as [[Berg and Gigerenzer 2010]] argue that the revision was only partial, how [[Hands 2010]] complicates the standard `psychology out and then back in` story by linking [[Ordinal utility theory]] and [[Revealed preference theory]] to changing views of scientific psychology and [[Behaviorism]], how [[Bruni and Sugden 2007]] widens that same story into the discipline-level [[Paretian turn]], how [[Sent 2004]] reconstructs the venues, actors, and professional conditions that made the newer, benchmark-based version of behavioral economics succeed, and how [[Angner 2019]] interprets the later synthesis thesis as evidence that the mainstream itself has become behaviorally methodological.
