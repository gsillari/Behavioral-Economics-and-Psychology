# Judgment, Belief, and Inference

This is **Topic 2 of 7** in the wiki's main topic sequence. The full order is: [[Foundations and Rationality]] -> [[Judgment, Belief, and Inference]] -> [[Choice, Preference, and Decision Under Risk]] -> [[Time, Attention, and Choice Architecture]] -> [[Social Behavior, Law, and Public Policy]] -> [[Ecological Rationality and Fast-and-Frugal Heuristics]] -> [[AI, Algorithms, and New Frontiers]].

## Scope

This topic gathers the parts of behavioral economics that focus on how people form judgments, represent uncertainty, draw inferences, and make predictions under cognitive pressure. In the current vault, this is the branch where the classic heuristics literature becomes most visible: similarity-based reasoning, salience, affect, anchoring, mistaken beliefs about control such as [[Illusion of control]], confirmation-biased evidence processing, motivated belief formation, perspective-bound reasoning such as [[Myside bias]], and the contrast between intuitive judgment and more explicit models. [[Wason 1968]] now adds the classic conditional-reasoning paradigm behind the [[Wason selection task]], while [[Cosmides and Tooby 2000]] shows how that paradigm becomes a major dispute over content, social reasoning, and evolved cognitive specialization. [[Kahneman et al. 1982]] now gives this branch a clearer volume-level backbone by collecting the core heuristics chapters together with chapters on calibration, correction, prediction, and perceived risk. [[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]] adds a useful later frame by showing how mature decision science organizes this material around the relationship between task analysis, descriptive evidence, and intervention.

For PPE students, this topic matters because it is where the descriptive psychology of reasoning becomes sharpest. It matters for economics because beliefs and inferences shape expectations, valuation, and response to policy. It matters for philosophy because the branch raises questions about rational inference, evidence, and norm violation. It matters for politics because public judgment about risk, expertise, and uncertainty often depends on exactly these mechanisms.

The branch therefore sits between the abstract benchmark material in [[Foundations and Rationality]] and the later topics on risky choice, welfare, policy, and law. It asks not only whether people depart from formal norms, but how they do so and which recurring inferential patterns matter most. The representativeness branch is now especially important here because it has grown into a fuller internal sequence, from early similarity-based probability judgment to conjunction errors and later process reinterpretation through [[Attribute substitution]]. At the same time, the Fischhoff-Broomell review makes clear that this topic is only one side of a larger judgment-and-decision architecture: judgment belongs in constant relation to preference, choice, and the institutional settings where inferential quality matters.

## Subtopics

### 2.1 [[Representativeness and Statistical Error]]

This branch focuses on similarity-based judgment and the statistical mistakes that follow from it. Its core pages are [[Representativeness heuristic]], [[Base-rate neglect]], [[Law of small numbers]], [[Conjunction fallacy]], and [[Overconfidence]]. The base-rate side is now anchored not only in the Kahneman-Tversky sequence, but also in [[Ajzen 1977]], [[Bar-Hillel 1980]], and [[Fischhoff and Bar-Hillel 1984]], which explain how causal relevance, specificity, and diagnosticity shape whether base rates are used.

### 2.2 [[Availability, Affect, and Risk Perception]]

This branch focuses on salience, feeling, and public risk judgment. Its core pages are [[Availability heuristic]], [[Affect heuristic]], [[Dread risk]], and [[Emotions in decision-making]].

### 2.3 [[Anchoring and Cognitive Substitution]]

This branch focuses on anchoring, adjustment, and the substitution of easier questions for harder ones. Its core pages are [[Anchoring]], [[Anchoring]], and [[Attribute substitution]].

### 2.4 [[Prediction and Models of Judgment]]

This branch focuses on how intuitive judgment compares with simple formal models and structured prediction rules. Its core pages are [[Clinical versus actuarial judgment]] and [[Clinical versus actuarial judgment]].

## Links to the rest of the topic

The point of this topic is to move from broad claims about bounded or biased judgment to more specific inferential mechanisms. The first branch treats statistical distortion and confidence. The second treats salience, affect, and public risk perception. The third examines anchoring and related process accounts. The fourth compares intuitive judgment with explicit predictive models. Cross-cutting those branches are [[Wason selection task]], [[Motivated reasoning]], [[Confirmation bias]], [[Myside bias]], [[Probability neglect]], [[Hindsight bias]], and [[Illusion of control]], which now give the topic a clearer place for conditional reasoning, beliefs shaped by directional goals, selective evidence use, perspective-bound evaluation, fear-driven insensitivity to likelihood, retrospective distortion, and mistaken agency as well as by shortcut-based inference. Together they supply much of the cognitive material that later feeds into choice, policy, and institutional design.

## Open questions

Which inferential mechanisms in this branch are best understood as genuine biases, and which are better modeled as context-sensitive shortcuts? How much should this topic be organized around classical norm violations, and how much around modern process theories? And which of these mechanisms deserve the strongest weight in a PPE-oriented course on behavioral economics?
