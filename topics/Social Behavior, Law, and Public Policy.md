# Social Behavior, Law, and Public Policy

This is **Topic 5 of 7** in the wiki's main topic sequence. The full order is: [[Foundations and Rationality]] -> [[Judgment, Belief, and Inference]] -> [[Choice, Preference, and Decision Under Risk]] -> [[Time, Attention, and Choice Architecture]] -> [[Social Behavior, Law, and Public Policy]] -> [[Ecological Rationality and Fast-and-Frugal Heuristics]] -> [[AI, Algorithms, and New Frontiers]].

## Scope

This topic gathers the parts of behavioral economics that move beyond isolated individual choice and toward social interaction, institutions, legal design, public policy, and political decision-making. It now has two internal halves. The first asks how people behave with and toward one another: social preferences, fairness, trust, cooperation, coordination, convention, and evolutionary game dynamics. The second asks how institutions should respond: public policy, law, welfare, and political economy.

For PPE students, this topic is especially important because it sits at the intersection of all three letters. It matters for economics because it revises assumptions about motivation, law, and institutional response. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about welfare, legitimacy, coercion, and fairness. It matters for politics because it concerns how institutions should react once behavioral findings are taken seriously.

This branch therefore turns the field outward: from how individuals choose to how people interact, how social order stabilizes, and how institutions should understand, constrain, or guide those patterns.

## Open questions

How much of behavioral public policy depends on controversial assumptions about welfare? When should social order be explained by preferences, norms, conventions, or institutions? And how far can behavioral law and economics revise the classical legal-economic benchmark before it becomes a different framework altogether?
