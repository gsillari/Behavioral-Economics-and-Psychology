# Kahneman et al. 1990: Experimental tests of the endowment effect and the Coase theorem
## Summary

[[Kahneman et al. 1990]] is the canonical experimental paper on the [[Endowment effect]] in the current vault. It argues that willingness to accept compensation for giving up a good often exceeds willingness to pay for acquiring the same good, and that this gap persists even in simple market settings where standard theory would predict more convergence through trade.

The paper matters because it ties three important lines together: entitlement-sensitive valuation, [[Loss aversion]] under [[Reference dependence]], and the challenge this poses to the classical bargaining optimism associated with the [[Coase theorem]]. In the current vault, it should anchor the endowment-effect branch directly rather than leaving that branch dependent mainly on later review articles.

## Key claims

The first claim is that randomly assigned ownership changes valuation. People given a mug or another ordinary consumption good tend to demand much more to give it up than nonowners are willing to pay to obtain it.

The second claim is that this is not just a survey artifact. The authors run actual trading situations and show that low trading volume persists even when people have opportunities to exchange goods in a market-like setting.

The third claim is that the pattern is better explained by reference-dependent valuation and loss aversion than by a simple transactions-cost story. In other words, ownership changes the reference point, giving up the endowed good feels like a loss, and that loss is weighted more heavily than an equivalent gain.

## Endowment effect and the Coase theorem

This source is especially important because it brings behavioral economics into direct contact with classical law-and-economics reasoning. If preferences are stable and bargaining is frictionless, then initial endowments should not matter much for efficient reallocation. The paper shows that, for ordinary consumption goods, initial assignment often matters a great deal.

That does not refute the [[Coase theorem]] as a theorem. Instead, it shows that its empirical applicability depends on stronger assumptions about valuation and exchange than are often acknowledged. In the current vault, that makes this source a useful bridge between valuation anomalies and institutional analysis.

## Relation to neighboring concepts

The paper should be read together with [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]], but the two are not identical. [[Endowment effect]] is the ownership-sensitive shift in valuation; [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]] is the more general measurement pattern in which willingness to accept exceeds willingness to pay. This paper is one of the clearest demonstrations of how the former can generate the latter.

It should also be linked closely to [[Loss aversion]] and [[Reference dependence]], because the paper makes the case that endowment is psychologically important largely through the reference point it creates.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows a direct collision between a classical benchmark and a behavioral mechanism. It matters for economics because it challenges simple assumptions about exchange, revealed value, and bargaining. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about whether ownership changes value or merely changes evaluation. It matters for politics and law because compensation, entitlement, and initial assignment of rights become much harder to treat as neutral once endowment effects are taken seriously.

This source is therefore especially useful for showing how behavioral economics moves from laboratory observation to critique of broader institutional reasoning.
