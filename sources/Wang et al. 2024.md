# Wang et al. 2024: Against predictive optimization
## Summary

[[Wang et al. 2024]] is a broad critique of what the authors call [[Predictive optimization]]: decision-making systems that use machine learning to predict future outcomes about individuals and then optimize decisions around those predictions. In the current vault, the paper matters because it widens the fairness branch into a stronger legitimacy critique. The issue is not just biased models. It is the entire institutional strategy of optimizing public or private decisions around predictive scores.

The paper argues that predictive optimization often fails on its own terms. Even when developers promise improved accuracy, efficiency, or fairness, the systems can founder on target mismatch, feedback effects, shifting populations, contestability problems, and hidden value judgments.

## Key claims

The first claim is that predictive optimization is a distinctive category of algorithmic decision-making and should be analyzed as such.

The second claim is that many applications in this category are not justified simply by showing better predictive performance.

The third claim is that normative and technical objections recur across domains and are not easily removed by incremental redesign.

The fourth claim is that the burden of proof should rest on the developers of these systems rather than on critics.

The fifth claim is that legitimacy, validity, and institutional effects are as important as narrow accuracy claims.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Wang et al. 2024]] matters because it turns the algorithmic-bias branch into a more general critique of predictive governance. It matters for politics and law because it asks whether certain classes of predictive systems should be deployed at all. It matters for philosophy because it challenges the legitimacy of optimization around contested targets and values.
