# Bohnet and Zeckhauser 2004: Trust, risk and betrayal

## Summary

[[Bohnet and Zeckhauser 2004]] asks whether trusting another person is just a risky bet or whether betrayal adds a distinct cost. The paper compares a binary [[Trust game]] with structurally matched decision and risky-dictator settings. The key measure is the minimum acceptable probability at which subjects are willing to choose the risky/trusting option over a sure payoff.

The main finding is that first movers require higher acceptable probabilities in the trust condition than in matched non-social risk conditions. The same material payoff distribution is less attractive when the bad outcome is produced by another person choosing to betray trust. That is the basis for the page [[Betrayal aversion]].

## What it adds to the wiki

This paper sharpens the trust branch in two ways. First, it separates [[Trust]] from ordinary [[Risk attitudes]]: a trust decision involves vulnerability to another agent's choice, not merely uncertainty over states of the world. Second, it shows why the [[Trust game]] is not reducible to benevolence or efficiency. People may hesitate to trust not because the expected value is low, but because being let down by another person is psychologically different from losing a lottery.

That makes the paper useful for connecting social preferences to the wider decision-theory architecture. Trust is a strategic and social relation; risk is a probabilistic relation to outcomes. Behavioral economics needs both distinctions.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Bohnet and Zeckhauser 2004]] matters because it distinguishes risk from betrayal in a way that is both experimentally tractable and institutionally important. It matters for economics because trust cannot be modeled as expected value plus ordinary risk aversion alone. It matters for philosophy because betrayal changes the meaning of a bad outcome. It matters for politics and policy because many institutions are designed either to make betrayal less likely or to make trust less necessary.
