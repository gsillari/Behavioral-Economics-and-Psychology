# Knetsch and Sinden 1984: Willingness to pay and compensation demanded
## Summary

[[Knetsch and Sinden 1984]] is an early experimental paper showing that willingness to accept compensation for a loss can greatly exceed willingness to pay to obtain an equivalent gain. In the current vault, the paper matters because it provides an earlier direct anchor for [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]] than the later review literature alone.

Its importance is that it demonstrates the disparity using actual cash payments and compensation rather than relying only on hypothetical survey responses. That gives the valuation branch a cleaner early empirical foothold.

## Key claims

The first claim is that compensation demanded and willingness to pay are often not close substitutes in practice, even when standard reasoning might lead one to expect near equivalence apart from modest income effects.

The second claim is that the gap persists in settings with real stakes. This matters because it weakens the idea that the asymmetry is merely a hypothetical or verbal artifact.

The third claim is that the size of the disparity creates problems for standard interpretations of value, entitlement, and indifference. In the current vault, that makes the paper one of the earliest empirical supports for the later endowment-effect literature.

## Relation to the endowment branch

This source should be read as an early valuation asymmetry paper rather than as a full theory of ownership-sensitive preference. It supports [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]] directly and points toward [[Endowment effect]], but it does not itself carry the same broader theoretical interpretation as [[Kahneman et al. 1990]].

That distinction matters for the current vault. [[Knetsch and Sinden 1984]] gives the early measurement result. [[Kahneman et al. 1990]] then gives the stronger integration of valuation, loss aversion, and market undertrading.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows how a seemingly technical pricing issue becomes a question about rights, compensation, and welfare. It matters for economics because it pressures the assumption that value can be read off in a single stable way from exchange preferences. It matters for philosophy because it raises the question of whether the two valuation measures are tracking the same thing. It matters for politics and policy because public decision-making often depends on compensation rules, damages, and cost-benefit comparisons.

## Open questions

How much of the WTA/WTP gap reflects entitlement, how much reflects elicitation method, and how much reflects broader instability in preference expression? Which institutional contexts make this asymmetry most consequential?
