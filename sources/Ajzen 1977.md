# Ajzen 1977: Intuitive theories of events and base rates

## Summary

[[Ajzen 1977]] is an important early refinement of the base-rate literature. The paper argues that people do not neglect all base rates uniformly. They use information when it fits an intuitive causal theory of the event being predicted, and they neglect information that appears statistically valid but causally unrelated to the target outcome.

In the current vault, the paper matters because it complicates a flat reading of [[Base-rate neglect]]. Base rates are not merely invisible to intuitive judges. They are more likely to matter when they can be interpreted as causally connected to the case, and less likely to matter when they look like coincidental background frequencies.

## Key claims

The first claim is that prediction is guided by intuitive theories of the event. People ask, implicitly, what sort of information could have produced or explained the outcome, and then give that information more weight.

The second claim is that causal base rates can affect judgment. When a base rate has causal implications for the criterion, subjects are much more willing to use it in prediction.

The third claim is that noncausal base rates are more vulnerable to neglect. When base rates appear merely statistical or coincidental, individuating information can dominate even when the base rate is normatively relevant.

## Why it matters for the vault

This source helps the vault avoid a too-simple story in which base-rate neglect is just a failure to notice frequencies. It shows that the perceived meaning of the frequency matters. The key question is not only whether a base rate is supplied, but whether it can be incorporated into the judge's causal model of the case.

That makes the paper an important bridge between [[Base-rate neglect]], [[Representativeness heuristic]], and [[Nonstandard beliefs]]. It also anticipates the later Bar-Hillel emphasis on relevance and specificity: base rates regain force when they are interpreted as genuinely bearing on the target case rather than as detached population statistics.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows that the rational use of statistical information is not only a computational problem. It is also an interpretive problem about what evidence is taken to mean. That makes the source relevant to law, policy, and institutional design whenever people must decide whether population-level information should bear on an individual case.
