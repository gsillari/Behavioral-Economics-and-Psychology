# Allais 1953: Le comportement de l'homme rationnel devant le risque
## Summary

[[Allais 1953]] is an early and influential challenge to the descriptive adequacy of [[Expected utility theory]]. The paper is best known in the later literature for the preference pattern now called the [[Allais paradox]], in which choices between lotteries violate the independence axiom central to expected-utility reasoning. In this vault, the source matters as a precursor that predates the Kahneman-Tversky program while helping explain why later theories such as [[Prospect Theory]] gained traction.

## Key claims

The key claim is that actual preferences under risk can conflict with the axiomatic structure of expected utility even when the options are well specified. The point is not merely that people make mistakes; it is that the standard theory may fail descriptively in a systematic way. The source is therefore central to the historical distinction between expected utility as a normative benchmark and expected utility as an empirical model of how people choose.

## Evidence and methods

The paper proceeds by comparing structured lottery choices and showing that plausible preferences can violate the independence condition. In later discussions this becomes the [[Allais paradox]]: a stable reversal pattern across lottery pairs that cannot be reconciled with standard expected-utility axioms. In the current vault, the paper is treated primarily as a foundational conceptual and decision-theoretic challenge rather than as part of the later experimental heuristics tradition.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Allais 1953]] is a clean entry point into one of the field's central tensions: the difference between a normative theory of rational choice and a descriptive account of how people actually choose. It matters for economics because it exposes a crack in expected-utility theory from within the economic tradition itself, for philosophy because it raises questions about which axioms of rationality are genuinely compelling, and for politics and policy because those distinctions matter whenever institutions assume citizens will respond to risk in formally consistent ways.

## Open questions

One open question is how much of the paper should be read as a purely descriptive challenge and how much as a broader critique of American decision theory. Another is whether the paradox undermines expected utility as a normative benchmark or only as a descriptive model. A third is how directly later behavioral theories should be seen as answers to Allais rather than as partially independent developments.
