# Wason 1968

## Summary

[[Wason 1968]] is the canonical article behind what later became known as the [[Wason selection task]]. The task asks subjects to test a conditional rule of the form "if P, then Q" by choosing which visible cards must be turned over. The logically relevant cards are P and not-Q, because only a case with P and not-Q can falsify the rule. Wason found that subjects usually choose P and Q instead: they inspect the named antecedent and the confirming consequent, while often failing to inspect the falsifying not-Q case.

In the current vault, the paper matters because it gives [[Confirmation bias]] a foundational reasoning-task anchor. The point is not only that people like evidence that confirms their expectations. It is that even in an abstract task where falsification is logically central, subjects often treat rule-testing as a search for correspondence, truth, or matching cases.

## Main claims

The first claim is that conditional reasoning is not automatically governed by the rules of propositional logic. Subjects do not behave as if material implication is transparent to them. They tend to treat the conditional as if matching cases are central and nonmatching cases are irrelevant.

The second claim is that the key difficulty is the contrapositive or falsifying inference. Wason treats the failure to select not-Q as more interesting than the fallacious selection of Q, because not-Q is exactly where the subject must look to discover whether the rule can be false.

The third claim is that this difficulty is resistant to simple correction. Wason tested "therapies" designed to make subjects project falsity or recognize the one falsifying contingency. These interventions did not reliably produce the correct selection pattern. That matters because the error is not just an instruction failure or a momentary lapse.

The fourth claim is developmental and architectural. Wason argues that the subjects' performance sits uneasily with a simple Piagetian expectation that educated adults have fully acquired formal-operational thought. The selection task therefore becomes a compact way to pressure views of rationality that assume adult reasoning naturally tracks formal logic.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Wason 1968]] matters because it is a small experimental paradigm with large philosophical consequences. It turns a logical benchmark into a psychological test, and it shows why the interpretation of "irrationality" is delicate. If subjects fail the abstract task, is the problem a bias toward confirmation, a mismatch between ordinary language and material implication, a limit of formal reasoning, or the absence of the right pragmatic context?

That last question is why the paper becomes so important for later work. [[Cosmides and Tooby 2000]] uses the same task to argue that performance changes dramatically when the conditional describes a social contract and the task becomes one of detecting cheaters. [[Fast and slow thinking]] and the later [[Rationality wars]] then use this family of findings to ask whether reasoning failure reflects a general weakness of intuition, a conflict between intuitive and reflective processing, or a sign that rationality should be evaluated relative to domain and function.
