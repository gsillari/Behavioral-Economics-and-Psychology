# Ellsberg 1961: Risk, ambiguity, and the Savage axioms
## Summary

[[Ellsberg 1961]] is a foundational challenge to the idea that rational choice under uncertainty can always be represented by a single sharp subjective probability distribution. Daniel Ellsberg shows that people often prefer known risks to unknown probabilities, even when standard subjective expected utility theory implies indifference or consistent treatment across the cases. In the current vault, the paper matters because it adds a belief-side anomaly to the classical benchmark: not only preferences, but also responses to uncertainty itself can put pressure on formal rationality.

## Key claims

The paper's central claim is that there is an important difference between risk and ambiguity. Risk involves known probabilities; ambiguity involves uncertainty about the probabilities themselves. Ellsberg argues that many people systematically prefer risky options with known chances over ambiguous options with unknown chances, and that this pattern conflicts with the Savage-style axioms used to extend expected utility reasoning into uncertain environments. In the current vault, this matters because it complicates the transition from [[Subjective probability]] to rational choice: even if probabilities can in principle be subjective, actual agents may resist acting as if all uncertainty can be compressed into a single coherent credence.

## Evidence and methods

This is a conceptual and decision-theoretic paper rather than an empirical field study. Ellsberg develops stylized choice problems designed to reveal stable preferences for known over unknown probabilities, and then uses those examples to argue that the standard axiomatic framework is descriptively and perhaps normatively incomplete. In the current vault, the paper functions as a benchmark-pressure source on the uncertainty side of rational choice, parallel to the way [[Allais 1953]] pressures expected utility on the preference side.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Ellsberg 1961]] matters because it sharpens a basic question at the intersection of economics and philosophy: what does rationality require when probabilities are not well specified? It matters for economics because much formal reasoning about uncertainty assumes that agents can behave as if they had determinate subjective probabilities. It matters for philosophy because it raises deep questions about ambiguity, confidence, and whether coherence alone is enough for rational belief and action. It matters for politics because public decision-making often occurs under precisely the kind of uncertainty Ellsberg highlights, from regulation and war to public health and technological risk. The paper helps show that behavioral economics does not only challenge preference consistency. It also challenges the assumption that uncertainty can always be domesticated into well-behaved probabilities.
