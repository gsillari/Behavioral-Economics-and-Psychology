# Skyrms 2014: Evolution of the Social Contract

## Work overview

[[Skyrms 2014]] is the second edition of Brian Skyrms's *Evolution of the Social Contract*. The original book appeared in 1996; the 2014 edition revises and updates several chapters, especially the discussions of correlation, ultimatum bargaining, inclusive fitness, and signaling games.

The book belongs to the Hume-Rousseau line of social-contract thinking rather than the Hobbes-Harsanyi-Rawls line. Its central question is not what fully rational parties would choose behind a veil of ignorance, but how social contracts, conventions, fairness norms, cooperation, ownership patterns, and meaning can emerge through biological or cultural evolutionary dynamics.

## Central theses

The book's organizing thesis is that rational-choice and evolutionary explanations can come apart. Natural or cultural selection does not simply weed out irrationality and leave the same answers as classical game theory. Once strategies replicate, mutate, recombine, correlate, or cluster locally, evolutionary dynamics can select outcomes that rational equilibrium analysis either leaves underdetermined or classifies as anomalous.

The first major mechanism is the "Darwinian veil of ignorance." In repeated population processes, individual identities disappear and strategies compete through their average success. This can select fair division in symmetric bargaining more sharply than the ordinary Nash-equilibrium analysis, which leaves many strict equilibria.

The second major mechanism is correlation. If like interacts with like through kinship, partner choice, signaling, repeated interaction, local clustering, or group structure, cooperation and altruism can be favored even where uncorrelated one-shot rational choice points toward defection. Correlation is therefore not an incidental complication; it is one of the book's main explanatory devices.

The third mechanism is symmetry-breaking. In coordination and hawk-dove situations, external cues, ownership roles, local noise, learning, and cultural transmission can transform symmetric mixed equilibria into correlated conventions. This links the book directly to [[Schelling 1960]] and [[Lewis 1969]], but with an evolutionary dynamic rather than a primarily rational-choice or common-knowledge account.

## Internal tensions or debates

Skyrms is careful not to make a Panglossian argument. Evolution can favor fair division and cooperation under some conditions, but it can also fall into polymorphic traps, support inefficient equilibria, or preserve strategies that fail sequential rationality. The point is not that evolution guarantees morality; the point is that dynamics can explain how some social norms become stable, why others fail, and why the relation between rationality and evolution is nontrivial.

The book also creates a useful tension with universalist interpretations of fairness experiments. Chapter 2 treats costly rejection in the ultimatum game as evolutionarily possible, but the second edition also incorporates anthropological evidence showing substantial cultural variation in offers and rejections.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Skyrms 2014]] is a foundational source for the evolutionary analysis of institutions. It shows how fairness, property, cooperation, convention, and social contract can be studied without assuming either explicit agreement or centralized enforcement. That makes it an important successor to [[Schelling 1960]] and [[Lewis 1969]] for the institutional side of game theory.

It is also important for behavioral economics because it reframes several "anomalies." Costly rejection, ownership-sensitive behavior, and cooperation are not merely failures of narrow rational choice; they may be products of evolutionary and cultural dynamics operating over recurring strategic environments.
