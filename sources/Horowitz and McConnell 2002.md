# Horowitz and McConnell 2002: A review of WTA/WTP studies
## Summary

[[Horowitz and McConnell 2002]] reviews the literature on willingness to accept and willingness to pay and shows that the two often diverge substantially. In the current vault, the source matters because it gives the valuation branch a disciplined overview rather than leaving the issue scattered across isolated examples. It is one of the clearest sources for why welfare measurement becomes difficult once valuation depends on ownership, entitlement, and framing.

The paper is especially useful because it does not merely restate that WTA exceeds WTP. It compares results across classes of goods and study designs and argues that the gap is systematically larger for less ordinary market goods. That gives the vault a more structured account of [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]] and its implications.

## Key claims

The first claim is that willingness to accept is typically higher than willingness to pay, often by more than standard theory would predict. The second claim is that the size of the gap varies systematically with the type of good: it is larger for nonmarket and unusual goods than for more familiar market-like goods.

The third claim is that the asymmetry cannot be dismissed simply as a trivial artifact of hypothetical elicitation. In the current vault, that matters because it strengthens the challenge to clean welfare inference from observed or stated valuation.

## Evidence and methods

This is a review article rather than a single experiment. Horowitz and McConnell synthesize a large set of valuation studies and compare patterns across good types, elicitation methods, and experimental conditions. The paper therefore functions as an organizing source for a dispersed empirical literature.

That makes it especially valuable for the vault. [[Ariely et al. 2003]] and [[Ariely et al. 2006]] already raise doubts about stable valuation and revealed preference. This source adds a more classical valuation-literature route to the same problem.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Horowitz and McConnell 2002]] matters because it shows that valuation is not a neutral window onto welfare. It matters for economics because cost-benefit analysis, compensation rules, and contingent valuation often lean on the assumption that WTP and WTA track a common underlying value closely enough for practical use. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about entitlement, ownership, welfare, and the normative meaning of preference reversals. It matters for politics because regulation, environmental policy, and legal compensation often depend on whether we ask how much people would pay to gain something or how much they would require to give it up.

The paper is particularly useful for PPE because it forces students to see that welfare measurement is partly institutional and conceptual, not only technical.

## Links into the wiki

The main derived concepts are [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]] and [[Endowment effect]]. The source also deepens [[Loss aversion]] by connecting valuation gaps to reference-dependent ownership effects, strengthens [[Constructed preferences]] by showing that elicited valuation is context-sensitive, and belongs near [[Behavioral public policy]] because policy often needs monetary measures of welfare. The broader topical home is [[Behavioral Economics]].

## Open questions

How much of the WTA/WTP gap is best explained by loss aversion, and how much by market experience, substitution possibilities, or procedural features of elicitation? Which policy domains are most vulnerable to mistakes when analysts treat WTP and WTA as interchangeable?
