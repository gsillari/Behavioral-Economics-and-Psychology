# Jolls and Sunstein 2006: Debiasing through law

## Summary

[[Jolls and Sunstein 2006]] asks what legal systems should do once bounded rationality is taken seriously. Its key contribution is to distinguish between insulating outcomes from the effects of bias and trying to reduce the bias itself through legal design. In the current vault, that makes it the clearest bridge from the classic debiasing literature into law, regulation, and institutional design.

The paper matters because it turns debiasing into a public-policy question rather than leaving it at the level of laboratory instruction or individual training. It shows that law can sometimes work by changing the informational or procedural environment so that people judge more well, not merely by restricting choice or imposing outcomes.

## Key claims

The first claim is that bounded rationality can be addressed in two quite different ways. One can insulate legal outcomes from its effects, or one can try to debias through law by steering legal actors in more rational directions.

The second claim is that many existing and possible legal strategies already fit the second model. The paper examines both procedural and substantive legal interventions that attempt to reduce judgment error rather than simply work around it.

The third claim is that debiasing through law carries risks of overshooting and manipulation. Legal interventions that change salience, framing, or available evidence can improve judgment, but they can also distort the choices of those who were not previously erring.

## Evidence and methods

This is a synthetic law-and-policy article. It combines the behavioral literature on optimism, availability, framing, and expected-utility departures with legal examples drawn from consumer safety, corporate law, property, and adjudication. Its method is architectural: it builds a policy vocabulary for legal debiasing rather than testing one intervention in isolation.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows how a psychological idea becomes an institutional one. It matters for economics and law because it expands the menu of legal responses beyond simple paternalistic blocking or laissez-faire deference. It matters for philosophy because it forces a legitimacy question: when is it acceptable for law to change how citizens perceive and process the world in order to reduce error? And it matters for politics because it helps explain why many legal rules are neither neutral nor merely coercive, but cognitively structured.

## Open questions

When should law try to debias rather than simply protect people from the effects of error? How much manipulation risk is acceptable if a legal intervention improves judgment on average? And how should the vault distinguish debiasing-through-law from nearby ideas such as nudging, disclosure, and asymmetric paternalism?
