# Simon 1955: A behavioral model of rational choice
## Summary

[[Simon 1955]] is one of the deepest foundation stones in this vault. The paper matters not because it catalogs a bias or proposes a narrow empirical effect, but because it changes the benchmark itself. Instead of starting from an ideal chooser with complete information, stable ordering over alternatives, and effectively unlimited computational power, Simon asks what rational choice should look like once those heroic assumptions are dropped.

Retrospectively, this is one of the canonical founding texts of [[Bounded rationality]]. Even where later behavioral economics takes very different empirical and theoretical forms, the background question is often still Simon's: what conception of rationality is compatible with the actual informational and computational situation of human agents?

## Key claims

The first central claim is that the "global rationality" of standard economic man is not an adequate model of real choice. Rationality should be modeled in ways that fit the information people actually have, the alternatives they actually notice, and the computations they can actually perform.

The second claim is that choice should be analyzed procedurally rather than only by its final ordering over outcomes. Simon therefore shifts attention toward search, consideration, aspiration levels, and feasible decision rules. This is a major conceptual move: the theory of rationality becomes a theory of how agents cope with complexity, not only a theory of what fully informed maximization would require.

The third claim is that agents often do not optimize across all available alternatives. They work with a reduced set of considered options and may select the first alternative that meets an acceptable threshold. In later vocabulary, this is the logic of satisficing. The paper matters because it treats this not as irrational residue but as a realistic structure of choice.

The fourth claim is that aspiration levels can be dynamic. What counts as acceptable may shift as agents encounter offers, search costs, disappointments, and new information. This matters because it opens the door to a behavioral model of choice that is path-dependent and context-sensitive without collapsing into randomness.

## Evidence and methods

The paper is conceptual and formally oriented rather than experimental. Simon does not present the later heuristics-and-biases style of bias demonstrations. Instead, he reconstructs the architecture of rational choice from the ground up under more realistic assumptions about search, computation, and information.

That difference is crucial for this vault. [[Simon 1955]] is not best read as an early bias paper. It is better read as a benchmark-revising paper. Where later behavioral economics often shows that actual judgment deviates from formal norms, Simon first argues that the norm itself cannot be modeled as if real agents were omniscient calculators.

One important historical nuance is that the later label `bounded rationality` becomes canonical after this paper. Even so, this source is the clearest place in the vault to see the underlying idea in its foundational form.
[[Simon 1963]] later makes explicit that this benchmark shift was also methodological. Simon's dissatisfaction with global rationality was tied to a direct rejection of Friedman's willingness to tolerate false assumptions under an `as-if` defense.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Simon 1955]] matters because it changes the question before it answers it. It matters for economics because it challenges the adequacy of the optimizing benchmark without abandoning formal analysis. It matters for philosophy because it asks whether rationality should be judged by abstract coherence alone or by procedures that finite agents can actually carry out. It matters for politics and public policy because institutions are built for agents who search imperfectly, attend selectively, and reason under pressure rather than under ideal conditions.

This source is especially valuable pedagogically because it sits upstream of many later branches in the vault. [[Heuristics and Biases]], [[Prospect Theory]], [[Field Evidence in Behavioral Economics]], and [[Behavioral public policy]] all become easier to interpret once Simon's benchmark shift is visible. He does not solve every later problem, but he changes the terms in which the field can think about them.

## Links into the wiki

The main derived concept is [[Bounded rationality]], which should now treat this paper as its anchor source rather than as a distant precursor. The source also deepens [[Foundations and Rationality]] and especially [[Competing Views of Rationality]], because Simon provides one of the earliest major alternatives to the idealized benchmark represented by [[Expected utility theory]] and related classical models.

The paper also belongs in the background of [[Behavioral law and economics]], where later work such as [[Jolls et al. 1998]] translates cognitive realism into legal analysis. At the field level, it should carry real weight in [[Behavioral Economics]] and in [[What Behavioral Economics Thinks Rationality Is]], because both pages ask what standard of rationality behavioral economics inherits, revises, or rejects.

## Open questions

How far should the vault distinguish Simon's procedural conception of rationality from the later bias-centered style of behavioral economics? Is bounded rationality best understood as a rival norm, a descriptive correction, or a metatheoretical warning against unrealistic benchmarks? And which later branches in the vault most faithfully extend Simon's project: heuristics-and-biases, ecological rationality, or institutional and organizational analysis?
