# Grove and Meehl 1996: Comparative efficiency of informal and formal prediction procedures

## Summary

[[Grove and Meehl 1996]] is a major later statement of the clinical-statistical debate and one of the clearest historical bridges between [[Meehl 1954]] and the later meta-analytic literature. In the current vault, the paper matters because it does three things at once: it summarizes the comparative evidence, rebuts the most common anti-actuarial objections, and reflects explicitly on the profession's long resistance to the statistical result.

That makes it more than a mere review. It is the paper that says, in a mature and policy-facing way, that the original Meehl problem did not go away and that the empirical verdict remained strikingly stable across decades. In the current branch, it therefore sits naturally between [[Meehl 1954]] and [[Grove et al. 2000]].

## Key claims

The first claim is that the mechanical method is almost always equal or superior to the clinical method when the comparison is set up properly.

The second claim is that many standard defenses of clinical judgment fail. Grove and Meehl argue that common objections either confuse the kind of data with the mode of combining data or rely on professional prestige rather than comparative evidence.

The third claim is that the debate has policy implications. If formal combination methods are usually better, institutions cannot treat reliance on intuitive expertise as professionally innocent.

## Evidence and methods

The paper is a synthetic review rather than a new experiment. It surveys the comparative literature and organizes the case for formal prediction around mode-of-combination logic. In the current vault, it is especially valuable because it explicitly continues the line from [[Meehl 1954]] and prepares the way for the more formal quantitative synthesis in [[Grove et al. 2000]].

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Grove and Meehl 1996]] matters because it turns a famous methodological controversy into a durable empirical and institutional question. It matters for psychology because it rebuts the professional defenses of informal expertise. It matters for public policy because it helps explain why later algorithmic-comparison debates did not emerge from nowhere. And it matters for philosophy because it shows how much resistance can persist even after an apparently strong comparative result is established.
