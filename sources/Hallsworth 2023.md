# Hallsworth 2023: A manifesto for applying behavioural science

## Summary

[[Hallsworth 2023]] is a programmatic paper about the future of applied behavioral science. Its central claim is that the field has grown far beyond the original `nudge` framing, but now needs to broaden further in scope, method, and values if it is to remain useful. Rather than treating behavioral science as a specialist tool for tweaking isolated interventions, Hallsworth argues that it should be used as a broader lens for understanding policy, products, services, and organizations.

In the current vault, the paper matters because it offers a constructive response to many of the critiques that have accumulated around nudging and behavioral insights. It does not reject the field. Instead, it tries to reorient it: beyond the narrow tool metaphor, beyond excessive dependence on randomized controlled trials alone, and beyond the assumption that behavioral science is only about small individual-level changes.

## Key claims

The first claim is historical: applied behavioral science has had real successes, but it has also outgrown the image of nudging as its defining activity.

The second claim is programmatic: behavioral science should be used as a lens across public and private action, not merely as a specialist intervention tool.

The third claim is organizational: behavioral science should be built into institutions and their standard processes rather than isolated in small expert teams.

The fourth claim is methodological: randomized controlled trials remain valuable, but they should be put in their place alongside broader methods and stronger theoretical development.

The fifth claim is systemic: many policy problems are embedded in complex systems, so applied behavioral science has to learn to see systems rather than only isolated target behaviors.

## Evidence and methods

This paper is a manifesto-style perspective piece rather than a new empirical study. Its method is synthetic and programmatic: it reviews the recent trajectory of applied behavioral science, identifies criticisms of the field, and proposes ten forward-looking responses grouped around scope, methods, and values.

That means the paper should not be treated as a foundational empirical anchor. Its importance is field-defining. It tells us how one influential practitioner-scholar thinks the behavioral-policy branch should evolve after the first wave of nudging and behavioral-insights teams.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Hallsworth 2023]] matters because it offers a more mature picture of behavioral policy than the classic first-wave `nudge` story. It matters for economics because it insists that behavioral reasoning should be integrated into core institutional analysis rather than treated as a marginal fix for anomalies. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about what values should guide applied behavioral science and how interventionist a field of this kind ought to become. It matters for politics and public administration because it pushes the field from isolated trials toward organizational design, implementation, and system stewardship.

Pedagogically, the paper is very useful because it makes explicit that the category of behaviorally informed intervention is broader than nudges, while still keeping one foot inside the applied-policy tradition rather than repudiating it.

## Open questions

Can applied behavioral science keep broadening without losing the clarity and discipline that made nudges institutionally attractive in the first place? How far can system-level work still count as behavioral science rather than ordinary policy analysis? And what should count as success for the field over the next decade: more interventions, better institutions, or a different way of framing policy problems altogether?
