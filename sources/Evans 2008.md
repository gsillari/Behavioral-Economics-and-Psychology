# Evans 2008: Dual-processing accounts of reasoning, judgment, and social cognition
## Summary

[[Evans 2008]] is a major review article on dual-processing theories of reasoning, judgment, and social cognition. In the current vault, it matters because it gives the [[Fast and slow thinking]] branch a broad theoretical anchor that predates and contextualizes the later refinements from [[De Neys 2012]] and [[De Neys 2023]].

The paper's importance is not that it simply repeats the familiar fast-versus-slow slogan. Its contribution is to survey how dual-process accounts were being used across several domains and to show both their explanatory power and their internal variety. That makes it a strong framework source for the vault's cognitive-architecture side of behavioral economics.

## Key claims

The first claim is that reasoning and judgment are often best understood through a distinction between more automatic, intuitive, associative processes and more reflective, deliberative, rule-based processes. The second claim is that this distinction recurs across multiple subfields, including reasoning, judgment, and social cognition, even if the exact formulations differ.

The third claim is that dual-processing accounts should be treated as a family of related approaches rather than one perfectly unified theory. In the current vault, that matters because it helps explain why later sources such as [[De Neys 2023]] can revise dual-process theory without simply abandoning it. The framework was already heterogeneous.

## Evidence and methods

This is a synthetic review article rather than a single-experiment paper. Evans surveys a large literature across cognitive and social psychology and organizes it around the recurring distinction between intuitive and reflective processing. The source therefore functions as a map of the dual-process field rather than as a narrow test of one mechanism.

That makes it especially useful in the vault as a bridge source. It sits between the classic bias literature and the later metatheoretical debate over how fast-and-slow models should be formulated. It helps keep the dual-process branch from depending only on late critical refinements.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Evans 2008]] matters because much of the popular and policy-facing language of behavioral science depends on a fast-versus-slow picture of the mind. It matters for economics because assumptions about intuitive and deliberative processing shape how bounded rationality is interpreted. It matters for philosophy because the paper raises questions about whether reflective thought is normatively privileged and how different kinds of cognition relate to standards of rationality. It matters for politics because ideas about deliberation, voter competence, and corrective policy often presuppose some version of dual-process architecture.

The paper is especially useful pedagogically because it shows that dual-process theory is broader than the behavioral-economics canon alone. That helps PPE students understand why later debates over rationality and correction become so influential.

## Open questions

How unified are dual-processing theories really? Which parts of the framework are doing explanatory work and which are loose metaphors? And how should the vault distinguish the broad dual-process family from the more specific revisions offered by the De Neys branch?
