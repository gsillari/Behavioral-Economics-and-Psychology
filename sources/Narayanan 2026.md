# Narayanan 2026: What if algorithmic fairness is a category error?

## Summary

[[Narayanan 2026]] argues that much of the algorithmic-fairness debate asks the wrong kind of question. If fairness is treated as a statistical property of an algorithmic subsystem, the debate becomes tractable but too narrow. The model may satisfy or violate a metric, yet the real-world fairness of the decision process depends on the surrounding organization, the decision subjects, the policy goals, the available procedural protections, and the political context in which the tool is deployed.

In the current vault, this source should be read as a system-level extension of [[Binns 2018]] and [[Wang et al. 2024]]. [[Binns 2018]] shows that fairness metrics encode contestable ideas from moral and political philosophy. [[Wang et al. 2024]] argues that predictive optimization often lacks a defensible account of goals, targets, and legitimacy. [[Narayanan 2026]] pushes the point further: fairness is not really a property of the algorithm alone. It is a property, if at all, of a sociotechnical decision-making system as a whole.

The paper's positive proposal is to study and design "algorithmic bureaucracies." That means shifting attention from model scores alone to the full architecture of institutional decision-making: how goals are set, how policies are encoded, how human adjudicators interact with automated systems, what appeals or explanations exist, and how affected people experience the system.

## Key claims

The first claim is that the discrimination-centered fairness frame has been politically powerful but substantively narrow. It can identify some important harms, such as error-rate disparities in criminal risk tools, while missing other problems such as pretrial detention's legitimacy, procedural opacity, validity failures, or the use of automation as a patch for broken institutions.

The second claim is that treating fairness as a property of an algorithmic subsystem is a category error. Statistical metrics can describe aspects of a model, but they do not by themselves answer whether the larger decision-making system is just.

The third claim is that the appeal of formal fairness comes partly from its tractability. It lets computer scientists, lawyers, auditors, and advocates work with clean definitions, but that neatness can depoliticize choices that are unavoidably value-laden.

The fourth claim is that algorithmic decision-making often requires goals and values to be specified more explicitly than ordinary bureaucratic decision-making does. That creates a problem because public institutions often discover and negotiate values through policy comparison, implementation, discretion, and contestation rather than through an upfront objective function.

The fifth claim is that a better research agenda should model the whole algorithmic bureaucracy: objective-setting, policy design, cost-benefit tradeoffs, human-computer interaction, street-level discretion, explanations, appeals, contestability, and the interaction between decision subjects and the system.

## Evidence and methods

The paper is a conceptual and programmatic essay rather than an empirical study. It draws examples from criminal justice risk assessment, hiring automation, welfare and social-services automation, content moderation, organ allocation, and delivery-service geography.

Its method is diagnostic: it explains why the algorithmic-fairness movement has been influential, why bias audits and formal metrics often fail to reach the deepest harms, and why bureaucratic theory and empirical social science are needed alongside technical work.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Narayanan 2026]] is valuable because it makes the algorithmic-bias branch comparative and institutional. It does not say fairness metrics are useless. It says they are incomplete unless embedded in a theory of public authority, administrative procedure, legitimacy, and social harm.

The paper also gives a strong bridge from philosophy to policy. It supports the course's conclusion that algorithms are fragile because humans choose the labels, objectives, procurement rules, audits, and override structures. The fairness question is therefore not "is the algorithm fair?" in isolation. It is whether the technosocial decision-making system has publicly defensible targets, procedures, transparency, contestability, and deployment rules.

## Open questions

How should algorithmic bureaucracies be evaluated when values cannot be specified cleanly in advance? Which procedural protections matter most when predictive tools are used as decision support rather than as replacements for human adjudicators? And when should reform focus on the model, the human-machine interface, the institution, or the decision domain itself?
