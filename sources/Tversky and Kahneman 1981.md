# Tversky and Kahneman 1981: Judgments of and by representativeness
## Summary

[[Tversky and Kahneman 1981]] extends the representativeness program by showing that judged plausibility and formal probability can come apart sharply. In the report version provided to the vault, the paper distinguishes several forms of representativeness and uses them to explain why specificity and coherence can make an event feel more likely even when they reduce its probability.

Its biggest contribution in the current vault is to give the representativeness branch a direct anchor for [[Conjunction fallacy]]. The paper shows that people can rate a more detailed, more representative conjunction as more probable than one of its broader components. That makes it one of the clearest cases where intuitive judgment violates a basic formal norm.

## Key claims

The first claim is that representativeness is not a single narrow judgment cue. The paper treats it as a more general family of relations: between an instance and a category, a sample and a population, an effect and a cause, or a value and a variable.

The second claim is that increased specificity can increase perceived representativeness. This is psychologically powerful because a more detailed description often feels more coherent and more diagnostic. But formally, additional detail can only hold probability constant or reduce it.

The third claim is that this conflict between coherence and probability helps explain systematic judgment errors, especially the [[Conjunction fallacy]]. In the current vault, this makes the paper one of the clearest demonstrations of how intuitive plausibility can outrun formal reasoning.

## Conjunction fallacy and representativeness

This source is especially important because it ties representativeness to one of the most teachable formal violations in behavioral economics. [[Conjunction fallacy]] is not just another bias label here. It is the point where the vault can show students exactly how intuitive judgment, descriptive richness, and formal probability diverge.

That matters for the representativeness branch as a whole. [[Kahneman and Tversky 1972]] establishes similarity-based subjective probability. [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] places representativeness inside a wider heuristics program. [[Tversky and Kahneman 1981]] then shows how the same mechanism helps produce a dramatic and philosophically important failure of extensional reasoning.

## Why this source is distinct from 1974

The 1974 paper is broader and more programmatic. This 1981 source is narrower and sharper. Its focus is not on introducing three heuristic families, but on examining what representativeness does to probability judgment when descriptive coherence becomes highly salient.

In the current vault, that means the source should not be treated as a duplicate of [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]]. It deserves a separate role as the main source note for the stronger representativeness branch now emerging under [[Judgment, Belief, and Inference]].

## Relation to later reinterpretation

[[Kahneman and Frederick 2002]] matters here because it later reframes representativeness as a case of [[Attribute substitution]]. On that view, the difficult question about probability is answered by an easier question about resemblance or coherence. In the current vault, that means this 1981 source now sits midway between the earlier heuristic statement and the later process-based reinterpretation.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it is an unusually clear case where formal rationality and intuitive judgment point in different directions. It matters for economics because probability judgment enters forecasting, risk assessment, and market expectations. It matters for philosophy because it puts norm violation front and center: the more compelling description is not the more probable one. It matters for politics because narrative coherence, descriptive vividness, and plausibility often dominate public reasoning even when they do not track formal likelihood well.

This source is therefore extremely useful pedagogically. It gives students a concrete case where they can see why behavioral economics became a challenge not only to empirical economics, but also to broader theories of rational reasoning.
