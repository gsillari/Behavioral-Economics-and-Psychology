# Laibson and List 2015: Principles of (Behavioral) Economics
## Summary

[[Laibson and List 2015]] is a short didactic overview designed for teaching behavioral economics in a principles course. In the current vault, its value is not that it introduces a new theory or anomaly. Its value is that it offers an unusually clear six-part compression of the field that can be used to organize existing concept pages without creating new ones.

The six principles are:

- people try to choose the best feasible option, but they sometimes make predictable mistakes
- people are more motivated by losses than by equivalent gains
- people have self-control problems
- people care about the actions and outcomes of others
- markets can be influenced by boundedly rational behavior when enough agents are affected
- paternalistic correction is tempting in theory but heavy-handed paternalism is risky in practice

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Laibson and List 2015]] matters mainly as a teaching and orientation source. It is especially useful for giving the current wiki a compact classroom-facing spine across [[Bounded rationality]], [[Loss aversion]], [[Time inconsistency]], [[Social preferences]], [[Field Evidence in Behavioral Economics]], and [[Behavioral public policy]].

Its contribution is therefore synthetic rather than concept-generating. The paper makes the field easier to teach without significantly changing the concept architecture already in the vault. That is valuable for economics because it compresses the field into a manageable structure, for philosophy because it makes the benchmark departures legible without inflating them into a theory of everything, and for politics and public policy because it ends with a deliberately cautious view of paternalism.

## Links into the wiki

The paper is best used to sharpen existing hub pages and major concepts. It gives [[Behavioral Economics]] a concise six-principle teaching frame. It gives [[Bounded rationality]] a didactic restatement of optimization with predictable mistakes. It gives [[Loss aversion]], [[Time inconsistency]], and [[Social preferences]] simple classroom summaries of three major behavioral departures from the standard model. It gives [[Field Evidence in Behavioral Economics]] a nice bridge from individual bias to market-level outcomes, and it gives [[Behavioral public policy]] a concise statement of why behavioral policy often ends in cautious, not maximal, paternalism.
