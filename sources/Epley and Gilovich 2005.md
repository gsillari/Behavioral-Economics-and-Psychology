# Epley and Gilovich 2005: When effortful thinking influences judgmental anchoring
## Summary

[[Epley and Gilovich 2005]] extends the mechanism argument of [[Epley and Gilovich 2001]] by asking when effortful thinking actually changes anchoring effects. The paper argues that forewarning and incentives do not reduce all anchoring effects equally. They mainly matter when the anchor is self-generated and judgment proceeds through effortful adjustment. In the current vault, this source matters because it makes the anchoring branch far more useful for questions about debiasing and process.

The paper therefore shifts the anchoring debate in an important way. Instead of asking whether anchoring can be reduced by making people think harder in general, it asks which anchoring effects are the kind that harder thinking could plausibly change. That is a much stronger and more practically relevant question.

## Key claims

The first claim is that anchoring effects are produced by multiple mechanisms. Self-generated anchors are more likely to involve an effortful process of adjustment, whereas externally provided anchors are less responsive to deliberate effort.

The second claim is that forewarning and incentives should therefore have differential effects. If an anchoring effect depends on adjustment, then greater motivation or explicit warning should increase adjustment and reduce the bias. If the anchoring effect depends on some other mechanism, these interventions should do little.

The third claim is that this is exactly what the experiments show. Effortful thinking influences judgments anchored on self-generated values more than judgments anchored on externally provided values. In the current vault, this matters because it gives a clean bridge from anchoring research to broader PPE questions about debiasing, institutional design, and the limits of reflective correction.

## Evidence and methods

This is an experimental paper using two studies that compare the effects of forewarning and incentives on different kinds of anchoring tasks. The key design move is to separate self-generated anchors from externally provided anchors and then test whether increased motivation or warning changes the amount of adjustment. The result is not a generic reduction in bias. It is a patterned difference across anchor types.

Methodologically, this source is especially useful because it turns anchoring into a process-sensitive field of inquiry. It shows that debiasing results are not informative unless the underlying mechanism is specified first. In that sense, the paper is as much about methodology and explanation as it is about anchoring itself.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Epley and Gilovich 2005]] matters because it shows how behavioral economics becomes more policy-relevant when it moves from surface phenomena to underlying processes. It matters for economics because the question is not just whether judgment is biased, but whether that bias is responsive to incentives and effort. It matters for philosophy because it complicates the relation between reflection and rationality: more effort does not always produce correction, and when it does, that may depend on the kind of process already in play. It matters for politics because many real-world attempts at debiasing rely on warning, incentives, or reflective prompts, and the paper suggests those tools will not work uniformly.

This is therefore one of the more practically important anchoring papers in the vault. It does not simply refine the theory of bias. It tells us when debiasing efforts are likely to be miscalibrated.

## Open questions

How far do these differential debiasing results travel outside laboratory judgment tasks? Which real institutional anchors are more like self-generated anchors and which are more like externally imposed ones? And how should policy design change when some reflective prompts are effective only for a subset of behavioral distortions?
