# Lichtenstein et al. 1978: Judged frequency of lethal events
## Summary

[[Lichtenstein et al. 1978]] is an important early bridge between the classic availability literature and later work on risk perception. The paper studies how people judge the frequency of different causes of death and shows that those judgments are systematically distorted in patterned ways rather than randomly inaccurate.

In the current vault, this source matters because it gives the [[Availability heuristic]] branch a more concrete and policy-relevant empirical foothold. It shows that vivid, dramatic, or highly imaginable lethal events can dominate perceived frequency, while more common but less salient causes are underestimated.

## Key claims

The first claim is that frequency judgments about lethal events are biased in regular directions rather than merely noisy.

The second claim is that salience, imaginability, and media prominence help explain those distortions.

The third claim is that risk perception is therefore partly shaped by cognitive availability rather than only by exposure to underlying statistical frequencies.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source helps the vault connect early judgment-under-uncertainty research to public-risk judgment. It belongs directly under [[Availability heuristic]], but it also deepens the later branch running through [[Affect heuristic]] and [[Dread risk]].

It also matters historically because it shows that Slovic and collaborators were already translating heuristic mechanisms into socially important risk domains before later work on catastrophic risk, terrorism, and public policy made that translation more explicit.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows how a cognitive mechanism becomes politically relevant. It matters for economics because misjudged frequency can distort private precaution and valuation. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about rational belief under salience pressure. It matters for politics because public attention to mortality risks often tracks vividness and narrative force more closely than underlying incidence.
