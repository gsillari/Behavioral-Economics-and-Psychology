# Tversky and Kahneman 1974: Judgment under uncertainty
## Summary

Tversky and Kahneman's 1974 paper argues that judgment under uncertainty is often guided by simple heuristics rather than by formal statistical reasoning.
These heuristics can be efficient, but they also generate systematic and predictable errors.
In the current vault, the paper is the anchor for [[Heuristics and Biases]] and for the three canonical mechanism pages [[Representativeness heuristic]], [[Availability heuristic]], and [[Anchoring]].

Its importance is larger than that trio alone.
The paper gives behavioral economics one of its founding vocabularies for talking about rationality under uncertainty.
It also sets up later disputes.
Without this source, there is no clear target for [[Gigerenzer 2008]] to criticize, no stable classic literature for [[De Neys 2012]] and [[De Neys 2023]] to reinterpret, and no obvious early bridge into the later model-building work of [[Prospect Theory]].

## Key claims

The paper's first claim is that people do not generally reason about uncertainty by applying probability theory or statistical logic directly.
Instead, they rely on recurring mental shortcuts.
The importance of that claim is not that intuition exists.
It is that intuitive judgment has structure.

The second claim is that the major heuristics can be grouped into recurring families.
Representativeness leads people to judge probability or category membership by resemblance.
Availability leads them to judge frequency or likelihood by ease of recall or imaginability.
Anchoring leads them to begin from an initial value or cue and adjust insufficiently away from it.

The third claim is that these heuristics generate patterned biases rather than random error.
The paper's ambition is therefore larger than listing mistakes.
It aims to show that there is a systematic psychology of judgment under uncertainty and that this psychology can be studied against identifiable benchmark norms.

## Three heuristic families

[[Representativeness heuristic]] matters because it explains why similarity can displace statistical logic.
People may ignore base rates, overlook sample-size considerations, or expect small samples to look too representative of a population or process.
In the current vault, this makes representativeness central to the relation between intuitive classification and formal probabilistic reasoning.

[[Availability heuristic]] matters because it shows that recall, vividness, and imaginability can shape judgments of frequency or probability.
An event or category can feel common, important, or likely because it is easy to bring to mind, not because it is objectively frequent.
That makes availability crucial for later questions about salience, media, and public attention.

[[Anchoring]] matters because it shows that judgments are often path-dependent.
People begin from a starting point and do not fully adjust away from it, even when the starting point is arbitrary.
This becomes especially important later in the vault because [[Ariely et al. 2003]] shows that anchoring can shape valuation and apparent demand, not only numerical estimation.

## Normative targets and benchmark norms

One of the most important things about this paper is that it presupposes formal targets.
The heuristics count as bias-producing because they depart from probability theory, statistics, or related norms of disciplined inference.
This means the paper is never just descriptive psychology.
It is an early benchmark argument about what good judgment under uncertainty is supposed to look like.

That benchmark dimension is exactly why the paper remains central to PPE.
The empirical claim that people use heuristics cannot be separated from the evaluative claim that some resulting judgments are worse than they would be under better statistical reasoning.
Later sources in the vault, especially [[Gigerenzer 2008]], [[Hortal 2025]], [[Samuels et al. 2002]], [[De Neys 2012]], and [[De Neys 2023]], all matter partly because they contest, reinterpret, or complicate those benchmark assumptions.

## Evidence and methods

The paper is built from experimental tasks and stylized judgment problems rather than from field data.
Participants estimate probabilities, frequencies, and magnitudes in settings designed to expose regular departures from formal norms.
The method is neither purely theoretical nor purely descriptive.
It is a mechanism-seeking experimental style that tries to infer stable cognitive processes from recurring classes of error.

That matters for the wiki because the paper occupies a distinctive place in the field's development.
It is more experimentally focused than the broad programmatic mapping of [[Camerer and Loewenstein 2004]].
It is less model-formal than [[Kahneman and Tversky 1979]].
It is also less institutionally grounded than [[DellaVigna 2009]].
Its special contribution is to identify recurring judgment mechanisms that later sources can formalize, contest, extend, or test in the field.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it supplies much of the vocabulary through which behavioral economics entered broader debates about rationality, judgment, and public decision-making.
It matters for economics because it challenges the assumption that agents reason statistically in a disciplined way.
It matters for philosophy because it raises the question of whether observed biases are genuine irrationality or context-sensitive shortcuts.
It matters for politics because the same patterns help illuminate how citizens and policymakers form judgments under uncertainty.

The paper is especially good for first-principles teaching.
It shows how an apparently technical question about probability judgment becomes a question about rationality standards, cognitive limits, and institutional interpretation.
That is why it should stay central to the course-facing spine of the vault, not just to one subfield page.

## Open questions

One open question is how far these heuristics should be treated as general cognitive tendencies rather than task-specific artifacts.
Another is when the identified biases reflect genuine error and when they reflect sensible adaptation to informational limits.

A third question concerns intellectual lineage.
How directly does the heuristics-and-biases program lead into later models such as [[Prospect Theory]], and how much should the two be treated as parallel rather than linear stages of the same project?
That is one of the key questions the vault should keep visible for PPE students.
