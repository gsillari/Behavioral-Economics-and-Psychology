# List 2004: Neoclassical theory versus prospect theory
## Summary

[[List 2004]] is the sharper theoretical field test in the current vault between neoclassical exchange predictions and [[Prospect Theory]] in marketplace settings. The paper argues that prospect-theory-style endowment effects organize behavior among inexperienced consumers, while experienced traders behave more in line with neoclassical exchange predictions.

In this wiki, the source matters because it does more than replicate [[List 2003]]. It frames the issue explicitly as a competition between theoretical paradigms and asks whether the endowment effect should be treated as a general feature of exchange or as a context-sensitive phenomenon.

## Key claims

The first claim is that prospect-theory predictions fit inexperienced traders better than experienced ones.

The second claim is that market experience can produce behavior closer to the neoclassical benchmark.

The third claim is that the endowment effect should not be assumed to operate uniformly across populations and institutional settings.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source is especially useful because it links the endowment-effect branch back to the central theoretical spine of the vault. It directly connects [[Endowment effect]] not only to [[Loss aversion]] and [[Reference dependence]], but also to the broader question of when [[Prospect Theory]] beats the neoclassical benchmark and when it does not.

It therefore belongs in the valuation branch and in the larger debate about the scope conditions of behavioral findings. Compared with [[List 2003]], this paper gives the vault a more explicit theory comparison and a cleaner link to benchmark choice.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows how empirical evidence can adjudicate between theoretical frameworks. It matters for economics because it asks when neoclassical exchange theory remains descriptively adequate. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about when anomalies count against a theory and when they count as scope-limited deviations. It matters for politics because policy often relies on assumptions about whether ordinary citizens behave more like inexperienced or experienced traders.

## Open questions

Does this evidence show that prospect theory is wrong for experienced agents, or that markets can train people out of some reference-dependent behaviors? Which parts of the endowment effect remain even under repeated trading?
