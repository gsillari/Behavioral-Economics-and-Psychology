# O'Donoghue and Rabin 1999: Doing it now or later

## Summary

[[O'Donoghue and Rabin 1999]] is one of the clearest formal sources for the self-control branch of behavioral economics. The local text is the reprint in [[Camerer et al. 2004]], but the vault catalogs it under the original article identity because it is a standalone, widely cited contribution to the literature on [[Time inconsistency]]. It is also one of the main downstream formal developments of the broader psychology-and-economics architecture in [[Rabin 1998]].

The paper uses a present-biased preference model to show why people may delay actions they should take now, take actions now that they should delay, and make different choices depending on whether they understand their own future self-control problems. Its central contribution is not merely the use of quasi-hyperbolic discounting. It is the analytic separation of immediate costs, immediate rewards, sophistication, and naivete.

## Main claims

The first claim is that present bias changes how future trade-offs are evaluated as one option becomes immediate. A person may prefer an earlier future action to a later future action when both are distant, but reverse that preference when the earlier action arrives. This is the formal structure behind many cases of dynamic inconsistency.

The second claim is that immediate costs and immediate rewards generate different behavioral pathologies. For immediate-cost activities, such as unpleasant tasks with future benefits, present bias produces procrastination: the person waits when she should act. For immediate-reward activities, such as tempting consumption with future costs, present bias can produce what the authors call preproperation: the person acts too soon when waiting would be better.

The third claim is that sophistication and naivete matter independently of present bias itself. Sophisticated agents anticipate their future present bias and may use that pessimism strategically. Naive agents overestimate their future discipline and therefore repeatedly postpone costly actions or overindulge in tempting ones. The paper is especially useful because it shows that sophistication is not always unambiguously good: it mitigates some procrastination problems, but can also worsen immediate-reward problems by inducing earlier indulgence.

The fourth claim is welfare-theoretic. The same observed behavior can be interpreted very differently depending on whether it expresses a stable preference or a self-control failure. This matters for [[Internalities]] and [[Behavioral welfare economics]], because policies aimed at smoking, saving, addiction, deadlines, or commitment devices depend on whether the current self is imposing costs on a later self.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[O'Donoghue and Rabin 1999]] matters because it turns self-control into a structured analytical problem rather than a loose moralized idea about weakness of will. It matters for economics because it gives a compact formal model of present bias that can be applied to saving, addiction, deadlines, and consumption. It matters for philosophy because it makes intrapersonal conflict explicit: welfare analysis must decide which temporally indexed perspective, if any, has normative priority. It matters for public policy because it clarifies why commitment devices, defaults, reminders, and sin taxes may be interpreted as future-self protection rather than only as external steering.
