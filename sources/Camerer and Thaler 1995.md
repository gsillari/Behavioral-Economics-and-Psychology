# Camerer and Thaler 1995: Anomalies: Ultimatums, dictators and manners

## Summary

[[Camerer and Thaler 1995]] is a compact survey of what ultimatum and dictator games had already taught behavioral economics by the mid-1990s. Its guiding contrast is simple: if subjects were narrow income maximizers, proposers in the [[Ultimatum game]] would offer very little and responders would accept. Instead, offers commonly average around a substantial minority of the pie, equal splits are frequent, and very low offers are often rejected.

The paper is especially useful for the vault because it prevents a too-simple reading of fairness. Ultimatum behavior is not just altruism. Proposers may be fair-minded, may fear rejection, or may want to appear fair. Responders may reject because unequal offers are costly signals of disrespect or bad "manners." The [[Dictator game]] then helps separate those motives by removing the responder's veto: giving usually falls, but it often does not disappear.

## What it adds to the wiki

This source makes [[Ultimatum game]] and [[Dictator game]] into a paired teaching device. The ultimatum game shows that unfair treatment can be punished at a cost. The dictator game shows that positive giving remains when strategic rejection is removed, but that this giving is highly sensitive to anonymity, framing, and social distance.

The paper also connects directly to [[Rabin 1993]]. Camerer and Thaler treat Rabin's formal model of fairness as a serious response to the evidence: game theory can be revised by changing the psychological content of payoffs rather than by abandoning strategic analysis. That is exactly the role this cluster should play in the wiki.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Camerer and Thaler 1995]] matters because it turns experimental anomalies into a conceptual map of social motivation. It matters for economics because it shows how simple games can reveal limits of narrow self-interest. It matters for philosophy because fairness, manners, and punishment raise questions about what counts as rational response to treatment by others. It matters for politics and policy because institutions often depend on whether people perceive offers, procedures, and bargains as legitimate.
