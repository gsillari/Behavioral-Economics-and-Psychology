# Tversky and Kahneman 1981 (Framing): The framing of decisions and the psychology of choice

## Summary

[[Tversky and Kahneman 1981 (Framing)]] is one of the clearest pedagogical statements of the framing branch of behavioral decision research. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman show that equivalent decision problems can generate sharply different choices depending on whether outcomes are described as gains or as losses, and they use that result to connect framing directly to the emerging logic of [[Prospect Theory]].

In the current vault, the paper matters not only as evidence but as exposition. It is one of the best short sources for teaching why descriptively equivalent formulations can produce opposite risk attitudes.

## Key claims

The first claim is that decision problems are not psychologically neutral containers. The representation of outcomes as gains or losses can shift choice even when the underlying prospects are extensionally equivalent.

The second claim is that risk attitudes depend on domain. People tend to be risk averse in gains and risk seeking in losses.

The third claim is that these reversals place pressure on [[Expected utility theory]] and support the descriptive logic later formalized in [[Prospect Theory]].

## Evidence and methods

The paper uses compact, memorable choice problems rather than a large theoretical apparatus. Its main value in the current vault is that it makes the gain-loss framing mechanism exceptionally legible for first encounters with behavioral economics.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it is one of the best teaching texts in the field. It matters for economics because it shows why equivalent descriptions can disrupt revealed-preference style reasoning. It matters for philosophy because it raises a direct challenge to extensional equivalence and rational-choice consistency. It matters for politics because framing effects immediately scale into public communication, policy presentation, and political choice.

## Open questions

How much of the framing effect comes from gain-loss coding specifically, and how much from broader representational changes? Which political and policy framings most faithfully parallel the classic laboratory structure? And when should framing-sensitive choice be treated as irrational rather than as evidence that representation is part of rational evaluation itself?
