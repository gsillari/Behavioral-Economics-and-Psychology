# Kunreuther 1996: Mitigating disaster losses through insurance
## Summary

[[Kunreuther 1996]] examines why individuals and institutions often underinsure against catastrophic loss even when insurance appears ex ante sensible. In the current vault, the paper matters because it adds a disaster-risk and insurance branch to [[Behavioral public policy]], showing that behavioral distortions do not only affect everyday judgment but also preparedness for low-probability, high-stakes events.

The paper is useful because it sits between descriptive psychology and institutional design. The problem is not simply that people misperceive risk. It is that those misperceptions interact with timing, incentives, and policy design in ways that make disaster protection systematically fragile.

## Key claims

The first claim is that people often fail to insure adequately against rare disasters because they underweight low-probability events before a disaster occurs and overreact afterward.

The second claim is that market provision alone may not solve this problem. Behavioral responses, myopia, and expectations about aid can undermine efficient insurance uptake.

The third claim is that disaster policy has to be designed with behavioral response in mind rather than assuming stable, well-calibrated private demand.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source should strengthen the policy branch rather than the core rationality branch. It gives the vault a concrete example of how risk perception, salience, and institutional design interact in a real policy domain. It also complements [[Gigerenzer 2004]] and [[Dread risk]] by focusing not on acute post-crisis substitution alone, but on pre-crisis underpreparation.

In the current wiki, it helps show that behavioral public policy is not only about nudges and paternalism. It is also about insurance markets, catastrophic risk, and institutional responses to predictable underprotection.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it connects behavioral economics to a classic policy problem: how societies prepare for catastrophic loss. It matters for economics because insurance demand and risk pricing are not purely actuarial. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about responsibility, paternalism, and how much the state should protect people against failures of foresight. It matters for politics because disaster preparedness and relief are highly salient institutional choices with distributive and legitimacy consequences.
