# Stanovich and West 2000: Individual differences in reasoning
## Summary

[[Stanovich and West 2000]] is a major intervention in the rationality debate because it argues that disputes about human rationality cannot be settled by looking only at average performance on classic tasks. They should also be disciplined by individual-differences evidence. The paper asks what it means when some people consistently give more normatively defensible answers than others on reasoning and judgment problems. In the current vault, that move matters because it turns the rationality debate from a fight over favored examples into a more constrained empirical program.

The source is especially useful because it organizes the main alternatives available when people violate normative benchmarks. A gap between descriptive responses and a formal norm might reflect mere performance error, computational limitation, use of the wrong normative standard, or a different construal of the task. Stanovich and West argue that some of these escape routes are overused. The point is not that every classical benchmark is beyond challenge, but that defenses of ordinary reasoning need to say more than "people may have interpreted the task differently" or "the norm may not apply."

## Key claims

The first claim is methodological. Individual-differences patterns can tell us whether a supposed bias is just noise or a stable dimension of reasoning competence.

The second claim is critical. Many Panglossian responses to bias findings are too easy if they are not tied to evidence about who performs better, under what conditions, and relative to which standard.

The third claim is classificatory. Explanations of norm violations should be sorted into a small set of live options rather than invoked loosely. That makes the rationality debate more cumulative and less rhetorical.

The fourth claim is disciplinary. Rationality research needs stronger links between descriptive psychology, normative theory, and psychometrics of reasoning performance.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source matters because it gives the [[Rationality wars]] a firmer empirical backbone. The current vault already has philosophical and architectural disputes about rationality. [[Stanovich and West 2000]] adds a methodological demand: competing interpretations should be judged partly by whether they can explain stable individual differences in rational responding. That is a major contribution because it prevents the debate from sliding into unrestricted benchmark pluralism.

It also helps the vault connect the dual-process branch to the rationality branch. The paper is not mainly a dual-process manifesto, but it contributes to the environment in which later type-1/type-2 accounts, debate over normative standards, and work on reasoning dispositions take shape. It therefore belongs close to both [[Rationality wars]] and [[Fast and slow thinking]].

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows that the rationality debate is not only philosophical. It is also about what counts as good empirical adjudication between competing interpretations of reasoning. It matters for economics because debates about bounded rationality and anomaly often depend on whether deviations from benchmark models are random, systematic, or trait-like. It matters for philosophy because it raises the question of how descriptive evidence should bear on claims about norms of reasoning. It matters for politics because public competence, expertise, and institutional design often presuppose that some people reason more carefully or more normatively than others.
