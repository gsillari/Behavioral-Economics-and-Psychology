# Camerer 2019: Artificial intelligence and behavioral economics
## Summary

[[Camerer 2019]] is a programmatic chapter on the relationship between artificial intelligence and behavioral economics. Rather than treating AI only as a new object to test, the chapter asks how machine learning can help behavioral economics discover better predictive variables, how familiar human judgment errors might be reinterpreted as failures analogous to weak machine-learning implementations, and how institutions might use AI to either overcome or exploit human behavioral limits. In this vault, it broadens the AI branch beyond LLM bias measurement alone.

## Key claims

The chapter advances three main claims. First, machine learning can help behavioral economists search for new behaviorally relevant predictors rather than relying only on theory-driven variable selection. Second, some human prediction failures can be understood more clearly by comparing them to poor learning architectures or weak prediction systems, which creates a useful bridge between [[Bounded rationality]] and formal prediction. Third, AI deployed by firms and institutions may either reduce human limitations or strategically exploit them, so behavioral economics has to study not only human error but also human-machine interaction.

## Evidence and methods

The chapter is conceptual and exploratory rather than centered on one original experiment. Camerer uses examples from bargaining, risky choice, and prediction to argue that machine learning can identify behaviorally important variables and generate new hypotheses. He also treats AI as an institutional force that changes the setting in which behavioral regularities matter, especially when firms can observe, predict, and steer users more effectively than before.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it shows that behavioral economics is not only a body of findings about biased humans. It is also a framework for thinking about how institutions learn, predict, and intervene. It matters for economics because it reframes behavioral variables as discoverable predictive inputs rather than only theoretical constructs, for politics because it raises governance questions about AI systems that can exploit attention and preference formation, and for philosophy because it complicates the boundary between human rationality, machine prediction, and institutional power.

## Open questions

How much of behavioral economics can actually be improved by machine-learning discovery rather than theory-led modeling? When does AI help people overcome bounded rationality and when does it mainly scale manipulation? Should the vault distinguish more sharply between AI as a target of behavioral evaluation and AI as a method for behavioral science?
