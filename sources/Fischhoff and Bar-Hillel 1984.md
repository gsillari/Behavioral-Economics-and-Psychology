# Fischhoff and Bar-Hillel 1984: Diagnosticity and the base-rate effect

## Summary

[[Fischhoff and Bar-Hillel 1984]] asks when base rates affect categorical predictions. The paper's key contribution is to connect base-rate use to the diagnosticity of the individuating information. When descriptions strongly differentiate between categories, representativeness dominates and base rates have little effect. When descriptions are neutral or weakly diagnostic, base rates have more room to influence judgment.

In the current vault, the paper matters because it makes [[Base-rate neglect]] more graded. The issue is not simply whether base rates are used or ignored. Their effect varies with the degree to which individuating information seems differentially representative of the possible categories.

## Key claims

The first claim is that diagnostic individuating information can swamp base-rate information even when the diagnostic value of the description is weak or questionable.

The second claim is that base-rate effects increase as differential representativeness decreases. In other words, the less a description clearly fits one category rather than another, the more base rates affect prediction.

The third claim is that neutral stimuli tend to be assigned in proportion to base rates. This suggests a probability-matching pattern: people use base rates when representativeness fails to guide the classification, but they do not necessarily use them in a fully Bayesian way.

The fourth claim is methodological. The paper independently scales the representativeness or neutrality of stimuli rather than relying only on experimenter intuition. This is important because the authors find that supposedly low-diagnosticity materials can still look diagnostic to subjects.

## Evidence and debate

The experiments vary prediction contexts such as engineer-lawyer, engineer-physicist, and executive-professor pairings. The central manipulation is how similar the categories are with respect to the descriptions. When categories have distinct stereotypes, base rates have little effect. When categories are more similar or descriptions are more neutral, base-rate effects become larger.

The paper also measures confidence. Subjects are more confident when predictions align with strong representativeness cues, and somewhat less confident when predictions run against majority base rates. This suggests that base rates may sometimes affect confidence even when they do not reverse categorical choice.

## Why it matters for the vault

This source gives [[Base-rate neglect]] an important intermediate position between simple neglect and full Bayesian updating. It shows that intuitive judges can use base rates, but often only in the residue left over by representativeness. The practical lesson is subtle: base-rate information becomes more influential when case descriptions are neutral, ambiguous, or hard to map onto a category stereotype.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows how institutional presentation can change the weight of statistical evidence. If decision makers rely on base rates mainly when case descriptions are not diagnostically compelling, then policy design must attend not only to what evidence is available but also to whether individuating descriptions crowd it out.
