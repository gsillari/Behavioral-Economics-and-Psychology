# Rich 2016: Axiomatic and ecological rationality: choosing costs and benefits

## Summary

[[Rich 2016]] is a philosophical paper about how to evaluate rationality when the goal is not only classification but guidance and improvement. In the current vault, its main importance is that it refuses the common winner-take-all framing of the rationality wars. Rich compares two major approaches to normative rationality: an axiomatic approach, which evaluates behavior by coherence with abstract criteria, and an ecological approach, which evaluates decision processes by how well they perform in relevant environments. Her conclusion is not that one side defeats the other. It is that each approach captures real advantages while incurring characteristic costs, and that a carefully designed hybrid can outperform either pure view for the purpose of helping agents improve.

That makes the paper especially useful in this wiki. It should not be used to flatten the rationality wars into easy pluralism, but it does show that some of the deepest disputes are partly disputes about evaluative purpose. Rich's target is normative methodology, not the empirical existence of biases or the existence of adaptive heuristics. The paper therefore refines the vault's rationality architecture without undermining the importance of [[Heuristics and Biases]], [[Prospect Theory]], or [[Ecological rationality]].

## Key claims

The first claim is that the axiomatic and ecological approaches should be compared as approaches to normative rationality, not only as rival descriptions of behavior. Rich is interested in which approach is better suited to guiding agents toward better judgment and choice.

The second claim is that neither approach is unserviceable. Axiomatic rationality is often criticized for being too formal, detached from context, or insensitive to bounded cognition. Ecological rationality is often criticized for depending too heavily on context-specific success criteria and for making normative evaluation difficult to generalize. Rich argues that the strongest versions of each view can answer more of these objections than their critics allow.

The third claim is that each approach accepts a different profile of costs and benefits. Axiomatic approaches gain generality, clarity, and relatively simple behavioral tests, but often at the cost of contextual sensitivity. Ecological approaches gain fit to environments and sensitivity to performance, but often at the cost of broader comparability and simpler normative guidance.

The fourth claim is that this trade-off suggests a hybrid approach. Rich's proposal is not a loose appeal to pluralism. It is a specific normative strategy: use axiomatic rationality criteria to evaluate the processes agents might use. In that way, ecological attention to process is retained while axiomatic standards are used to assess whether the candidate procedures are worth adopting.

## Evidence and methods

The paper is philosophical and methodological rather than experimental. Its method is comparative reconstruction. Rich first interprets the strongest available versions of the axiomatic and ecological approaches, then compares them in terms of the costs and benefits each accepts for the purpose of rational improvement. The paper draws on work in decision theory, heuristics and biases, ecological rationality, and belief revision, but its core contribution is meta-level: it clarifies what kind of normative job each approach is trying to do.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Rich 2016]] matters because it shows that rationality disputes are not only about who has the better experiments or more realistic psychology. They are also about what rationality research is for. It matters for philosophy because it reframes the rationality wars as a dispute about normative method. It matters for economics because it helps explain why benchmark choice remains contested even after decades of behavioral evidence. And it matters for politics and public policy because different rationality standards support different views of when guidance, correction, or institutional redesign are justified.

## Open questions

Can Rich's hybrid approach be operationalized in concrete empirical settings, or is it mainly a philosophical reconciliation? Does evaluating processes by axiomatic standards preserve the strengths of ecological rationality, or does it quietly reassert formalism at a higher level? And when behavioral public policy chooses a standard of rationality, can it ever avoid making exactly the kind of cost-benefit trade-off Rich describes?
