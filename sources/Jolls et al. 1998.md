# Jolls et al. 1998: A behavioral approach to law and economics
## Summary

[[Jolls et al. 1998]] argues that law and economics should incorporate more realistic assumptions about human behavior rather than relying only on the fully rational, fully self-interested, fully self-controlled agent of neoclassical models. The paper organizes that revision around three departures from the standard model: [[Bounded rationality]], bounded willpower, and [[Social preferences]]. In the current vault, that self-control branch is represented mainly through [[Time inconsistency]]. This source matters as an early foundational bridge from behavioral economics into legal analysis, institutional design, and later debates in [[Behavioral public policy]].

## Key claims

The paper claims that many familiar legal and policy questions look different once actual behavior is taken seriously. First, people exhibit [[Bounded rationality]], so legal rules cannot assume perfect information processing, unbiased judgment, or frictionless choice. Second, they exhibit bounded willpower, so short-run choices may conflict with long-run interests in ways that matter for legal design; in the current vault, that problem is treated mainly through [[Time inconsistency]]. Third, they exhibit [[Social preferences]], so fairness, reciprocity, acrimony, and scruples can shape behavior alongside narrow payoff maximization. The paper also claims that these behavioral findings matter not only for positive analysis of how people respond to law, but also for prescriptive and normative analysis, including the critique of simple anti-paternalist positions.

## Evidence and methods

This is a synthetic law-and-economics article rather than a new empirical study. It translates early behavioral findings into legal analysis by drawing on examples such as endowment effects and loss aversion, hyperbolic discounting and self-control problems, and fairness-related behavior in bargaining and exchange. Its method is explicitly architectural: the paper groups behavioral evidence into a reusable framework and then asks what follows for positive analysis, prescriptive institutional design, and normative debates about paternalism and legal goals.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows one of the earliest systematic attempts to convert behavioral economics into a theory of institutions rather than just a list of anomalies. It matters for economics because it challenges the behavioral assumptions embedded in law-and-economics analysis, for philosophy because it raises questions about welfare, preference respect, paternalism, and the comparative competence of citizens and regulators, and for politics because it shows how behavioral findings change the design and justification of legal rules. It is one of the most useful source notes in the vault for seeing how behavioral economics moved outward into law, regulation, and public reasoning.

## Open questions

One open question is which parts of the paper's three-part framework remain most useful once later field evidence and policy work become more fine-grained. Another is whether the article's critique of anti-paternalism still works once attention turns to regulator error and pluralist disputes over rationality benchmarks. A third is how much behavioral law and economics should aim to refine neoclassical analysis versus replace its underlying assumptions.
