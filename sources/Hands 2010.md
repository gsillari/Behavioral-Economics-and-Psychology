# Hands 2010: Economics, psychology and the history of consumer choice theory

## Summary

[[Hands 2010]] is a methodological and historical reconstruction of how psychology was handled inside twentieth-century consumer choice theory. The paper matters because it challenges one of the simplest stories economists and behavioral economists often tell: psychology was in early utility theory, then fully driven out by the ordinal and revealed-preference revolutions, and is only now coming back in. Hands argues that this is too simple.

The paper's core claim is that the real transition was selective rather than total. The ordinal revolution did reject hedonistic and introspective psychology, but it did not cleanly purge all psychology from consumer theory. Hands shows that figures such as Slutsky and Robbins still relied on psychologically meaningful ideas about purposive choice, expectations, and preference. The sharpest move toward explicit anti-psychologism came later and more specifically in Samuelson's early revealed-preference program, which Hands presents as a strongly behaviorist and operationalist attempt to ground consumer theory only in observable choice.

## Key claims

The first claim is that the standard `psychology in, psychology out, psychology back in` narrative is historically misleading. What disappeared in the ordinal revolution was mainly hedonistic, introspective, cardinal psychology, not every psychological element in the theory of choice.

The second claim is that [[Ordinal utility theory]] should not be read too quickly as a simple elimination of psychology. Hands shows that the move from cardinal to ordinal utility was also a move to different kinds of psychology, especially more austere and less introspective ways of talking about purposive choice, preference, and expectation.

The third claim is that the strongest behaviorist turn came with [[Revealed preference theory]]. Hands emphasizes that Samuelson's original project was not merely to tidy up ordinalism. It was an attempt to reconstruct consumer theory on operational and observational foundations, in line with a behaviorist view that distrusted unobservable mental states.

The fourth claim is that later textbook consumer theory stabilized through a compromise. Economics largely gave up hedonistic psychology, flirted with behaviorist operationalism, but ultimately retained a theory of purposive agents whose choices still express preferences rather than mere stimulus-response regularities. That is why economics never fully became behaviorist in the psychological sense.

The fifth claim is that this history matters for contemporary behavioral economics. If the older history of consumer choice theory already involved selective acceptance and rejection of different psychologies, then the relation between economics and psychology cannot be captured by a simple story of exclusion followed by return.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Hands 2010]] matters because it clarifies a deep background assumption in the current vault: behavioral economics did not arrive in a discipline that had ever been simply free of psychology. It arrived in a discipline that had already sorted among rival psychologies and rival scientific ideals. It matters for economics because it reconstructs how consumer theory stabilized. It matters for philosophy because it distinguishes hedonism, introspection, purposive choice, and [[Behaviorism]] rather than treating them as one thing. And it matters for politics and public policy because later appeals to revealed preference, welfare, and bias inherit this older methodological history.

## Open questions

Did revealed preference succeed because it solved a scientific problem, or because it offered a politically and methodologically acceptable compromise between psychology and formalism? How much of the old anti-psychology story still shapes how economists read their own history? And if economics never fully escaped psychology, what exactly changed when behavioral economics rose to prominence?
