# Kahneman and Frederick 2002: Representativeness revisited
## Summary

[[Kahneman and Frederick 2002]] revisits the representativeness tradition and gives it a clearer process interpretation. Instead of treating representativeness only as a rule of thumb that generates errors, the paper argues that many such judgments can be understood through [[Attribute substitution]]: a difficult question is answered by replacing it with an easier one.

In the current vault, this source matters because it upgrades the representativeness branch from a catalogue of statistical mistakes into a more general cognitive architecture. It therefore strengthens both [[Representativeness heuristic]] and [[Attribute substitution]], while also tying the representativeness tradition more directly to later dual-process language and to [[Fast and slow thinking]].

## Key claims

The first claim is that intuitive judgment often substitutes an easier attribute for a harder target. A hard question about probability, frequency, or prediction is answered through similarity, fluency, or some other more accessible cue.

The second claim is that representativeness should be read through that substitution process. In other words, similarity is not just one more input into judgment. It can become the effective answer to a different question from the one formally asked.

The third claim is that the classic heuristics literature should be reorganized with more attention to process and not only to error labels. In the current vault, that makes this source one of the clearest bridges between the older heuristics tradition and the later architecture of intuitive versus reflective cognition.

## Why this matters for the representativeness branch

This source changes the interpretation of the earlier papers. [[Kahneman and Tversky 1972]] shows that subjective probability is often judged by representativeness. [[Tversky and Kahneman 1981]] shows how this can produce striking errors such as [[Conjunction fallacy]]. [[Kahneman and Frederick 2002]] then adds a deeper explanation: the mind is not merely miscalculating probability, but often answering a different and easier question.

That makes the source especially important for the current topic structure. The representativeness branch now no longer sits only under statistical error. It also touches the architecture branch through [[Attribute substitution]] and [[Fast and slow thinking]].

## Attribute substitution and dual-process interpretation

The paper matters beyond representativeness because it helps the vault connect classic bias findings to a broader process model. In the current vault, [[Kahneman 2003]] had already introduced [[Attribute substitution]] at a high level. This 2002 source now gives that concept a stronger direct tie to the representativeness literature itself.

That means the topic boundary between [[Representativeness and Statistical Error]] and [[Anchoring and Cognitive Substitution]] becomes more permeable. The topics should remain distinct, but this source makes it clear that the representativeness branch is not only about norm violation. It is also about how intuitive cognition generates answers in the first place.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows a characteristic move in mature behavioral economics: do not stop at anomaly description, but ask what cognitive process is producing the anomaly. It matters for economics because it offers a more general explanation of why formally irrelevant cues can influence probabilistic judgment. It matters for philosophy because it complicates the evaluation of irrationality: the problem may be not only a wrong answer, but a substitution of questions. It matters for politics because many real-world judgments in public discourse and policy are driven by accessible impressions rather than by disciplined evaluation of the target problem.

This source is therefore especially useful when the course moves from first-generation bias findings to second-generation interpretations of cognitive process.

## Open questions

How general is attribute substitution as an explanation of intuitive judgment? When does it illuminate classic bias findings, and when does it merely redescribe them? And should the vault eventually place representativeness more centrally at the border between statistical error and cognitive architecture?
