# Arkes and Blumer 1985: The psychology of sunk cost
## Summary

[[Arkes and Blumer 1985]] studies the [[Sunk cost effect]], the tendency to continue an endeavor after money, time, or effort has already been invested. The paper argues that people often persist in failing or unattractive projects because abandoning them would make prior expenditure feel wasteful. In the current vault, this source matters because it gives behavioral economics a clear example of how actual choice can diverge from the forward-looking logic of standard decision theory even when the past investment is normatively irrelevant.

## Key claims

The paper claims that sunk costs can distort decision-making by increasing commitment to an endeavor that should instead be evaluated only on expected future costs and benefits. It argues that one important psychological mechanism is the desire not to appear wasteful. The paper also reports evidence that people who have incurred sunk costs sometimes become more optimistic about a project's likely success, as if the prior investment itself justifies continued commitment. In the paper's own framing, the tendency to throw good money after bad is broadly consistent with [[Prospect Theory]] and difficult to reconcile with simple forward-looking rational choice.

## Evidence and methods

The paper combines a field study with questionnaire-style experiments. The best-known field evidence shows that theatergoers who paid more for a season subscription attended more plays in the following months, even though the money had already been spent. The paper also uses hypothetical scenarios to test how prior investments affect willingness to continue projects. Together, these designs show that the effect appears both in everyday behavior and in more stylized judgment tasks.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it is one of the cleanest demonstrations that decision-making can be shaped by normatively irrelevant past costs. It matters for economics because it challenges the idea that agents evaluate only future consequences, for philosophy because it raises questions about rationality, regret, waste, and the interpretation of commitment, and for politics because public institutions often face sunk-cost pressures in projects, wars, procurement, and infrastructure decisions. It is a foundational page in the vault for thinking about persistence, escalation, and the psychological grip of past investment.

## Open questions

One open question is whether the sunk cost effect is best understood as loss aversion, self-justification, norm compliance, or some mixture of these. Another is how strongly the effect survives market experience and organizational accountability. A third is how institutions can reduce sunk-cost distortions without encouraging irresponsibility or short-termism.
