# Achtypi et al. 2021: The endowment effect and beliefs about the market

## Summary

[[Achtypi et al. 2021]] reinterprets the [[Endowment effect]] through beliefs about market prices. The paper asks whether buyers and sellers differ because ownership changes the intrinsic value of the good, or because the two sides use market-price beliefs differently when deciding what counts as an acceptable transaction.

The central result is that ownership does not appear to change participants' beliefs about either the product's quality or its appropriate market price. Sellers' willingness to accept is close to what they believe the product should cost in the market, given its quality. Buyers' willingness to pay is lower: buyers want a discount relative to the market price, so their offers often reflect the demand for a good deal rather than a belief that the item is worth little.

## Key claims

The first claim is that the endowment effect need not be explained by ownership-induced changes in intrinsic preference. In the experiments, owners and nonowners share similar beliefs about product quality and market-price distributions.

The second claim is that buyers and sellers appear to treat the valuation task differently. Sellers anchor their WTA near perceived market worth, while buyers offer less than perceived market worth because they are reluctant to overpay for an item they do not especially need.

The third claim is methodological. Even incentive-compatible procedures such as BDM may not reveal a clean underlying preference if the reported buying or selling price also reflects beliefs about market price, product quality, and transaction fairness.

The fourth claim is interpretive. The paper does not prove that [[Loss aversion]] plays no role in endowment effects, but it argues that loss aversion should not be assumed when a market-belief or good-deal account can explain the observed WTA-WTP gap.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source is important because it keeps the endowment-effect branch from becoming too mechanically loss-aversion-centered. [[Kahneman et al. 1990]] remains the canonical experimental anchor, and [[Horowitz and McConnell 2002]] remains a major review anchor, but [[Achtypi et al. 2021]] adds a later pressure point: the WTA-WTP gap may partly reflect market cognition and deal evaluation rather than a direct ownership-induced shift in taste.

The paper also strengthens the link between [[Endowment effect]], [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]], and [[Constructed preferences]]. The important point is not that preferences are arbitrary. It is that elicited valuations may mix several things that standard interpretation tries to separate: desire for the object, beliefs about market price, beliefs about quality, and the acceptability of the transaction.

This makes the paper relevant to [[Procedure invariance]] as well. The BDM mechanism may be formally incentive-compatible, but participants may still interpret buying and selling as different tasks. A selling price can become an estimate of acceptable market compensation, while a buying price can become a demand for a bargain.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this source matters because it complicates a simple behavioral-economics story. The endowment effect is often taught as a straightforward application of [[Loss aversion]]: ownership creates a reference point, giving up the object is a loss, and sellers demand more than buyers will pay. Achtypi et al. show why that explanation may be incomplete.

It matters for economics because it questions how to read incentivized valuation data. It matters for philosophy because it raises a familiar measurement problem: what exactly is a stated valuation measuring? It matters for politics and policy because compensation, market design, and cost-benefit analysis often rely on WTP and WTA measures that may partly express market-price norms rather than settled welfare.

## Open questions

How general is the market-belief account outside ordinary consumer goods? Does it explain endowment effects for risky assets, public goods, environmental goods, or deeply personal possessions? When does psychological ownership or emotional attachment become strong enough that market-price beliefs no longer do most of the explanatory work?
