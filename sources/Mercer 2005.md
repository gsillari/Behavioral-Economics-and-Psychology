# Mercer 2005: Prospect theory and political science

## Summary

[[Mercer 2005]] is a review essay on the uptake of [[Prospect Theory]] in political science. Jonathan Mercer argues that the theory has had major influence in international relations, especially security studies, but far less in other areas of political science. The paper's distinctive contribution is to ask whether that limited uptake reflects genuine theoretical problems or a broader reluctance among political scientists to use behavioral assumptions.

## Key claims

The first claim is that prospect theory is one of the most influential behavioral theories in the social sciences and has clear potential political applications.

The second claim is that the theory faces real translation problems when moved from laboratory tasks to complex political decisions in the field. Reference points, domain coding, collective actors, and strategic interaction are harder to specify in politics than in stylized experiments.

The third claim is that these difficulties do not fully explain the political-science response. Mercer suggests that disciplinary resistance to behavioral explanation is itself part of the story.

## Evidence and scope

This is a review rather than a new experiment. Its value in the current vault is interpretive and field-facing: it maps where prospect theory has been politically productive, where it has stalled, and why.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows how a core behavioral model travels across disciplinary boundaries. It matters for economics because it treats [[Prospect Theory]] as more than a consumer-choice model. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about external validity, benchmark transfer, and the status of behavioral explanation in political theory. It matters for politics because it identifies foreign policy, security, and framing as especially fertile political uses of the theory.

## Open questions

When do laboratory regularities about gains and losses survive the move to collective and strategic political settings? Are the main obstacles conceptual, empirical, or disciplinary? And which parts of prospect theory travel best into politics: [[Loss aversion]], framing, reference dependence, or probability weighting?
