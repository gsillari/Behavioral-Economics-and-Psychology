# Chapman and Johnson 2002: Incorporating the irrelevant
## Summary

[[Chapman and Johnson 2002]] is one of the clearest synthetic sources on [[Anchoring]] currently available in the vault. Its main contribution is to show that anchoring is not a narrow effect confined to rough numerical estimates. It also shapes judgments of belief and judgments of value, which means the branch reaches both into ordinary inference and into valuation.

In the current vault, this source matters because it sharpens the anchoring page beyond the older sequence of [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]], [[Strack and Mussweiler 1997]], and [[Epley and Gilovich 2001]]. Chapman and Johnson make the branch easier to teach by organizing it around a few clear distinctions: anchors can shape factual belief as well as willingness to pay; irrelevant anchors can matter even when their evidential status is weak; and anchoring can therefore be a judgment problem and a valuation problem at once.

## Key claims

The first claim is that anchors influence judgments of belief. Estimates of probability, frequency, quantity, and plausibility can all be pulled by prior numbers or salient starting points.

The second claim is that anchors also influence judgments of value. This matters because it links anchoring to pricing, valuation, and consumer behavior rather than treating it only as a laboratory estimation effect.

The third claim is that anchoring should be understood as a family of effects rather than as one single mechanism. In the current vault, that matters because it lets [[Anchoring]] be more structured: classic adjustment, selective accessibility, and value construction now look like neighboring articulations of one branch rather than like redundant replications.

## Why it matters for anchoring

This chapter gives the anchoring branch a broader architecture. [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] made anchoring canonical. [[Strack and Mussweiler 1997]] then gave it one major process model, and [[Epley and Gilovich 2001]] gave it another by distinguishing self-generated from externally provided anchors. [[Chapman and Johnson 2002]] matters because it sits at the branch level rather than only at the effect or mechanism level. It shows why the literature keeps spilling from factual estimation into valuation and choice.

That makes it particularly useful for connecting [[Anchoring]] to [[Constructed preferences]] and [[Coherent arbitrariness]]. Anchors do not merely distort beliefs about the world; they can also help construct what looks like stable value.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this source matters because it shows how one cognitive effect can travel across domains. It matters for economics because anchoring can influence valuation and demand, not only probability judgments. It matters for philosophy because it complicates the assumption that irrelevant information is simply screened off by rational agents. It matters for politics because anchors enter public communication through suggested ranges, targets, forecasts, and negotiating baselines.

## Open questions

Which anchoring effects are best explained by selective accessibility, which by adjustment, and which by valuation construction? And how should the vault distinguish useful anchors from genuinely irrelevant ones without collapsing that distinction into hindsight?
