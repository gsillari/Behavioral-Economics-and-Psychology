# Soll et al. 2015: A User's Guide to Debiasing

## Summary

[[Soll et al. 2015]] is the clearest synthetic map of the debiasing literature currently in the vault. Its central contribution is to organize debiasing around a simple but powerful distinction: some interventions modify the decision maker, while others modify the environment. In the current vault, that makes it the best bridge between the classic Fischhoff chapter and later work in policy, design, and institutional choice.

The chapter is especially useful didactically because it treats debiasing as a practical design problem rather than as a list of isolated tricks. It also introduces the idea of decision readiness, which helps explain why some interventions fail even when they supply the right information.

## Key claims

The first claim is that debiasing should be understood as improving judgments and decisions that depart from objective or normative standards, including both coherence failures and systematic misperceptions of reality.

The second claim is that debiasing techniques divide broadly into two families. One family modifies the decision maker through education, rules, strategies, or tools. The other modifies the environment by changing how information is presented, what defaults are used, or how choice is structured.

The third claim is that the right method depends on the source of the problem, the readiness of the chooser, and the costs of intervention. The chapter therefore turns debiasing into a comparative design problem rather than a one-size-fits-all corrective project.

## Evidence and methods

This is a handbook review chapter. It synthesizes decades of judgment-and-decision research and organizes the literature around sources of bias, decision readiness, and alternative debiasing techniques. Its method is programmatic and pedagogical rather than experimental.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it gives the debiasing branch its cleanest modern teaching architecture. It matters for economics and policy because it links bias correction to institutional and environmental design rather than only to individual reasoning skills. It matters for philosophy because it clarifies that improving decisions is not reducible to making people deliberate harder; sometimes the environment has to do more of the work.

## Open questions

How far can the modify-the-environment approach go before it becomes steering rather than repair? When is decision readiness the real limiting factor? And should the vault eventually split environmental debiasing into finer families such as informational redesign, decision aids, and default architecture?
