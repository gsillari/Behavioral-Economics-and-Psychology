# Epley and Gilovich 2001: Putting adjustment back in the anchoring and adjustment heuristic
## Summary

[[Epley and Gilovich 2001]] reopens one of the central mechanistic questions inside the anchoring literature: when people are influenced by an anchor, are they really adjusting away from a starting value, or is something else going on? The paper argues that the adjustment story should not be discarded altogether. Instead, it applies selectively. In the current vault, the source matters because it refines [[Anchoring]] from a broad empirical regularity into a more differentiated mechanism story.

The paper's main contribution is to distinguish between self-generated anchors and experimenter-provided anchors. Epley and Gilovich argue that these two cases do not work the same way. When people generate an anchor themselves, especially one known to be close to the right answer but not identical to it, they often seem to engage in effortful adjustment. When anchors are simply imposed from outside, that adjustment process is much weaker or absent. This gives the vault a more precise account of why some anchoring effects look like insufficient adjustment and others do not.

## Key claims

The paper's first claim is that the standard anchoring-and-adjustment explanation was rejected too quickly. Earlier evidence had made it look as if adjustment was not doing much explanatory work, but Epley and Gilovich argue that this conclusion came from studying the wrong kinds of anchors.

The second claim is that self-generated anchors activate a qualitatively different process from externally supplied anchors. People can begin from a plausible initial value and then effortfully move away from it toward what seems like a better estimate. In those cases, anchoring effects can genuinely reflect insufficient adjustment rather than only passive acceptance of an externally suggested number.

The third claim is that this distinction helps explain why some anchoring manipulations seem sensitive to cognitive effort and others do not. In the current vault, this matters because it gives [[Anchoring]] a more layered architecture: one anchoring family is tied to deliberate adjustment, while another may need a different mechanism.

## Evidence and methods

This is an experimental paper built around judgment tasks designed to separate self-generated from experimenter-provided anchors. The studies examine participants' verbal reports and behavioral responses under different conditions to test whether people show signs of active adjustment. The key result is not just that anchoring occurs. It is that the process differs depending on the source of the anchor.

Methodologically, the paper matters because it is unusually focused on mechanism rather than only effect size. The anchoring literature often asks whether estimates move toward anchors. This paper asks how that movement happens and under what conditions the classic adjustment story is actually warranted.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Epley and Gilovich 2001]] matters because it shows a more mature stage of behavioral research. It is not just documenting another bias. It is asking what kind of cognitive process a bias actually reflects. That matters for economics because behavioral models become more useful when their mechanisms are better specified, for philosophy because the paper complicates the interpretation of bias and deliberation, and for politics because many interventions designed to reduce bias implicitly assume a process model of how judgment works.

The paper is especially helpful for PPE because it shows why behavioral findings should not be treated as flat labels. If different anchoring effects come from different processes, then their normative interpretation and practical correctability may also differ. That is a valuable lesson well beyond anchoring itself.
