# Binns 2018: Fairness in machine learning
## Summary

[[Binns 2018]] connects fair-machine-learning debates to moral and political philosophy. Rather than defending one metric directly, it asks which older philosophical ideas about discrimination, equality, and justice are echoed in contemporary technical definitions of fairness. In the current vault, the paper matters because it stops the fairness-criteria branch from becoming a purely technical catalog.

Its main role is interpretive. It shows that different fairness definitions are not just alternative engineering tools. They embody rival moral ideas about equal treatment, distributive concern, and the significance of group-based disadvantage.

In the current vault, [[Narayanan 2026]] is a later system-level extension of this point. Binns shows that fairness definitions are philosophically loaded; Narayanan argues that even philosophically interpreted model metrics still miss the larger fairness question if they ignore the sociotechnical decision-making system.

## Key claims

The first claim is that formal fairness definitions in machine learning encode deeper philosophical assumptions about discrimination and justice.

The second claim is that no single fairness metric can simply be read off from ordinary moral language.

The third claim is that debates over disparate impact, equality of opportunity, and related criteria echo longstanding disputes in political philosophy.

The fourth claim is that fair machine learning needs conceptual interpretation, not only technical implementation.

The fifth claim is that fairness discussions should remain attentive to social context and to the institutions in which algorithms operate.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Binns 2018]] matters because it makes the fairness branch legible as philosophy as well as computer science. It matters for politics because it links algorithmic debate to broader questions of equality and discrimination. It matters for philosophy because it treats fairness metrics as partially formalized theories of justice.
