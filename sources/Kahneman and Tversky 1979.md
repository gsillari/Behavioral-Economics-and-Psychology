# Kahneman and Tversky 1979: Prospect theory
## Summary

Kahneman and Tversky's 1979 paper introduces [[Prospect Theory]] as a descriptive alternative to [[Expected utility theory]] for choice under risk.
The paper does not merely add another anomaly to the behavioral record.
It tries to reorganize several previously disconnected anomalies into a single psychological architecture.
In the current vault, it is the most important single source for understanding how behavioral economics moved from criticism of the classical benchmark to a positive model of choice.

The paper matters because it links three ideas that now structure much of the wiki.
First, outcomes are evaluated relative to a benchmark rather than only as final wealth states.
Second, losses carry more psychological weight than equivalent gains.
Third, the impact of risky prospects is not captured by a simple linear reading of objective probabilities.
That combination makes the paper foundational for [[Loss aversion]], [[Reference dependence]], [[Framing effects]], and the modern contrast with [[Expected utility theory]].

## Key claims

The paper's first claim is that [[Expected utility theory]] is often a weak descriptive model of actual risky choice.
People do not typically evaluate prospects only in terms of final asset positions.
They code outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point, and that coding changes the shape of choice.

The second claim is that the value function over gains and losses is asymmetric.
It is typically concave in gains, convex in losses, and steeper for losses than for gains.
That structure later becomes one of the central intuitions behind [[Loss aversion]].

The third claim is that the psychological impact of probabilities is not exhausted by their objective magnitude.
The paper's treatment of decision weights helps explain why certainty can carry disproportionate force and why people can look risk-averse in gains yet risk-seeking in losses.
In the current vault, this matters because the theory is not just "loss aversion plus framing."
It is an integrated account of valuation, benchmark coding, and the treatment of risk.

The fourth claim is that descriptions matter.
Equivalent problems can elicit different choices when they are framed in ways that shift which outcomes are experienced as gains or losses.
This makes [[Framing effects]] central rather than peripheral to the theory.

## Conceptual architecture

The paper's architecture has three main parts.
The first is the reference point.
People do not evaluate outcomes from nowhere.
They evaluate them relative to a status quo, expectation, entitlement, aspiration level, or salient baseline.
Without that benchmark structure, neither gains nor losses are psychologically intelligible.

The second part is the value function.
The value function is not simply a utility function renamed.
It is explicitly defined over deviations from the reference point.
Its curvature captures diminishing sensitivity within gains and within losses, while its asymmetry captures the greater force of losses.
In the current vault, this is what makes [[Reference dependence]] and [[Loss aversion]] internal parts of the theory rather than detachable add-ons.

The third part is the treatment of risky prospects.
The paper introduces decision weights to explain why probabilities do not translate straightforwardly into psychological impact.
That helps explain the certainty effect and the unstable relation between objective risk and subjective response.
In other words, prospect theory changes both what is being evaluated and how uncertainty enters that evaluation.

## Limits of the original formulation

The original paper is foundational, but it is not the final form of the theory.
Its formal treatment is designed mainly for risky prospects with a small number of outcomes.
It uses a separable weighting scheme that transforms outcome probabilities individually rather than cumulatively over ranked outcomes.
That gives the 1979 paper enormous pedagogical and theoretical force, but it also creates limits.
Most importantly, the original weighting scheme does not generalize cleanly to more complex prospects and can violate stochastic dominance.

In the current vault, that limitation should be made explicit because later readers often remember only the value function and the gain-loss contrast.
The original paper gave the field its breakthrough architecture.
It did not yet provide the most general or formally stable version of prospect theory.

## How later cumulative prospect theory responds

[[Tversky and Kahneman 1992]] keeps the central behavioral intuitions of the 1979 paper while replacing the original separable weighting scheme with cumulative, rank-dependent decision weights.
That later formulation extends the theory to risky and uncertain prospects with any finite number of outcomes, preserves the reference-point structure and [[Loss aversion]], allows separate weighting functions for gains and losses, and avoids the dominance problem that burdened the original version.

For the purposes of this vault, the right way to read the sequence is therefore cumulative rather than competitive.
[[Kahneman and Tversky 1979]] is the founding breakthrough.
[[Tversky and Kahneman 1992]] is the major repair and extension that turns that breakthrough into a more general framework.

## Relation to the classical benchmark

The most important contrast is with [[Expected utility theory]].
Expected utility theory evaluates final states and assumes that the same formal structure can represent rational choice across gain and loss cases.
Kahneman and Tversky argue that this misses the psychological importance of benchmarks, asymmetry, and the special force of certainty.

This is why [[Allais 1953]] matters in the background.
The [[Allais paradox]] shows that expected utility has descriptive trouble.
[[Kahneman and Tversky 1979]] matters because it responds to that trouble with a theory rather than only with a counterexample.
In the current vault, the two sources should therefore be read together.
Allais helps explain why the classical benchmark was vulnerable.
Kahneman and Tversky explain what a more psychologically realistic alternative looks like.

## Evidence and methods

The paper is built from stylized experimental choice problems rather than field observation or econometric identification.
Participants compare sure outcomes with lotteries and reveal stable patterns that conflict with standard expected-utility predictions.
The significance of the paper is not that it discovers only one anomaly.
It groups several recurring anomalies into a compact framework with interpretable psychological parts.

Methodologically, that matters for the vault because the source sits between two styles of work.
It is more formally organized than the earlier anomaly tradition.
At the same time, it is still rooted in controlled choice problems rather than in the field evidence later emphasized by [[DellaVigna 2009]].
This makes it an ideal bridge page for PPE students: it retains the clarity of formal decision theory while introducing a more realistic model of actual behavior.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this is one of the core texts in the entire vault.
It matters for economics because it offers a structured descriptive alternative to one of the central models of rational choice.
It matters for philosophy because it complicates simple views of rational agency, welfare, and the meaning of consistency.
It matters for politics because framing, entitlements, and perceived losses help explain why citizens and institutions react differently to formally similar policies.

The paper is especially useful for PPE because it forces movement across the three disciplines rather than allowing them to stay separate.
Its economic importance comes from the benchmark contrast with expected utility.
Its philosophical importance comes from its challenge to final-state evaluation and stable preference interpretation.
Its political importance comes from the way losses, benchmarks, and frames structure public resistance, support, and institutional legitimacy.

## Open questions

One open question is how much of the original 1979 formulation should be treated as a stable psychological account and how much has been refined or partially replaced by later work such as [[Tversky and Kahneman 1992]] on cumulative prospect theory and related models.
Another is how far the original framework travels beyond laboratory risky choice into political judgment, administrative behavior, and public policy.

A third question is normative.
When prospect-theory patterns diverge from [[Expected utility theory]], should that be read as evidence of irrationality, as evidence that the classical benchmark is descriptively too thin, or as evidence that there are multiple legitimate ways to model choice?
That question is exactly why the paper remains central to the PPE orientation of the vault.
