# Rabin and Thaler 2001

## Summary

[[Rabin and Thaler 2001]] is the cleanest short source in the vault on why ordinary small- and moderate-stakes [[Risk attitudes|risk aversion]] cannot be explained by the standard expected-utility story that risk aversion comes from the concavity of utility over lifetime wealth. The paper is a Journal of Economic Perspectives `Anomalies` article, but its role in the vault is foundational: it turns a familiar explanation of risk aversion into a calibration problem.

The central claim is that if an expected-utility maximizer rejects small favorable gambles because her marginal utility of wealth declines over wealth, then the same utility curve implies absurd aversion to much larger favorable gambles. The problem is not a special functional form. Rabin and Thaler draw on [[Matthew Rabin|Rabin]]'s calibration theorem to argue that the implication follows from increasing, concave utility itself.

## Main claims

The first claim is negative and methodological. Expected utility can represent risk aversion, but the usual wealth-curvature explanation cannot plausibly explain ordinary aversion to small or moderate monetary risks. If rejecting a modest positive-expected-value gamble is attributed to sharply diminishing marginal utility of wealth, the implied decline in marginal utility is so steep that the agent should reject extraordinarily favorable large-stakes bets as well.

The second claim is diagnostic. Economists often use expected utility literally but not seriously: they invoke it to justify small-stakes risk aversion in experiments, insurance choices, and asset-pricing examples without checking what the same calibrated utility curve implies elsewhere. This is why the source belongs directly on [[Calibration]].

The third claim is constructive. Rabin and Thaler argue that the better descriptive explanation for ordinary small-stakes risk aversion combines [[Loss aversion]] with [[Mental accounting]]. People evaluate many risks one at a time, code losses relative to a reference point, and overweight losses relative to equivalent gains. In that setting, rejection of small favorable gambles is no longer explained by global concavity over lifetime wealth; it is explained by local gain-loss coding and narrow evaluation.

The fourth claim concerns aggregation and money-pump arguments. The paper uses Samuelson's famous colleague example to argue that people often reject an isolated favorable gamble while accepting many such gambles bundled together. Rabin and Thaler treat this not as a small mistake about aggregation but as evidence for narrow bracketing or [[Myopic loss aversion]]: the same risk can be evaluated differently when seen as a local loss possibility or as part of a broader portfolio.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Rabin and Thaler 2001]] matters because it separates three questions that are often blurred. First, can expected utility formally represent risk aversion? Yes. Second, can wealth curvature plausibly explain ordinary small-stakes risk aversion? Rabin and Thaler argue no. Third, what should replace it descriptively? Their answer is a behavioral architecture built from loss aversion, mental accounting, and narrow framing.

This is philosophically important because it shows how a model can be elegant, normative, and tractable while still being descriptively misleading when used outside its proper range. It is economically important because the argument affects experiments, insurance demand, asset pricing, and welfare interpretation. It is politically relevant because risk communication and consumer protection often concern exactly the small-scale insurance and warranty choices that the paper says expected utility misdescribes.
