# Hortal 2025: Rationality wars
## Summary

[[Hortal 2025]] examines the contemporary "rationality wars" across economics, psychology, philosophy, and [[Behavioral public policy]]. The paper argues that disputes between formal or logical models of rationality and ecological or context-sensitive models are not merely empirical disagreements. They reflect different epistemological aims and disciplinary standards. In this vault, the source anchors [[Rationality wars]], [[Ecological rationality]], and [[Behavioral public policy]], while also reinforcing a pluralist critique of single-standard accounts of rationality.

## Key claims

The paper claims that rationality cannot be reduced to a single universal framework without distorting the purposes of different disciplines. It contrasts the heuristics-and-biases tradition, associated with formal benchmarks and error detection, with ecological rationality, associated with environmental fit and adaptive success. Drawing on Gustavo Bueno's distinction between concepts and ideas, the paper argues that rationality should be treated as both discipline-specific and philosophically comparative. The practical upshot is pluralism rather than reductionism.

## Evidence and methods

This is a conceptual and philosophical article rather than an original experimental paper. It reconstructs the historical and theoretical background of rationality debates, then applies that framework to current conflicts among behavioral economics, psychology, and policy. The paper uses the contemporary opposition between logical rationality and ecological rationality as its main live case and connects that dispute to broader philosophical questions about norms, methods, and explanation.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Hortal 2025]] makes explicit the meta-level question that sits underneath much of behavioral economics: by what standard are we calling a judgment rational or irrational? It matters for philosophy because it clarifies competing conceptions of rationality, for economics because it reframes familiar behavioral findings as benchmark-dependent rather than self-interpreting, and for politics because it shows why behavioral public policy cannot avoid normative choices when it decides which cognitive patterns count as mistakes worth correcting.

## Open questions

One open question is how policy makers should choose among competing rationality frameworks when they point toward different interventions. Another is whether pluralism can be operationalized in concrete institutions without collapsing back into one dominant benchmark. A third is how far ecological rationality and heuristics-and-biases can be reconciled rather than merely balanced.
