# Finucane et al. 2000: The affect heuristic in judgments of risks and benefits
## Summary

[[Finucane et al. 2000]] argues that people often judge risks and benefits through affective impressions rather than by analytically separating probability, harm, and gain. In the current vault, the paper matters because it adds an important post-1974 extension to the heuristics tradition: not only recall, similarity, and anchors, but also global positive or negative feeling can structure judgment under uncertainty.

The core insight is that affect can create an inverse relation between perceived risk and perceived benefit. When an activity or technology feels good, people tend to judge it as more beneficial and less risky. When it feels bad, they tend to judge it as more risky and less beneficial. That makes the paper especially useful for the vault's bridge between [[Heuristics and Biases]], risk perception, and policy communication.

## Key claims

The first claim is that judgments of risk and benefit are not always made by separately estimating two independent dimensions. Instead, they can be guided by a single evaluative impression or affective tag.

The second claim is that this mechanism helps explain why perceived risks and benefits are often negatively correlated in public judgment, even when a more analytic perspective might treat them as independently variable. In the current vault, this matters because it shows a distinct route by which intuitive judgment can depart from formal reasoning.

The third claim is that reliance on affect becomes stronger under time pressure and weaker under more deliberative conditions. That gives the source a clear relation to the broader [[Fast and slow thinking]] branch, even though its main home is still the heuristics literature.

## Evidence and methods

This is an experimental and judgment-focused paper. The authors study how people evaluate the risks and benefits of different activities and technologies, and they test whether affective impressions help explain the structure of those evaluations. The method is important because it broadens the older heuristics literature from classic probability-judgment tasks to more policy-relevant judgments about hazards, technologies, and social risks.

In the current vault, the paper works best as a mechanism-expansion source. It does not replace [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]]. It shows how the heuristics tradition grew into a richer account of intuitive judgment in domains closer to public controversy and policy.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Finucane et al. 2000]] matters because it shows how apparently factual judgments about risk and benefit are partly shaped by value-laden and affective impressions. It matters for economics because preferences over risky activities are not formed only through explicit trade-off calculation. It matters for philosophy because it complicates the relation between reason, emotion, and normatively defensible judgment. It matters for politics because public responses to technologies, regulation, and collective risk often depend on the affective framing of an issue as much as on formal evidence.

The paper is particularly useful for PPE because it helps explain why public disagreement about risk is often not just ignorance of facts. It may reflect different affective constructions of the same object of judgment.
