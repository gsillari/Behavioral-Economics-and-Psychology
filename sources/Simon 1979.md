# Simon 1979: Rational decision making in business organizations
## Summary

[[Simon 1979]] is one of the richest Simon sources in the vault because it does not merely introduce bounded rationality in outline. It restates the whole project at a mature stage, after Simon's work on organizations, administrative behavior, and decision theory had already reshaped several fields. The paper matters because it argues that descriptive decision theory is not a peripheral curiosity inside economics. It belongs near the center of the economic sciences once the object of study is understood as actual decision making in real organizations rather than idealized optimization by frictionless agents.

In that sense, this source should function as a spine text for the current vault. [[Simon 1955]] provides the founding break with global rationality. [[Simon 1956]] adds the importance of environmental structure. [[Simon 1979]] then gathers those moves into a broader vision: rationality must be procedural, organization-sensitive, empirically testable, and compatible with finite cognition.
[[Simon 1963]] belongs in that same sequence because it states the methodological side of Simon's project in unusually direct terms: Friedman's `as-if` rationality is not enough, and idealization has to be defended by continuity of approximation rather than by indifference to false actor-level assumptions.

## Key claims

The first central claim is that economics should not confine itself to market equilibria while leaving the internal processes of choice to the margins. Simon argues that decision making inside business organizations is itself a central economic phenomenon, not an optional psychological supplement. This matters because it widens the object of behavioral economics beyond isolated choice anomalies.

The second claim is that theories of rationality should be judged by whether they illuminate actual decision procedures. Simon criticizes the standard maximizing picture not simply because it is unrealistic in detail, but because it often avoids direct contact with the processes that agents and organizations actually use when they search, compare, simplify, and stop. This keeps the pressure on ideal theory at a deeper level than many later anomaly papers do.

The third claim is that bounded rationality is not a small correction to optimization theory. It is a different image of agency. Agents operate under information costs, search costs, attention limits, computational limits, and organizational constraints. Their rationality is therefore procedural and satisficing rather than global and omniscient.

The fourth claim is that economics itself behaves more pragmatically than its idealized agents. Simon notes, in effect, that economists often accept workable theories because they perform well enough for some purposes, even while those same economists describe agents as if they solved optimization problems of impossible complexity. That contrast matters because it reveals a methodological asymmetry inside the classical benchmark.

The fifth claim is that organization is not an afterthought. Firms and administrative systems structure attention, information flow, authority, and choice sets. That means rationality cannot be understood only at the level of an isolated chooser. It must also be understood through the environments and institutions in which choice is embedded.

## Evidence and methods

The paper is synthetic and programmatic rather than experimental. Simon draws on organizational analysis, decision theory, and the broader development of the economic sciences to argue for a different research agenda. In the current vault, that makes the source especially useful as a bridge between formal benchmark critique and later behavioral-economics field formation.

That is also why this source should not be read as just another statement of [[Bounded rationality]]. It is richer than that. The paper helps show why bounded rationality became foundational: it connects the concept to firms, institutions, testability, and the general scope of economics as a discipline. It therefore strengthens existing core pages better than it would support a large number of narrow new concepts.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Simon 1979]] matters because it brings together three questions that the field often keeps separate. It matters for economics because it argues that decision procedures inside organizations belong within economic analysis rather than outside it. It matters for philosophy because it treats rationality as a procedural and institutionally embedded achievement rather than as mere abstract coherence. It matters for politics and public policy because it implies that institutions should be designed for finite agents operating in structured environments, not for impossible maximizers.

Pedagogically, this is one of the best Simon texts for understanding why bounded rationality is not just an early precursor to behavioral economics but one of its deepest background commitments. It also helps PPE students see that behavioral economics is not only a psychology of mistakes. It is also a theory of how real agents and real organizations make decisions under constraint.

## Open questions

How far should the vault distinguish Simon's mature organizational decision theory from later behavioral economics centered on judgment biases and risky choice? Is Simon best read as a precursor to ecological rationality, a founder of bounded rationality, or a broader architect of the economic sciences of finite agents? And how much of modern behavioral public policy still depends on Simon's organizational and procedural picture even when it does not cite him directly?
