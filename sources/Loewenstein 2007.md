# Loewenstein 2007: Exotic preferences

## Summary

[[Loewenstein 2007]] is the top-level work note for *Exotic Preferences: Behavioral Economics and Human Motivation*. In the current vault, the book matters as a curated Loewenstein-centered map of behavioral economics beyond the classic heuristics-and-biases core. It gathers work on intertemporal choice, visceral influences, social utility, prediction of future tastes, emotion, bargaining, and valuation, and it therefore gives the vault a richer picture of motivation than one gets from judgment errors or risky choice alone.

The book is not best treated as one continuous monograph with a single unfolding argument. It is better understood as a selective collection of important papers framed by an original introduction and short section introductions. For the current wiki, that makes it valuable as a structured source family: some chapters are already represented elsewhere in the vault, while others would deepen thin but important branches such as [[Projection bias]], [[Time inconsistency]], [[Emotions in decision-making]], and the social side of preference formation.

## Structural map

The book is organized into six parts:

- The rise of psychological explanations in economics
- Emotion and social utility
- Preference reversals and valuation
- Time, projection, and intertemporal choice
- Visceral influences and risk
- Neural and broader motivational extensions

In the current vault, the most important ingested units are Chapter 3 on psychological explanations in intertemporal choice, Chapter 8 on self-serving bias in bargaining, Chapter 9 on joint and separate evaluation, Chapter 13 on [[Projection bias]], Chapter 15 on anomalies in intertemporal choice, Chapter 18 on visceral influences, and Chapter 19 on risk as feelings. The strongest remaining future candidates are the introduction, Chapter 11 on prediction of tastes, Chapter 12 on mispredicting the endowment effect, and Chapter 17 on mental accounting of savings and debt.

Two chapters are already effectively represented by existing source notes and should therefore be mapped rather than duplicated: the chapter on coherent arbitrariness belongs with [[Ariely et al. 2003]], and the chapter on incidental emotion and valuation belongs with [[Lerner et al. 2004]].

## Main claims

At the work level, the book's importance is that it broadens what counts as a behavioral explanation. Rather than treating departures from the standard model mainly as statistical or inferential mistakes, it shows how behavior is shaped by motivation, emotion, anticipation, social context, bodily state, and failures to predict future tastes.

That matters because some of the most important deviations from standard theory do not look like simple irrationality. They look instead like evidence that preference is unstable across contexts, projected incorrectly into the future, or transformed by visceral and social forces that standard models treat as exogenous or irrelevant.

The book also helps situate [[George Loewenstein]] more accurately inside the field. He is not only a contributor to isolated topics such as empathy gaps or intertemporal anomalies. He is one of the figures who pushed behavioral economics toward a thicker account of human motivation.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this book matters because it deepens behavioral economics along all three dimensions. It matters for economics because it challenges thin accounts of utility and stable preference. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about welfare, preference formation, anticipation, weakness of will, and the authority of present versus future selves. It matters for politics and public policy because many of the mechanisms it studies bear directly on savings, addiction, risk regulation, bargaining, consumer protection, and institutional design.

In the current vault, the book is especially important because it gives shape to branches that are present but not yet equally mature. It can strengthen the time-choice branch, the emotion-and-risk branch, the social-utility branch, and the constructive-preference branch without forcing the wiki into a separate Loewenstein silo.
