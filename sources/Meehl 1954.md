# Meehl 1954: Clinical versus statistical prediction

## Summary

[[Meehl 1954]] is the pathbreaking source for the comparison between unaided expert judgment and formal predictive procedures. In the current vault, it should now function as the historical anchor of [[Clinical versus actuarial judgment]] and as the deep background source for later arguments about algorithmic prediction. The book's lasting contribution is not a naive celebration of tests or machines. It is a more precise claim: when the same pieces of information are available, explicit and consistent methods of combining them will often outperform informal, intuitive, case-by-case clinical synthesis.

That distinction matters. Meehl is careful to separate the kind of data from the mode of combination. His thesis is not that psychometric scores always beat interviews, or that mechanical prediction is universally superior because it uses different information. The central claim is instead about how information is integrated. Once that point is in place, much later work on improper linear models, actuarial prediction, machine learning, and algorithmic governance can be read as elaborations, confirmations, or complications of the original Meehl problem.

## Key claims

The first claim is that clinical and statistical prediction should be compared as alternative modes of combining evidence, not as alternative kinds of evidence. Meehl repeatedly warns against conflating objective tests with actuarial combination or interviews with clinical synthesis.

The second claim is that mechanical combination has a structural advantage because it is explicit and consistent. Human judges may notice subtle patterns, but they are also vulnerable to inconsistency, overweighting of vivid cues, and unstable intuitive weighting of evidence across cases.

The third claim is that the comparative issue is empirical rather than honorific. Meehl insists that the prestige of the clinician or the richness of the case narrative cannot settle the question. Only predictive performance can.

The fourth claim is methodological. The book helps turn judgment comparison into a serious research program by formulating the question clearly enough to be tested repeatedly across domains.

## Evidence and methods

[[Meehl 1954]] is both theoretical and review-based. It does not rely on one decisive experiment. Instead, it reconstructs the logic of the clinical-statistical comparison and surveys the evidence available at the time. That is why the book remains so important historically. It supplies the problem-formulation that later work keeps returning to. In the current vault, [[Dawes 1979]] sharpens the argument by showing how even improper linear models can do strikingly well, and [[Dawes et al. 1989]] functions as the later synthesis that overwhelmingly confirms the original comparative lesson across many predictive domains.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Meehl 1954]] matters because it is one of the clearest early demonstrations that realism about human judgment need not entail trust in intuitive expertise. It matters for psychology because it attacks the privileged status of informal clinical synthesis. It matters for economics and public policy because it creates the deep ancestor of later disputes about whether institutions should trust expert discretion or explicit procedures. And it matters for philosophy because it raises enduring questions about explanation, prediction, expertise, and what kind of rationality institutions should reward.

## Open questions

How far does the Meehl lesson generalize beyond structured prediction tasks? When should institutions treat formal prediction as advisory and when as authoritative? And how much do modern fairness, target-variable, and welfare-alignment problems complicate the original clinical-statistical comparison without overturning it?
