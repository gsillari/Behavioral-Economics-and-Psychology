# Ashraf et al. 2005: Adam Smith, behavioral economist

## Summary

[[Ashraf et al. 2005]] argues that [[Adam Smith]] should not be read only through the narrow self-interest image associated with simplified readings of *The Wealth of Nations*.
The paper reconstructs *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* as a psychologically rich account of economic agency, organized around the conflict between the passions and the impartial spectator.
In the current vault, its main value is historical and conceptual: it gives the behavioral-economics branch a pre-Paretian ancestor in which motivation, sympathy, self-command, fairness, social regard, adaptation, and market exchange are already intertwined.

The paper should not be used to claim that Smith already had modern behavioral economics.
Its better role is more precise.
It shows that several themes now central to [[Behavioral Economics]] were visible in Smith's moral psychology before economics became methodologically reconstructed around ordinal utility, revealed preference, axiomatic choice, and as-if modeling.
This makes the source an important companion to [[Bruni and Sugden 2007]], [[Hands 2010]], and the [[Paretian turn]].

## Key claims

The first claim is that Smith's model of agency is dual in a recognizable sense. The passions move behavior directly, while the impartial spectator supplies self-command, propriety, conscience, and a standpoint from which agents can evaluate their own impulses. This gives the paper a natural link to [[Fast and slow thinking]], but the link should be handled carefully. Smith is not a modern dual-process theorist; rather, the paper uses Smith to show that conflicts between impulse and reflective self-regulation long predate the contemporary type-1/type-2 vocabulary.

The second claim is that Smith anticipates several modern behavioral mechanisms. The paper discusses experiential [[Loss aversion]], underweighting of opportunity costs relative to out-of-pocket costs, intertemporal self-control, and overconfidence. These are not presented as formal models in Smith. They are historical anticipations that help explain why behavioral economics can be read as a recovery of older psychologically rich economic themes rather than as a wholly new importation from psychology.

The third claim is that Smith's social psychology matters for markets.
Sympathy is erratic, but fairness, justice, and the internalized standpoint of the impartial spectator help stabilize social interaction.
The paper links this to modern evidence on [[Altruism]], [[Fairness]], [[Trust]], reciprocity, and market exchange.
This is especially useful for the vault's social-preferences branch because it shows that the market is not conceptualized as self-interest alone even in Smith.

The fourth claim is that Smith offers a sophisticated account of consumption and welfare.
People pursue wealth and status partly because they misforecast how much happiness those goods will bring.
They adapt to circumstances more than they expect, and the pursuit of wealth may be socially productive while failing to generate the happiness agents imagine.
This makes the paper a historical bridge to [[Experienced utility]], affective forecasting, and welfare questions in behavioral economics.

## Unexploited ideas

The paper also identifies Smithian themes that had not been fully exploited in behavioral economics.
These include desire for posthumous reputation, negative reactions to being misjudged, naive realism about taste, and sympathy for the rich and great.
In the current vault, these should remain open leads rather than immediate concept pages.
They are relevant to reputation, procedural justice, cultural variation in tastes, status, and political economy, but the source by itself is not enough to justify separate mature concepts for each.

The sympathy-for-the-rich discussion is especially interesting for future behavioral political economy.
Smith treats admiration for the rich as morally corrupting but socially stabilizing.
Ashraf, Camerer, and Loewenstein suggest that this may help explain resistance to taxing the rich without relying only on self-interest or expectations of upward mobility.
That is a useful hypothesis for later work, but the current vault should mark it as suggestive rather than established.

## Why it matters here

This source fills a gap in the vault's historical arc.
The vault already has a strong story from the nineteenth-century utility tradition through ordinalism, revealed preference, decision theory, bounded rationality, and behavioral economics.
[[Ashraf et al. 2005]] extends that story backward by showing that a psychologically rich image of economic agency is already present in Smith.
It therefore strengthens the claim that behavioral economics is not only a late-twentieth-century correction to economics, but also a partial reopening of questions that earlier economics had not yet excluded.

The paper is also useful pedagogically.
It helps students avoid the false contrast between "Adam Smith equals self-interest" and "behavioral economics equals psychology."
Smith's own moral psychology makes the historical picture more interesting: market exchange, self-interest, sympathy, justice, self-command, and status-seeking are not separate worlds.

## Why it matters for PPE

For economics, the paper matters because it complicates the standard self-interest story at the historical source most often used to motivate it.
It shows that Smith's market theory sits beside a moral psychology of self-command, sympathy, justice, status, and adaptation.

For philosophy, the paper matters because it reconnects economic agency to moral psychology.
The impartial spectator is not just a behavioral mechanism; it is also a standpoint of evaluation, propriety, and self-scrutiny.
That makes the source useful for questions about whether preferences, welfare, and rationality can be understood without a richer account of persons.

For politics and policy, the paper matters because Smith's account of sympathy, justice, trust, and admiration for the rich points toward institutional questions.
Markets may rely on moral sentiments, welfare may diverge from consumption, and public attitudes toward inequality may be shaped by status imagination rather than by material interest alone.
