# Simon 1963: Discussion
## Summary

[[Simon 1963]] is a short but unusually explicit methodological statement in which Simon attacks Milton Friedman's `as-if` defense of rationality assumptions. In the current vault, the paper matters less as a standalone bounded-rationality theory piece than as a sharp formulation of why Simon thought Friedman's methodology was untenable.

The most important target is what Simon calls Friedman's `principle of unreality`. The idea under attack is that it does not matter if assumptions about actors are false, provided the theory's predictions at the market level are good enough. Simon argues that this position is not only logically weak, but also methodologically confused: economists do not directly observe the supposed profit-maximizing equilibrium that the theory attributes to firms, and therefore cannot save the theory merely by claiming that market outcomes come out right. If the theory works, Simon argues, it must be because it is grounded in empirically sound propositions about actors and valid composition laws.

## Key claims

The first claim is that the relation between propositions about actors and propositions about markets is not arbitrary. Economic explanation typically moves from the microscopic to the macroscopic, and this gives assumptions about actors an explanatory role that cannot be dismissed as mere dispensable scaffolding.

The second claim is that Friedman's defense of unrealistic assumptions fails because the allegedly observable market-level proposition is not in fact directly testable in the way Friedman needs. No one can simply observe whether firms occupy profit-maximizing positions.

The third claim is positive and constructive. Simon proposes replacing the `principle of unreality` with a `principle of continuity of approximation`: if the real world approximates an ideal type closely enough, then derivations from that idealization may be approximately correct. That is a much narrower and more empirically disciplined defense of idealization than Friedman's.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Simon 1963]] matters because it makes Simon's methodological disagreement with Friedman unusually plain. It matters for economics because it shows that bounded rationality was never only a descriptive tweak to standard theory. It was also a critique of how economics licenses idealized rationality assumptions in the first place. It matters for philosophy because it distinguishes legitimate approximation from methodological immunity. And it matters for politics and public policy because later institutional and legal applications of bounded rationality inherit Simon's insistence that real decision procedures matter.
