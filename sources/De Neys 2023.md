# De Neys 2023: Advancing theorizing about fast-and-slow thinking
## Summary

[[De Neys 2023]] argues that dual-process and fast-and-slow models remain useful, but only if they are theorized more carefully. The paper pushes back against simple pictures in which fast thinking is always heuristic and slow thinking is always normatively corrective. In the current vault, it matters because it revises the architecture often used to interpret bias findings without discarding the fast-and-slow framework altogether.

## Key claims

The paper claims that theorizing about [[Fast and slow thinking]] should move beyond a strict default-interventionist picture. Fast and slow processes should not be treated as fully exclusive systems linked by a simple switch, and conflict-sensitive intuitive processing needs to be taken seriously. A second claim is that the field should preserve the explanatory power of dual-process language while making room for overlap, interaction, and variability in how intuitive and reflective processes contribute to reasoning.

## Evidence and methods

This is a synthetic and programmatic article. De Neys reviews the recent debate over dual-process theories and uses evidence on conflict detection, norm sensitivity, and reasoning performance to argue for a more refined model. In the current vault, the paper functions as a higher-level theoretical update to the cluster opened by [[De Neys 2012]].

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[De Neys 2023]] matters because it shows that one of the most famous frameworks in behavioral science, fast versus slow thinking, is itself contested and evolving. It matters for economics because interpretations of bounded rationality depend on how automatic and reflective processes are modeled. It matters for philosophy because the paper presses on the relation between intuition and reason without collapsing one into the other. And it matters for politics because theories of citizen competence, deliberation, and paternalism often rest on implicit assumptions about how fast and slow reasoning interact.

## Open questions

Can fast-and-slow theorizing remain explanatory without becoming so flexible that it loses predictive power? How much revision can dual-process theory absorb before it becomes a different framework altogether? And what empirical findings would most clearly distinguish refined fast-and-slow theories from their main rivals?
