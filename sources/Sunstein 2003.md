# Sunstein 2003: Terrorism and probability neglect
## Summary

[[Sunstein 2003]] is a major bridge between behavioral risk judgment and public policy. The paper argues that in some emotionally charged contexts, especially terrorism, people focus so strongly on bad outcomes that they neglect probability differences that should matter to rational evaluation.

In the current vault, this source matters because it adds [[Probability neglect]] as a sharper mechanism for the policy-risk branch. It complements [[Availability heuristic]], [[Affect heuristic]], and [[Dread risk]] by showing how catastrophic fear can flatten sensitivity to likelihood.

## Key claims

The first claim is that fear and vivid catastrophic outcomes can lead people to neglect probabilities.

The second claim is that this creates a distinctive policy problem, because public demand for action may not track expected harm well.

The third claim is that terrorism is a paradigmatic case where cognitive bias, emotion, and institutional response become tightly linked.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source deepens the risk-perception branch by making the move from salience to policy more explicit. [[Gigerenzer 2004]] shows a downstream behavioral consequence of dread after September 11. [[Sunstein 2003]] clarifies one of the judgmental mechanisms that can help produce such distorted responses in the first place.

It also helps the vault distinguish probability neglect from simple availability. What matters here is not only that some events are more cognitively available, but that emotional focus on the bad outcome can suppress attention to likelihood itself.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it turns behavioral judgment into a question of democratic governance. It matters for economics because distorted probability weighting can redirect public and private resources inefficiently. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about fear, rational belief, and legitimate collective response. It matters for politics because terrorism and disaster policy are exactly the cases where vivid worst-case scenarios can dominate public reasoning.
