# Bar-Hillel 1980: The base-rate fallacy in probability judgments

## Summary

[[Bar-Hillel 1980]] is one of the central theoretical and experimental sources on [[Base-rate neglect]]. The paper defines the base-rate fallacy as the tendency to ignore base rates in favor of individuating or diagnostic information rather than integrating the two. Its distinctive contribution is an account based on perceived relevance.

Bar-Hillel argues that people order information by how relevant it seems to the target case. Information that appears more specific, individuating, identifying, or causally connected can dominate information that appears merely general or coincidental. Base rates are neglected when they are treated as low-relevance background facts, but they can be used when they are made equally relevant to the judged case.

## Key claims

The first claim is that the base-rate fallacy is not simply a failure to understand the diagnostic value of base rates. Subjects can use base rates when they are the only information available, and in some task structures they integrate base rates with other evidence.

The second claim is that perceived relevance is the key mediator. The cab problem makes this vivid: a witness report feels more directly about the target cab than the distribution of cab colors in the city, even though Bayes' rule requires both.

The third claim is that specificity is one route to relevance. Information about a smaller set, an individuated case, or an identifying indicator tends to feel more relevant than population-level information. Causality is another route to relevance, because causal base rates can be read as saying something about the individual case rather than only about a population.

The fourth claim is that base-rate neglect has broad applied significance. Bar-Hillel connects it to clinical prediction, legal evidence, dangerousness prediction, medical diagnosis, and interpersonal perception. This makes the paper especially relevant to the vault's human-versus-statistical-judgment branch.

## Evidence and debate

The paper uses a series of probabilistic inference problems, including variants of the famous cab problem. In the standard cab problem, many subjects give answers close to the witness's accuracy and far from the Bayesian posterior. The paper then varies whether the base-rate information and the individuating information appear equally relevant or unequally relevant.

The important result is not that people always ignore base rates. In problems where base rates and indicators are framed as equally relevant, the base-rate fallacy weakens or disappears. This supports the claim that the phenomenon depends on how information is interpreted, not only on whether subjects can calculate Bayes' rule.

This source should now be one of the main anchors for [[Base-rate neglect]]. It deepens the page beyond the simple representativeness story by giving a more general relevance-and-specificity account. It also connects the base-rate branch to [[Clinical versus actuarial judgment]] because the paper explicitly discusses why test interpretation and clinical prediction can go wrong when diagnostic signs are evaluated without antecedent probabilities.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, the paper is useful because it connects a formal Bayesian benchmark to high-stakes institutional judgment. Clinical testing, legal evidence, dangerousness prediction, and medical diagnosis all depend on whether people integrate prior probabilities with case-specific evidence. The paper therefore helps show why apparently psychological errors can become legal, political, and policy problems.

## Open questions

How much of base-rate neglect is due to representativeness, how much to relevance ordering, and how much to pragmatic assumptions about what information the experimenter meant to make useful? When does making a base rate causal improve rational inference, and when does it merely make it more persuasive?
