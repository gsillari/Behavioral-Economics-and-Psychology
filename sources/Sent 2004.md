# Sent 2004: How psychology made its limited way back into economics
## Summary

[[Sent 2004]] is one of the best historical reconstructions in the current vault of how [[Behavioral Economics]] rose as a recognized field inside economics rather than remaining an external psychological critique of it. The paper matters not mainly because it introduces one new concept, but because it reconstructs the profession-level story: who the major actors were, which journals, conferences, foundations, and prizes mattered, what debates structured the transition from older to newer behavioral economics, and why the newer version succeeded where the older one remained marginal.

The paper's title already signals its main thesis. Psychology made its way back into economics, but only in a limited form. The victorious version of behavioral economics did not simply revive the broader, more plural ambitions of earlier behavioral economics. Instead, it largely entered the mainstream by using standard rationality as a benchmark, translating deviations into formal economic models, and presenting itself as a way to strengthen rather than replace mainstream economics.

## Key claims

The first claim is historical. There was an older behavioral economics, centered around figures such as [[Herbert A. Simon]], the Carnegie school, Katona's Michigan group, Oxford contributors, and Stirling contributors, which was broader, more plural, and often more openly oppositional to the mainstream.

The second claim is that a transitional period in the 1980s institutionalized behavioral economics without settling what the field really was. Sent emphasizes the importance of the Society for the Advancement of Behavioral Economics, the *Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization*, the *Journal of Economic Psychology*, early conferences, and competing anthologies and handbooks. In the current vault, that makes the paper especially valuable for the sociology of the profession.

The third claim is that the newer behavioral economics of Kahneman, Tversky, Thaler, Camerer, Loewenstein, Laibson, Mullainathan, Rabin, and others succeeded partly because it used mainstream rationality as a benchmark from which to model deviations, rather than trying to build an entirely separate alternative in the old behavioral-economics style.

The fourth claim is that mainstream economics itself changed in ways that opened space for psychology. Sent points to difficulties in general equilibrium theory, game theory, and rational expectations as part of the background that made bounded-rationality and behavioral moves more acceptable.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Sent 2004]] matters because it shows that the history of behavioral economics is not only a sequence of ideas and experiments. It is also a professional and institutional story about prizes, journals, edited volumes, conferences, funding networks, methodological compromises, and successful boundary-crossing. It matters for economics because it shows how new research programs become acceptable inside a discipline. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about pluralism, scientific change, and how alternatives get absorbed or domesticated. And it matters for politics because many later policy branches depend on the fact that behavioral economics became mainstream enough to shape law, regulation, and public administration.

## Open questions

Did new behavioral economics succeed because it was substantively better, or because it was institutionally more compatible with the mainstream? How much of old behavioral economics was lost when the newer field standardized around deviations from a rational benchmark? And should the vault eventually make more room for the sociology of the profession as a distinct explanatory layer rather than leaving it scattered across source notes?
