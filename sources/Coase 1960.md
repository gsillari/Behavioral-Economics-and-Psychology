# Coase 1960: The problem of social cost
## Summary

[[Coase 1960]] is the classical law-and-economics paper that reframes externality problems as reciprocal conflicts over the use of scarce resources rather than as one-sided harms automatically requiring correction. In the current vault, the paper matters as a benchmark source behind later behavioral work: it clarifies how standard law and economics thinks about bargaining, rights, and institutional design before behavioral economics adds bounded rationality, self-control problems, and welfare complications.

## Key claims

The paper's central claim is that the key issue in social-cost problems is not merely how to stop one party from harming another, but how to compare alternative institutional arrangements when actions are interdependent. The famous simplifying result later called the [[Coase theorem]] says that if [[Transaction costs]] were absent, parties could bargain to efficient outcomes regardless of the initial allocation of rights. But Coase's deeper claim is that in the real world transaction costs are not absent, which is why legal rules, institutional structures, and comparative analysis matter so much.

## Evidence and methods

This is a conceptual and legal-economic argument rather than an experimental paper. Coase develops the analysis through legal cases and comparative institutional reasoning, asking how different assignments of rights affect bargaining, production, and harm. In the current vault, the paper functions as a benchmark institutional framework rather than as part of the behavioral anomaly literature.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Coase 1960]] matters because it is one of the clearest statements of the nonbehavioral benchmark that later behavioral law and policy work complicates. It matters for economics because it reorients the analysis of external harms around bargaining and [[Transaction costs]] rather than simple Pigovian correction. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about efficiency, rights, reciprocal harm, and what legal institutions should try to optimize. It matters for politics because many disputes about regulation, nuisance, environmental policy, and market design still turn on whether private bargaining can solve social conflicts or whether institutional intervention is necessary.

## Open questions

One open question is how often the paper is misread as a general anti-regulation argument rather than as a plea to take institutions and transaction costs seriously. Another is how behavioral frictions such as inattention, self-control problems, and preference instability should alter Coasean bargaining intuitions. A third is whether the vault should eventually add an explicit page on externalities to sit between Coasean benchmarks and later behavioral notions such as [[Internalities]].
