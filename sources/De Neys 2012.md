# De Neys 2012: Bias and conflict
## Summary

[[De Neys 2012]] argues that many classic reasoning biases should not be interpreted as evidence that intuitive thought is simply blind to logic. The paper uses conflict-detection findings to suggest that reasoners often sense a clash between a tempting heuristic response and a normatively stronger response, even when they ultimately choose the heuristic option. In the current vault, the paper matters because it complicates a crude reading of the heuristics-and-biases program.

## Key claims

The paper's central claim is that people can have [[Logical intuitions]]: they may detect that a response is problematic without being able to inhibit it or replace it successfully. This means that biased responding does not always imply total logical insensitivity. A second claim is that the standard dual-process contrast between an error-prone intuitive system and a correcting reflective system needs refinement. Intuition may sometimes register normatively relevant conflict before deliberate correction occurs.

## Evidence and methods

This is a theoretical review paper rather than a single experiment. De Neys synthesizes evidence from reasoning tasks such as base-rate problems and syllogistic reasoning, focusing on response times, confidence patterns, and conflict effects. In the current vault, the paper functions as an interpretive bridge between the classical [[Heuristics and Biases]] program and later rethinking of [[Fast and slow thinking]].

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[De Neys 2012]] matters because it shows that calling a response biased does not settle the deeper question of whether the agent recognized the better answer. It matters for economics because models of bounded rationality and belief error may need to distinguish failure to detect a norm from failure to override a tempting response. It matters for philosophy because it complicates the relation between intuition, norm sensitivity, and rational agency. And it matters for politics because institutions that correct citizen error may need to ask whether people are wholly ignorant, dimly aware, or actively conflicted.

## Links into the wiki

The main derived concept is [[Logical intuitions]], with [[Fast and slow thinking]] as the broader theoretical frame. The paper directly updates [[Heuristics and Biases]] by pushing against the idea that bias implies purely illogical intuition, and it strengthens [[Rationality wars]] because it complicates the standards used to classify intuitive reasoning as rational or irrational. At the synthesis level, it sharpens [[What Behavioral Economics Thinks Rationality Is]] by showing that norm sensitivity can exist even inside biased responding.

## Open questions

How far do conflict-detection effects generalize beyond laboratory reasoning tasks? Does conflict detection support a genuine logical intuition, or only a vague sense that something is off? And how should public policy interpret choices that look mistaken but may already be internally recognized as suspect by the chooser?
