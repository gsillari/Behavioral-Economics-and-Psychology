# Levy 1997: Prospect theory, rational choice, and international relations

## Summary

[[Levy 1997]] is a major review of how [[Prospect Theory]] has been used in international relations. Jack S. Levy argues that prospect-theory-based hypotheses about foreign policy and international conflict are theoretically suggestive, but that their external validity is much more difficult to establish than their internal validity in experimental settings.

In the current vault, the paper matters because it gives the prospect-theory branch a serious political uptake without romanticizing that move. It is both an application and a methodological caution.

## Key claims

The paper's first claim is that prospect theory offers genuinely interesting hypotheses for international behavior, especially around framing, [[Loss aversion]], the reflection effect, and risk acceptance in losses.

The second claim is that many of the most familiar prospect-theory hypotheses can be partly absorbed into rational-choice analysis unless framing and probability weighting are specified carefully. That keeps the theory in direct dialogue with [[Expected utility theory]] rather than placing it fully outside it.

The third claim is that the hard problem is external validity. Real foreign-policy decisions involve collective actors, strategic interaction, and high stakes, all of which complicate the laboratory mechanisms on which prospect theory was built.

## Evidence and scope

The source is a theoretical and methodological review. Its value in the current vault is that it gives PPE students a disciplined picture of what happens when a behavioral model moves into international politics: the gain is explanatory reach, but the cost is heavier demands on framing, reference-point identification, and empirical design.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it sits exactly at the boundary between economics-style decision theory and political explanation. It matters for economics because it tests whether a descriptive theory of risky choice can travel beyond markets. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about realism, benchmark comparison, and theory choice. It matters for politics because it connects prospect theory to foreign policy, strategic behavior, and the study of war and peace.
