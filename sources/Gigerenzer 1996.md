# Gigerenzer 1996: On narrow norms and vague heuristics

## Summary

[[Gigerenzer 1996]] is the matching reply in the foundational 1996 exchange at the center of the [[Rationality wars]]. In the current vault, the paper matters because it clarifies what Gigerenzer took his critique of the heuristics-and-biases program to be about. His claim is not simply that frequency formats make all cognitive illusions disappear. It is that the program often imposes unnecessarily narrow norms of sound reasoning and relies on explanatory notions that remain too vague to generate genuinely predictive process models.

That is why the paper matters so much. It shifts the debate from "are there errors or not?" to a more demanding methodological question: what counts as a proper norm of reasoning, and what counts as a satisfactory psychological explanation? Gigerenzer insists that the central issue is not whether some bias tasks can be repaired by reframing, but whether the field should move from global bias diagnosis toward detailed models that explain when and why performance improves or deteriorates.

## Key claims

The first claim is that the heuristics-and-biases program often uses narrow and contestable norms. Gigerenzer argues in particular that formal probability rules are sometimes imposed too indiscriminately on judgments about singular events and context-rich reasoning tasks.

The second claim is that the classic heuristics remain too vague as explanatory constructs. Terms such as representativeness, in his view, are often not specified tightly enough to yield strong process predictions.

The third claim is that frequency formats are not a magical solvent for all bias findings. Gigerenzer argues instead that researchers should build models predicting when frequency judgments will be good and when they will not.

The fourth claim is that the real target should be explanatory precision. The question is not merely whether people violate a benchmark, but what cognitive process model best explains both success and failure across tasks.

## Evidence and methods

This is a theoretical reply, but it is organized around empirical interpretation. Gigerenzer revisits conjunction tasks, single-event reasoning, and frequency formats in order to argue that apparent illusions depend heavily on representational setup and normative choice. The paper is methodologically important because it explicitly rejects an all-or-nothing reading of the debate. Its own standard is stronger: psychology should build detailed models that specify the conditions under which particular representations and reasoning strategies succeed.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Gigerenzer 1996]] matters because it is one of the cleanest statements of the ecological and methodological critique of bias-centered rationality. It matters for philosophy because it challenges the move from formal benchmark to general norm of sound reasoning. It matters for economics because later ecological-rationality arguments grow partly out of this same dissatisfaction with universal benchmark evaluation. And it matters for public policy because it weakens any too-quick inference from experimental benchmark failure to the conclusion that citizens need correction.

## Open questions

How much explanatory vagueness remains in the classic heuristics literature after later process refinements such as [[Attribute substitution]]? When are Gigerenzer's norm objections genuinely decisive, and when do they leave the empirical bias findings mostly intact? And can ecological-rationality research really deliver the more detailed process models that this paper calls for?
