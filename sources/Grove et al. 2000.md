# Grove et al. 2000: Clinical versus mechanical prediction

## Summary

[[Grove et al. 2000]] is the decisive meta-analytic consolidation of the clinical-statistical prediction literature. In the current vault, it should function as the strongest later confirmation source in the Meehl branch. Its headline result is that mechanical prediction methods are, on average, more accurate than clinical prediction methods, and that this advantage is remarkably robust across tasks, judges, and data types.

The paper matters because it turns what might otherwise look like a historically interesting controversy into a durable empirical pattern. After [[Meehl 1954]] posed the problem and [[Grove and Meehl 1996]] restated the comparative case, this meta-analysis makes the vault's historical story much harder to dismiss as anecdotal or domain-specific.

## Key claims

The first claim is that mechanical prediction is on average more accurate than clinical prediction by a meaningful margin.

The second claim is that this superiority is robust across judgment tasks, judge experience, and types of predictor information.

The third claim is that clinical prediction is occasionally as accurate and only rarely clearly superior, which means the burden of proof shifts strongly toward anyone defending unaided intuitive judgment as the default.

## Evidence and methods

This is a meta-analysis of studies comparing human clinical judgment with mechanical prediction procedures in medicine and psychology. That makes it especially valuable in the current vault. It is not merely another theoretical statement in favor of formal prediction. It is the most explicit quantitative consolidation of the literature already anticipated by [[Meehl 1954]] and defended programmatically by [[Grove and Meehl 1996]].

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Grove et al. 2000]] matters because it gives the judgment branch a clear empirical backbone. It matters for psychology because it is one of the strongest quantitative rebukes to the privileged status of informal expertise. It matters for economics and policy because later algorithmic comparisons inherit the same structure: when should institutions trust discretion, and when should they trust a reproducible formal rule?
