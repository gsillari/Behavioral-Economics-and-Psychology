# Frederick et al. 2009: Opportunity cost neglect

## Summary

[[Frederick et al. 2009]] is the current vault's main source on [[Opportunity cost neglect]]. Shane Frederick, Nathan Novemsky, Jing Wang, Ravi Dhar, and Stephen Nowlis argue that consumers often fail to consider what else their money could buy unless those displaced alternatives are made unusually salient. That matters because standard economic reasoning treats opportunity-cost consideration as basic to rational choice, while the paper shows that in ordinary consumer settings it is often not spontaneous at all.

The paper is important, but in a contained way. It does not need to become a large new branch of the vault. Its real value is to give one precise, teachable mechanism for why choices can look overly purchase-friendly when forgone alternatives remain implicit.

## Key claims

The paper's central claim is that opportunity costs are commonly neglected rather than merely underweighted. Consumers do not reliably generate the relevant outside options on their own, even when they have the cognitive ability to do so.

The second claim is that small framing cues can correct this neglect. Describing the nonpurchase option as preserving money for other purchases, or making residual cash explicit in a multioption choice, reduces purchase rates and increases preference for cheaper options.

The third claim is that greater effort alone does not reliably solve the problem. Even when participants are induced to think harder about the focal options, opportunity-cost reminders still shift choice. That makes the paper useful in the current vault as a bridge between [[Limited attention]] and more specific consumer-choice framing effects.

The fourth claim is that sensitivity to such cues differs across people. Those who are more dispositionally reluctant to spend appear less affected by the intervention, suggesting that some consumers spontaneously represent outside options more readily than others.

## Evidence and methods

The paper uses a sequence of consumer-choice studies rather than one flagship experiment. Participants choose whether to buy a focal product, or choose between a cheaper and a more expensive option, while the researchers manipulate whether the forgone use of money is left implicit or made explicit. The paper also includes a consequential choice study with real products and a study using a priming task rather than a direct reminder, which helps reduce the worry that the effect is only experimental demand.

The main empirical pattern is stable across the studies: when outside uses of money are cued, purchase rates fall and the cheaper option becomes more attractive. The authors also show that the effect is not well explained by a simple segregation account from prospect theory alone, because a reminder that evokes alternative uses of money works differently from a reminder that merely isolates the price premium without making those alternatives salient.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it sharpens a basic contrast between economic theory and behavioral evidence. It matters for economics because opportunity cost is one of the core pieces of economic reasoning. It matters for philosophy because it raises the question of whether a chooser is irrational when normatively relevant alternatives are simply not represented. It matters for politics and public policy because many interventions work by making hidden tradeoffs more visible rather than by changing incentives themselves.

Pedagogically, this is a good leaf-level paper: concrete, intuitive, and easy to connect to larger ideas about salience, framing, and consumer welfare without requiring a whole new subfield.

## Open questions

How often does opportunity-cost neglect survive repetition and market experience? Should the effect be modeled mainly as inattention, as framing, or as a failure of internal accounting? And when policy or marketing makes forgone alternatives more salient, when is that a welfare-improving correction and when is it just another persuasive frame?
