# Slovic 1995: The construction of preference
## Summary

[[Slovic 1995]] is the high-level American Psychologist statement of the construction-of-preference view. It argues that people's preferences are often constructed in the act of evaluation rather than read out from a stable internal ordering. In the current vault, this source matters because it gives the branch a concise, canonical statement that connects experimental anomalies to deeper questions about rational choice and welfare.

The paper is complementary to [[Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006]]. The chapter gives the broad programmatic overview. [[Slovic 1995]] gives the sharper standalone argument for why the branch matters so much: if different elicitation procedures generate different orderings, then some of the central assumptions behind rational-choice theory and revealed preference become much harder to defend.

## Key claims

The first claim is that preferences are frequently constructed rather than merely revealed. The elicitation procedure can help determine the preference that gets expressed.

The second claim is that [[Preference reversals]] and related procedure effects undermine [[Procedure invariance]], a principle that standard rational-choice models typically need.

The third claim is that this is not a minor measurement problem. It creates a major challenge for how economics and policy infer welfare from expressed choices and valuations.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source should help move the constructed-preferences branch upward in the vault hierarchy. Until now, the branch leaned heavily on the Ariely papers, which are important but narrower. [[Slovic 1995]] gives the vault a more foundational, field-defining articulation of the idea that preference is often contingent, context-sensitive, and elicitation-dependent.

That means it belongs not only in [[Constructed preferences]], but also in the higher-level syntheses where the vault asks what behavioral economics thinks rationality is and how policy should interpret unstable choice.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows how a technical-seeming experimental literature opens into a larger philosophical challenge. It matters for economics because it destabilizes simple revealed-preference reasoning. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about what preferences are, whether they can ground welfare claims, and how context affects agency. It matters for politics because public institutions often need to rely on elicited preferences in law, regulation, and cost-benefit analysis.
