# Dawes et al. 1989: Clinical versus actuarial judgment
## Summary

[[Dawes et al. 1989]] synthesizes the evidence comparing clinical judgment with actuarial judgment in prediction and diagnosis. The paper argues that formal actuarial methods, including simple rules, scoring systems, and model-based combinations of cues, generally outperform unaided expert intuition. In the current vault, it deepens the Dawes branch by moving from the power of [[Clinical versus actuarial judgment]] in particular to the broader institutional comparison between discretionary expertise and explicit predictive procedures.

## Key claims

The paper's central claim is that actuarial judgment is typically more accurate than clinical judgment across a wide range of domains. It also argues that this superiority does not depend on using especially complex or opaque models: consistency in how evidence is combined matters more than narrative flexibility or case-by-case intuition. A further claim is that resistance to actuarial methods is partly social and professional. Experts often prefer discretion, even when formal methods perform better, because discretion feels more responsive to individuality and preserves professional authority.

## Evidence and methods

This is a synthetic review article rather than a new experiment. Dawes, Faust, and Meehl survey the comparative literature on predictive performance in settings such as diagnosis, prognosis, and other judgment tasks involving multiple cues. Their method is comparative and evaluative: they ask which procedure performs better when clinical and actuarial methods are tested against outcomes. In the current vault, the paper functions as a stronger empirical consolidation of themes that appear more structurally in [[Dawes and Corrigan 1974]] and more programmatically in [[Dawes 1979]].

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it turns a methodological point into an institutional one. It matters for economics because it bears on prediction, expertise, and the design of decision procedures; for philosophy because it presses on what should count as justified judgment when intuition conflicts with transparent rules; and for politics because many institutions, from courts to hospitals to schools, must decide how much authority to give experts relative to explicit procedures. It is also an important bridge to contemporary debates over algorithms, discretion, and accountability.

## Open questions

When should institutions allow expert override of actuarial tools, and under what standards? How should accuracy trade off against transparency, fairness, and legitimacy when formal procedures outperform intuition? Does the paper support a broad defense of rule-based decision-making, or only in well-structured predictive environments?
