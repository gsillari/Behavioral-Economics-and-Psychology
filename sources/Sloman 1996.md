# Sloman 1996: The empirical case for two systems of reasoning
## Summary

[[Sloman 1996]] is a major theoretical defense of dual-process thinking before the later type-1/type-2 vocabulary becomes standard. Steven Sloman argues that human reasoning is best understood as involving two distinct forms of computation: an associative system that operates through similarity and temporal contiguity, and a rule-based system that operates on symbolic structures with logical form and variables. In the current vault, the paper matters because it gives the rationality-debate branch a serious early articulation of why two-system theorizing had such appeal.

## Key claims

The paper's central claim is that the empirical case for two systems of reasoning is strong. One system is associative and draws inferences through statistical, similarity-based, and temporally structured relations. The other is rule based and exhibits the productivity and systematicity usually associated with symbolic reasoning. Sloman argues that these systems can generate different responses to the same problem and that the rule-based system can sometimes suppress, though not fully eliminate, associative outputs.

This matters in the current vault because the debate over rationality is partly a debate over cognitive architecture. If judgment errors arise from the interaction of two distinct reasoning systems, then heuristics-and-biases findings, ecological critiques, and later dual-process refinements all need to be interpreted differently than if there were only one generic reasoning engine.

## Evidence and methods

This is a theoretical review paper rather than a new experimental study. Its contribution is to synthesize evidence from reasoning, categorization, and cognitive theory into a unified case for two computationally distinct systems. In the current vault, the paper functions as a bridge between the classic heuristics literature and the later, more disciplined dual-process formulations associated with Evans and Stanovich.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Sloman 1996]] matters because it shows that the rationality debate is not only about norms and anomalies. It is also about what sort of mind humans have. It matters for economics because many behavioral findings depend on assumptions about automatic versus reflective reasoning. It matters for philosophy because the paper raises deep issues about rules, association, symbolism, and the structure of inference. It matters for politics because public reasoning and institutional design often rely on simplified pictures of what deliberation can correct and what intuition will continue to supply.

## Open questions

How much of later dual-process theory is already present in Sloman's associative-versus-rule-based distinction? Does the paper overstate the separateness of the two systems, or does it remain one of the cleanest formulations of the architecture? And how should the vault position Sloman between the heuristics-and-biases tradition and later critics of strong two-system claims?
