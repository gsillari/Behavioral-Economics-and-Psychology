# Gigerenzer 2004: Dread risk, September 11, and fatal traffic accidents
## Summary

[[Gigerenzer 2004]] argues that fear of a rare catastrophic risk can trigger behavior that increases exposure to more familiar but statistically larger dangers. Using the aftermath of September 11, the paper suggests that fear of flying pushed some Americans toward driving, producing excess road fatalities. In the current vault, the source matters because it extends the heuristics-and-risk branch from laboratory judgment into a vivid public-policy case.

The paper is useful because it links risk perception directly to behavior. It is not only about how people misjudge probabilities. It is about how a salient and dreaded event can reorganize action in ways that create downstream harm. That makes it an important bridge between [[Availability heuristic]], affective risk judgment, and [[Behavioral public policy]].

## Key claims

The first claim is that dread risks exert disproportionate psychological force relative to their statistical frequency. A low-probability, high-consequence event can dominate attention and choice even when it is not the largest objective threat.

The second claim is that this kind of fear can generate substitution effects across modes of action. After September 11, avoidance of air travel may have increased road travel enough to raise traffic fatalities.

The third claim is that psychological research on risk perception can therefore have life-saving policy relevance. In the current vault, that matters because the source turns abstract talk about salience and heuristics into an argument about real institutional communication after crises.

## Evidence and methods

This is a short empirical paper built around observational evidence from transportation data after September 11. Gigerenzer uses the behavioral response to a dread event as the main object of analysis. The source therefore sits somewhere between psychological theory and applied public-risk analysis.

Methodologically, the paper matters because it shows how behavioral ideas travel into naturalistic contexts without becoming vague metaphors. The mechanism is still psychological, but the stakes are clearly institutional and real-world.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Gigerenzer 2004]] matters because it shows how risk perception affects policy-relevant behavior outside the lab. It matters for economics because perceived risks can shift behavior in ways that generate unintended aggregate costs. It matters for philosophy because it raises the question of whether dread-sensitive judgment is irrational, understandable, or even ecologically adaptive in some deeper sense. It matters for politics because crisis communication, terrorism, and public safety all depend on how citizens perceive and respond to salient hazards.

This paper is especially useful in PPE because it shows how a single event can create a wedge between objective risk and perceived risk, with consequences large enough to matter for public policy.
