# List 2003: Does market experience eliminate market anomalies? *The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 118*(1), 41-71
## Summary

[[List 2003]] is a foundational field-evidence paper on the endowment effect in the current vault. The paper studies experienced market participants and argues that market experience can substantially reduce or eliminate the endowment effect that appears strongly among inexperienced traders.

In this wiki, the source matters because it moves the endowment-effect branch beyond classroom exchange experiments and into actual marketplaces. It provides one of the clearest pieces of evidence that the strength of the effect depends on experience and institutional setting, not only on a universal psychological law.

## Key claims

The first claim is that the endowment effect is strong among inexperienced market participants but attenuates with market experience.

The second claim is that the attenuation is not just selection; experience itself appears to play a causal role.

The third claim is that some market anomalies may shrink when agents repeatedly face environments that teach them to trade away from entitlements.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source gives the endowment-effect branch a serious field-evidence anchor. It belongs directly in [[Endowment effect]] and [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]], but it also strengthens [[Field Evidence in Behavioral Economics]] because it shows how a famous laboratory anomaly changes in real markets.

It is also important because it creates tension inside the vault rather than merely adding support. The classical Kahneman-Knetsch-Thaler line treats the endowment effect as strong evidence for loss aversion and reference dependence. [[List 2003]] complicates that by showing that market learning matters.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it sharpens the relation between psychology and institutions. It matters for economics because it shows that market experience can discipline valuation behavior. It matters for philosophy because it raises the question of whether a preference anomaly that disappears with experience should be treated differently from one that does not. It matters for politics because policy often deals with citizens who are much closer to inexperienced traders than to professional market participants.

## Open questions

How far does market experience really generalize beyond the specific goods and institutions studied here? Does experience eliminate the endowment effect, or mainly reduce it in repeated exchange environments?
