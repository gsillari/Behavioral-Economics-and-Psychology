# Epley and Gilovich 2016: The mechanics of motivated reasoning
## Summary

[[Epley and Gilovich 2016]] is a concise synthetic account of the process logic behind [[Motivated reasoning]]. In the current vault, it matters because it takes a concept that can otherwise sound vague or moralized and explains its mechanics more clearly: people are often not motivated to abandon standards of reason, but to search selectively among available considerations in ways that support a desired conclusion.

The paper is especially useful because it comes from authors already present in the anchoring branch. That gives the vault a cleaner picture of Epley and Gilovich as process theorists of judgment more broadly, not only as anchoring specialists.

## Key claims

The first claim is that motivated reasoning works through ordinary cognitive mechanisms. People attend to, recruit, and interpret information selectively in ways that preserve a sense of reasonableness.

The second claim is that people are often blind to the motivational steering of their own reasoning. The process feels objective from the inside even when it is directionally tilted.

The third claim is that motivated reasoning should not be confused with crude wishful thinking. It is constrained by the need to find arguments, memories, and interpretations that can support the preferred conclusion without feeling obviously irrational.

## Relation to Kunda and the broader branch

This source should be read as complementary to [[Kunda 1990]] rather than as a replacement for it. [[Kunda 1990]] gives the canonical theoretical statement. [[Epley and Gilovich 2016]] gives a shorter and more accessible explanation of the mechanisms that make the idea plausible and empirically tractable.

In the current vault, that makes the pair especially valuable for PPE teaching. Kunda provides the foundational article. Epley and Gilovich provide a cleaner way of explaining how directional goals can bias cognition while preserving a felt sense of objectivity.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it turns motivated reasoning into a workable analytical concept rather than a slogan about bad faith. It matters for economics because policy beliefs, market expectations, and self-assessments may be filtered by directional goals without ceasing to look evidence-based. It matters for philosophy because it sharpens the difference between deliberate deception and sincerely biased reasoning. It matters for politics because democratic disagreement often involves exactly this phenomenon: people offer real arguments, but their search and weighting of evidence are not neutral.
