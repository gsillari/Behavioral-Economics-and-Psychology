# Tversky and Kahneman 1983: Extensional versus intuitive reasoning
## Summary

[[Tversky and Kahneman 1983]] is the main journal-article anchor for the [[Conjunction fallacy]] in the current vault. It contrasts extensional probability reasoning with intuitive reasoning and shows that descriptively coherent stories can appear more plausible than formally more probable simpler events.

In this wiki, the paper matters because it gives the representativeness branch one of its clearest and most philosophically important norm violations. It sharpens the difference between intuitive plausibility and formal probability judgment.

## Key claims

The first claim is that people often violate the conjunction rule in systematic ways.

The second claim is that these violations are driven by intuitive judgments of representativeness or coherence.

The third claim is that extensional reasoning and intuitive reasoning can diverge sharply even in simple probability tasks.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source should now anchor [[Conjunction fallacy]] more directly and strengthen the representativeness branch as a formal challenge to rational belief. It belongs beside [[Kahneman and Tversky 1972]], [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]], and [[Kahneman and Frederick 2002]] as part of the fuller sequence through which similarity-based judgment becomes a challenge to statistical reasoning.

It also helps the vault keep the normative target explicit: the issue is not merely unusual wording or odd intuition, but a clear conflict with the probability ordering required by conjunction logic.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows how narrative coherence can defeat formal reasoning even when the formal rule is simple. It matters for economics because forecasts and scenarios are often judged by plausibility rather than by strict probability structure. It matters for philosophy because it raises classic questions about rational belief, norm violation, and intuitive judgment. It matters for politics because coherent public stories can feel more convincing than less dramatic but more probable alternatives.
