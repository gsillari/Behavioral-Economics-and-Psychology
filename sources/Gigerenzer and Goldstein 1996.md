# Gigerenzer and Goldstein 1996: Reasoning the fast and frugal way
## Summary

[[Gigerenzer and Goldstein 1996]] is one of the core positive statements of the fast-and-frugal-heuristics program. The paper argues that bounded rationality should not be understood only as a story of cognitive limitation. Simple heuristics can achieve strong performance precisely because they ignore part of the available information and exploit environmental structure efficiently. In the current vault, the source matters because it deepens the ecological-rationality branch beyond abstract benchmark critique and gives it a concrete model of inference.

The paper is especially important because it shows what an ecological alternative to optimization looks like in algorithmic terms. Rather than integrating all cues and computing weighted combinations, agents can use one-reason decision strategies that stop search early and still perform well. This makes the source central to [[Ecological rationality]], [[Adaptive toolbox]], and the specific heuristic now tracked in the vault as [[Take-the-Best heuristic]].

## Key claims

The first claim is that good inference under uncertainty does not always require integrating all available information. In some environments, a heuristic that searches cues in order and stops at the first discriminating cue can outperform more information-hungry methods.

The second claim is that bounded rationality should be modeled positively rather than merely normatively. The question is not only where people depart from formal ideals, but also which simple rules succeed under realistic constraints of time, knowledge, and computation.

The third claim is that heuristic performance is environment-dependent. In the current vault, that matters because it gives [[Ecological rationality]] a sharper meaning: heuristics are not globally rational or irrational, but better or worse matched to cue structures and tasks.

## Evidence and methods

This is a theoretical and modeling paper with simulation-based comparisons rather than a standard lab experiment. Gigerenzer and Goldstein specify fast-and-frugal algorithms and compare their predictive performance with more information-intensive strategies across environments. The paper therefore contributes a model of bounded inference, not only a philosophical defense of heuristics.

That makes it especially valuable in the vault. [[Gigerenzer 2008]] states the ecological-rationality view compactly, but this paper shows its inner mechanism. It therefore serves as a more technical anchor for the ecological branch.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Gigerenzer and Goldstein 1996]] matters because it gives a serious alternative to the assumption that rationality always requires fuller information and more computation. It matters for economics because it suggests that simple decision rules may sometimes outperform optimization-oriented ideals in practice. It matters for philosophy because it reframes the normative debate: rationality may involve fit to environment rather than only coherence or maximizing calculation. It matters for politics because institutions often have to design procedures for real agents under time pressure, uncertainty, and incomplete data.

The paper is especially useful in PPE because it turns ecological rationality from a slogan into a concrete research program. Students can see exactly what it means to claim that a heuristic can be smart.
