# DellaVigna 2009: Psychology and economics
## Summary

[[DellaVigna 2009]] surveys the field evidence for behavioral economics across three broad classes of departures from the standard model: preference-side mechanisms, [[Nonstandard beliefs]], and [[Nonstandard decision-making]].
Rather than focusing on laboratory anomalies alone, the paper assembles evidence from domains such as consumption, finance, crime, voting, charitable giving, labor supply, and market interaction.
In the current vault, this source functions as the main empirical organizer of the field.

Its importance is not just that it says behavioral findings "travel."
It offers a way of classifying how they travel, where they travel most strongly, and why that matters for economic theory and policy.
That is why the source now sits at the center of [[Field Evidence in Behavioral Economics]] and why so many mechanism pages depend on it for their applied backbone.

## Key claims

The paper's first claim is that behavioral economics has accumulated credible field evidence for systematic deviations from the standard model in at least three areas.
First, individuals often display preference-side departures from the standard model, especially [[Time inconsistency]], [[Reference dependence]], and [[Social preferences]].
Second, they exhibit [[Nonstandard beliefs]], including [[Overconfidence]], [[Projection bias]], and the [[Law of small numbers]].
Third, they display [[Nonstandard decision-making]], including [[Framing effects]], [[Limited attention]], [[Menu effects]], interpersonal influence, and [[Emotions in decision-making]].

The second claim is that these deviations are not all equally important.
Some mechanisms are repeatedly visible across domains and institutions, while others appear more selectively.
That matters for the vault because this paper should not be used only as a list of labels.
It should be used to sort stronger field-backed mechanisms from thinner or more context-dependent ones.

The third claim is that once predictable behavioral patterns are present, strategic actors respond to them.
Firms, employers, investors, politicians, and organizations do not merely observe behavioral deviations.
They price around them, design contracts around them, exploit them, or sometimes help correct them.
In the current vault, that equilibrium-level idea is tracked under [[Strategic response to behavioral anomalies]] and is one of the paper's most important contributions.

## Taxonomy and field architecture

The paper's architecture is one of the main reasons it deserves expansion in the wiki.
It does not simply alternate between examples.
It groups evidence into a reusable map of the field.
That map remains one of the best available guides for teaching PPE students how behavioral economics scales from famous experiments to real institutions.

The preference branch includes mechanisms where the content or shape of what people want departs from the standard model.
In the vault, that includes [[Time inconsistency]], [[Reference dependence]], and [[Social preferences]].
This branch is especially important because it immediately connects behavioral evidence to welfare questions and to later policy ideas such as [[Internalities]].

The belief branch includes cases where the problem is not what people want, but what they think.
That includes [[Overconfidence]], the [[Law of small numbers]], and [[Projection bias]].
In the current vault, this branch is especially important because it connects the heuristics tradition to real institutional settings rather than leaving it at the level of classroom demonstrations.

The decision-making branch includes cases where preferences and beliefs may be held fixed, but the process of choice still departs from the standard model.
That includes [[Framing effects]], [[Limited attention]], [[Menu effects]], interpersonal influence, and [[Emotions in decision-making]].
This is the branch that most directly bridges into actual intervention design, because it tells policy makers which part of the decision environment is doing the work.

## Evidence and methods

This is a synthetic survey article rather than a single original study.
Its contribution is to collect and organize field evidence across many application areas, showing where behavioral deviations appear outside the lab and how they interact with markets, firms, and political institutions.
The method is comparative and synthetic rather than experimental in the narrow sense.

That method matters for the vault because the paper is not supposed to compete with the classic founding experiments.
Its role is different.
It tests the reach and significance of the broader behavioral program in naturally occurring settings.
In effect, it asks whether the branch built from sources like [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] and [[Kahneman and Tversky 1979]] is merely laboratory psychology in economic dress, or whether it genuinely changes how economists should think about markets and institutions.

The paper is also methodologically useful because it encourages discrimination.
It does not imply that every famous behavioral effect is equally well established in the field.
Instead, it invites the reader to ask which patterns appear repeatedly, under what institutional conditions they survive, and how strategic actors react once those patterns become predictable.

## Stronger and weaker parts of the field

One reason this source deserves more weight in the wiki is that it helps rank behavioral mechanisms by empirical seriousness.
In the current vault, the strongest field-backed clusters appear to be [[Time inconsistency]], [[Reference dependence]], [[Social preferences]], [[Overconfidence]], [[Limited attention]], and some forms of interpersonal influence.
These mechanisms recur in multiple domains and are relatively easy to connect to institutional stakes.

Other mechanisms are still important but often appear more dependent on context, design, or interpretation.
In the current vault, that includes some uses of [[Framing effects]], the [[Law of small numbers]], and some cases in [[Emotions in decision-making]].
The point is not that these are weak ideas.
It is that DellaVigna gives the vault a basis for being more selective and comparative about them.

This selectivity is especially important for PPE teaching.
If every behavioral result is treated as equally central, the field collapses into an undifferentiated list of deviations.
DellaVigna helps resist that collapse by showing that behavioral economics can be organized by comparative empirical weight, not just by theoretical fame.

## Strategic response and equilibrium implications

The most underused part of this source in the current vault is its equilibrium logic.
The paper emphasizes that once behavioral patterns are stable enough to predict, other actors adapt.
Firms design contracts around inattention or self-control problems.
Employers structure incentives around social preferences and motivational frictions.
Political actors respond to salience and voter attention.
Investors and intermediaries may exploit slower-moving or biased agents.

This is crucial because it changes the significance of behavioral findings.
The question is no longer only whether an individual deviates from a benchmark.
The question becomes whether those deviations reshape market outcomes, institutional design, and the distribution of power.
That is exactly why [[Strategic response to behavioral anomalies]] should be a much more central page than a simple afterthought.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows behavioral economics operating at the scale of a real empirical research program rather than as a handful of famous experiments.
It matters for economics because it demonstrates that preference-side mechanisms, [[Nonstandard beliefs]], and [[Nonstandard decision-making]] show up in markets and institutions.
It matters for philosophy because it sharpens the question of how much empirical deviation is needed before a rationality benchmark should be revised.
It matters for politics because it highlights how policy makers, firms, employers, and other strategic actors respond to predictable patterns in human behavior.

The paper is also especially useful for course design.
It gives students a way to move from headline behavioral ideas to a structured view of the field.
Instead of learning biases one by one, they can learn how behavioral economics organizes empirical departures, how those departures enter institutions, and where policy arguments begin to look more or less justified.

## Open questions

One open question is which behavioral patterns survive most robustly once market experience, learning, and competition intensify.
Another is whether field evidence primarily confirms the lab-based behavioral framework or instead forces more domain-specific and institution-sensitive interpretations.

A third question is how policy should respond when field evidence reveals stable behavioral patterns but welfare implications remain ambiguous.
That question matters especially when strategic actors can exploit the same patterns that policy makers might try to correct.
The paper therefore remains central to future expansions of both the empirical branch and the policy branch.
