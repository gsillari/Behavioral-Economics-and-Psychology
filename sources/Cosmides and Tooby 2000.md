# Cosmides and Tooby 2000

## Summary

[[Cosmides and Tooby 2000]] is a compact statement of the evolutionary-psychology interpretation of social reasoning. The chapter argues that cognitive science needs the adaptationist program: theories of evolved function should guide hypotheses about the computational machinery of the mind. Its central case is social exchange. Humans, they argue, possess domain-specific machinery for reasoning about cooperation for mutual benefit, including a subroutine specialized for cheater detection.

In the current vault, the chapter matters because it gives [[Evolutionary psychology]] a much stronger source than a generic placeholder. It also gives the [[Wason selection task]] its most important reinterpretation: poor performance on abstract conditional rules need not show a general incapacity for conditional reasoning if performance rises sharply when the rule expresses a social contract.

## Main claims

The first claim is methodological. Evolutionary analysis can generate testable hypotheses about cognitive architecture. Instead of treating adaptive function as an after-the-fact story, Cosmides and Tooby use it to predict what kinds of representations and inference procedures should exist.

The second claim is architectural. The mind should not be modeled only as a small set of content-free, general-purpose reasoning mechanisms. Many cognitive systems may be domain-specific expert systems whose procedures embody information about recurrent adaptive problems.

The third claim is empirical. The Wason selection task reveals content effects that are difficult to explain as mere familiarity, clearer thinking, or activation of ordinary propositional logic. Subjects who perform poorly on descriptive rules often perform much better on social-contract versions, especially when the correct choice identifies someone taking a benefit without satisfying a requirement.

The fourth claim is functional. Social-contract reasoning is not just better logical reasoning in a familiar costume. Cosmides and Tooby emphasize cases in which the cheater-detection response diverges from the formally logical response. In those cases, subjects often choose the adaptively relevant cards rather than the cards that would be correct under a content-free logical analysis.

The fifth claim is contrastive. Their account competes with permission-schema and general deontic explanations. The chapter argues that social-contract and precaution reasoning can dissociate, suggesting more than one content-sensitive mechanism rather than one broad permission schema.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Cosmides and Tooby 2000]] matters because it changes what the selection task is evidence for. In a simple heuristics-and-biases reading, the abstract task looks like a demonstration of confirmation bias or failure of formal reasoning. In the evolutionary reading, the same contrast between abstract and social-contract tasks suggests that humans may be poorly adapted to arbitrary logical descriptions but well adapted to recurrent social problems.

That does not make the rationality question disappear. It relocates it. The question becomes whether rationality should be assessed by formal logic alone, by ecological or adaptive fit, or by some hybrid standard that distinguishes laboratory task structure from evolved problem structure. This is why the chapter belongs in [[Rationality wars]] and not only in [[Evolutionary psychology]].

The chapter also matters for [[Fast and slow thinking]]. It resists a crude dual-process moral in which abstract failures are just fast intuitive errors waiting for slow correction. Social-contract performance is often fast and confident, yet on Cosmides and Tooby's interpretation it is not merely irrational. It may reflect specialized intuitive machinery. That makes the source useful for teaching the difference between dual-process architecture and benchmark-based evaluation.
