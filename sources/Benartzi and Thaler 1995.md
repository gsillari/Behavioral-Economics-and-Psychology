# Benartzi and Thaler 1995: Myopic loss aversion and the equity premium puzzle
## Summary

[[Benartzi and Thaler 1995]] argues that the [[Equity premium puzzle]] can be explained behaviorally through [[Myopic loss aversion]]. The paper combines the prospect-theory idea of [[Loss aversion]] with a short evaluation horizon: if investors look at their portfolios frequently, then temporary losses are experienced often and loom larger than long-run gains, making stocks look less attractive than standard models would suggest.

## Key claims

The core claim is that two ingredients together matter more than either alone. [[Loss aversion]] by itself does not explain the premium unless investors repeatedly evaluate outcomes over relatively short horizons. Short evaluation periods by themselves do not explain the premium unless losses carry disproportionate psychological weight. Their combination, which the paper labels [[Myopic loss aversion]], can make an historically large equity premium consistent with ordinary investor behavior. The paper therefore matters as a bridge from general behavioral decision theory to a major anomaly in finance.

## Evidence and methods

The paper is not a lab experiment or a field trial. Its method is explanatory and calibrational: it takes a well-known asset-pricing anomaly, the unusually high historical return on equities relative to safe assets, and asks whether a prospect-theoretic model with short evaluation horizons can rationalize it. In the current vault, the paper therefore functions as an application paper showing how behavioral microfoundations can be exported into finance.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows behavioral economics moving from choice experiments to macro-relevant market questions. It matters for economics because it offers a behavioral explanation for one of finance's most famous puzzles. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about which benchmark of rationality should govern asset choice and whether frequent self-evaluation can systematically distort prudent long-run decisions. It matters for politics because retirement saving, pension design, default fund choice, and public communication about investment risk all depend on how citizens experience short-run losses.

## Open questions

One open question is whether myopic loss aversion explains the whole premium or only part of it. Another is whether the relevant evaluation horizon should be treated as a psychological primitive or as an institutional variable shaped by reporting frequency, pension design, and market culture. A third is how this explanation compares with later behavioral-finance accounts that rely on disappointment aversion, ambiguity, or learning.
