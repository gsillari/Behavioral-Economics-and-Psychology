# Rabin 1998: Psychology and economics

## Summary

[[Rabin 1998]] is one of the vault's most important field-level statements of the psychology-and-economics program. Its central contribution is architectural: it shows that psychological evidence can enter economics at several different depths. Some findings enrich the content of utility while preserving rational-choice form. Some findings show systematic errors in belief formation. Still others suggest that it can be misleading to model people as maximizing stable, coherent, and accurately perceived preferences at all.

That three-part structure makes the paper a bridge between the older [[Heuristics and Biases]] tradition, the preference-side anomalies of behavioral economics, and the later methodological question of how much economics can change while still looking like formal economics.

## Main claims

The first claim is methodological. Economics need not abandon formal modeling in order to use psychology. Rabin argues instead for empirically better assumptions: economists can keep the discipline of models while replacing inherited assumptions about motivation, belief, and welfare with psychologically informed ones.

The second claim concerns richer preferences. The paper treats departures from narrow self-interest, especially [[Fairness]], altruism, reciprocity, and procedural concerns, as economically relevant preference content rather than as noise. It also places reference dependence, loss sensitivity, and self-control problems inside a broader program of making utility functions more realistic.

The third claim concerns belief formation. Rabin gives economists a compact map of the judgment literature: base-rate neglect, the law of small numbers, regression neglect, confirmation bias, belief perseverance, overconfidence, and self-serving inference all matter because they can generate systematic mistakes even when people are trying to pursue their goals.

The fourth claim is more radical. Some psychological findings do not merely say that people have unusual preferences or mistaken beliefs. They question the very picture of agents as maximizing a stable and well-defined preference ordering. This is the branch that matters for [[Constructed preferences]], [[Reason-based choice]], melioration, and [[Experienced utility]].

The fifth claim is welfare-theoretic. If decision utility diverges from experienced utility, then observed choice may fail to identify welfare. That makes the paper an early and important source for [[Behavioral welfare economics]], especially where people mispredict how experiences will feel or where remembered and moment-by-moment utility diverge.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Rabin 1998]] matters because it makes behavioral economics readable as a structured challenge to the standard model rather than as a pile of anomalies. It matters for economics because it shows how psychological realism can be incorporated into formal analysis. It matters for philosophy because it reopens questions about preference, welfare, rationality, and the relation between reasons and choice. It matters for politics and policy because fairness, self-serving beliefs, welfare misprediction, and reason-based choice all affect institutional design.
