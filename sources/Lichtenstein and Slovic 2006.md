# Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006: The construction of preference

## Summary

[[Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006]] is the top-level work note for *The Construction of Preference*. In the current vault, the volume matters as the clearest source-family home for the claim that preferences are often constructed in the process of elicitation rather than simply revealed from a stable internal ranking. It gathers the classic preference-reversal literature, later psychological and process theories, and a set of wider extensions into consumer choice, affect, contingent valuation, and policy.

For the current wiki, the book matters most as a structured backbone for [[Constructed preferences]], [[Preference reversals]], and [[Procedure invariance]]. It should deepen those pages and their nearest welfare-facing implications without turning the vault into an encyclopedia of every adjacent chapter.

## Structural map

The volume is organized into nine parts:

- introduction
- preference reversals
- psychological theories of preference reversals
- evidence for preference construction
- theories of preference construction
- affect and reason
- miswanting
- contingent valuation
- preference management

In the current vault, the strongest first-wave units are Chapter 1 as the architectural overview, Chapter 2 as an early empirical foothold, Chapter 5 as the economics-facing statement of the challenge, Chapter 6 as a mechanism chapter on contingent weighting, and Chapter 17 as the broadest later theoretical extension into consumer choice.

## Main claims

The first claim is that normatively equivalent elicitation procedures often generate systematically different rankings or valuations. That is the empirical route into the challenge to revealed preference.

The second claim is that these divergences are not mere noise. They suggest that preferences are partly shaped by response mode, evaluability, context, and task structure.

The third claim is that the implications are both descriptive and normative. If elicited preferences are context-sensitive, then economics, law, and policy cannot automatically treat observed choice or valuation as transparent evidence of stable welfare.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this work matters because it turns a family of decision anomalies into a general challenge to how economics interprets preference. It matters for economics because it pressures revealed-preference reasoning. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about autonomy, value, and the ontology of preference. It matters for politics and public policy because institutions often rely on elicited valuations in compensation, cost-benefit reasoning, and regulatory design.

In the current vault, the book should function as the main structural source for the preference-construction branch, while later sources such as [[Ariely et al. 2003]] and [[Ariely et al. 2006]] remain the strongest downstream extensions rather than the sole anchors of the branch.
