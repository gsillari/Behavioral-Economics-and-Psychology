# Sunstein 2025: Brave new world? Human welfare and paternalistic AI
## Summary

[[Sunstein 2025]] is a conceptual and policy-oriented paper about AI-powered "choice engines" that personalize recommendations, defaults, and warnings in order to improve human welfare. In the current vault, the paper matters less as a source of a brand-new concept than as a bridge between two branches that were already present but not yet tightly connected: [[Behavioral public policy]] and the newer AI branch.

Its central claim is that AI may allow much more precise responses to behavioral frictions than older one-size-fits-all interventions. Because AI systems can incorporate information about what different people value, and about which people are especially vulnerable to present bias, inattention, or other behavioral problems, they may be able to preserve freedom of choice while still steering people toward better outcomes. At the same time, the paper insists that the same systems can be manipulative, deceptive, biased, or enlisted by self-interested actors. The result is a more personalized version of familiar [[Nudge]] and paternalism debates, not an escape from them.

## Key claims

The first claim is that AI-powered choice engines can reduce both informational deficits and behavioral biases. In this respect, the paper extends familiar behavioral-public-policy logic into a more personalized technological setting.

The second claim is that personalization matters for welfare. Uniform interventions such as labels or warnings may help some people and harm others, whereas AI systems might target those who would actually benefit. That makes the paper especially relevant to heterogeneous-treatment and welfare-analysis questions inside behavioral policy.

The third claim is that AI can be used not only to respond to [[Internalities]], understood as harms current selves impose on future selves, but also to respond to externalities. A choice engine might therefore guide people by taking account of both private and social costs.

The fourth claim is that AI-powered choice engines may sit naturally inside [[Libertarian paternalism]] by preserving formal freedom while improving recommendation quality. But the same tools can also move toward more coercive forms of intervention if they make exit difficult or if they invisibly steer behavior.

The fifth claim is that AI systems are not neutral. They may reproduce behavioral biases, introduce new ones, or be designed and deployed by actors who seek profit or control rather than user welfare. Legal safeguards against deception, manipulation, and opaque steering are therefore indispensable.

## Evidence and methods

The paper is primarily conceptual and normative rather than a new empirical study. Sunstein develops the argument through examples involving calorie labels, home energy reports, sugary-drink warnings, fuel economy, and AI-generated recommendations. The method is to combine existing behavioral-policy findings with a forward-looking analysis of how AI might individualize the design of choice architecture.

That means this paper should not be treated as the empirical anchor for AI effects on welfare. Its contribution is architectural. It shows how existing ideas about heterogeneity, [[Internalities]], personalization, and paternalism change once AI becomes a live policy instrument.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Sunstein 2025]] matters because it shows how an old behavioral-policy debate mutates under new technological conditions. It matters for economics because it reframes welfare analysis around heterogeneity and personalized intervention rather than average treatment effects alone. It matters for philosophy because it sharpens the questions of autonomy, manipulation, future selves, and the legitimacy of paternalistic steering when recommendations become individualized and opaque. It matters for politics and law because it makes clear that AI governance is not only about safety or fairness in the abstract, but also about who gets to design, authorize, regulate, and contest systems that shape everyday choice.

Pedagogically, the paper is especially useful because it connects several parts of the vault that students might otherwise keep separate: [[Libertarian paternalism]], [[Internalities]], and the AI branch.

## Open questions

Should the vault eventually create a reusable concept around AI-mediated choice architecture or choice engines, or is that still too thin as a standalone branch? How much personalization is compatible with genuine autonomy rather than just better-targeted steering? And when AI systems are said to improve welfare, whose welfare standard is actually doing the work?
