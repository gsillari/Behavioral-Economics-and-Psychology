# Aumann 1962: Utility theory without the completeness axiom
## Summary

[[Aumann 1962]] is a foundational decision-theory paper that asks whether utility theory really requires the [[Completeness axiom]]. The paper's importance in this vault is not as a behavioral result, but as an internal softening of the classical benchmark: it shows that formal utility theory can still be meaningful even when agents are not assumed to rank every pair of alternatives.

## Key claims

The central claim is that the classical demand for complete comparability is stronger than utility theory strictly needs. Incompleteness does not automatically destroy formal analysis; it can instead mark cases where preferences are only partially ordered or not fully settled. This matters because later behavioral economics often criticizes highly idealized rational-choice models, and [[Aumann 1962]] shows that part of that idealization had already been questioned from within formal economic theory itself.

## Evidence and methods

The paper is conceptual and axiomatic rather than experimental. It works by reconsidering one of the standard rationality assumptions built into utility theory and showing how the formal framework can be reconstructed without requiring that every pair of options be comparable. In the current vault, the paper therefore functions as a benchmark-refinement source rather than as part of the anomaly literature. Aumann later published a short correction in 1964, so this source should be treated as foundational but not as the final word on every theorem in the original paper.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Aumann 1962]] matters because it complicates the usual contrast between rigid classical rationality and later behavioral critique. It matters for economics because it shows that decision theory is internally revisable and need not treat full comparability as sacrosanct. It matters for philosophy because the [[Completeness axiom]] is a major candidate requirement of rationality, and relaxing it raises questions about indecision, incommensurability, and underdetermined choice. It matters for politics and policy because institutions often assume citizens have complete and well-ordered preferences, even when many real choices involve conflict, ambiguity, or value pluralism.

## Links into the wiki

The main derived concept is [[Completeness axiom]]. The source directly deepens [[Expected utility theory]] by showing that the classical benchmark is less monolithic than it first appears. It also supports the topic page [[Behavioral Economics]] and the broader synthesis in [[What Behavioral Economics Thinks Rationality Is]] by adding an internal refinement of formal rationality alongside the later behavioral challenges to it.

## Open questions

One open question is how far incompleteness should be treated as rational permissibility rather than as an admission of theoretical weakness. Another is whether relaxing completeness changes only formal representation or also the normative meaning of rational choice. A third is how this older decision-theory move should be connected to later behavioral findings about unstable, constructed, or conflicted preferences. A fourth is whether the vault should eventually ingest Aumann's 1964 correction as its own note.
