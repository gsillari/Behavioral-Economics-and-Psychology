# Arkes 1991: Costs and benefits of judgment errors

## Summary

[[Arkes 1991]] argues that many judgment errors should not be treated as sheer maladaptive noise. Some are costs of otherwise adaptive cognitive systems. That move matters for the current vault because it gives the debiasing branch a more discriminating architecture: before asking how to correct a bias, we should ask what kind of process produced it and what benefit that process normally provides.

In the current wiki, this paper is especially useful because it pushes back against a flat reading of the heuristics tradition. It suggests that the existence of error does not by itself settle the question of correction, and that the likely success of a debiasing strategy depends on the kind of error involved.

## Key claims

The first claim is that laboratory demonstrations of bias do not automatically show that the underlying processes are globally maladaptive. Cognitive systems can be highly useful overall while still generating predictable local errors.

The second claim is that judgment errors can be grouped into distinct classes. Arkes proposes a three-part taxonomy centered on strategy-based errors, association-based errors, and psychophysical-based errors.

The third claim is that debiasing should be matched to that taxonomy. There is no single generic debiasing technique that works across all cases. Some errors will respond to training or explicit rules, while others are more resistant because they arise from deeper associative or perceptual structures.

## Evidence and methods

This is a conceptual review article rather than a new experiment. Its method is classificatory and strategic: it surveys the bias literature, interprets some errors as the costs of adaptive systems, and uses that taxonomy to generate hypotheses about which corrective strategies are likely to work.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it complicates the easy slide from "bias exists" to "correction is obviously warranted." It matters for economics and public policy because it suggests that interventions should be tailored to the underlying source of the error rather than built around a generic anti-bias stance. It matters for philosophy because it raises a deeper issue about normativity: if some errors are by-products of systems that are broadly adaptive, then the case for correction becomes comparative and context-sensitive rather than automatic.

## Open questions

Which of Arkes's three error classes map best onto the current concept structure of the vault? When do adaptive benefits outweigh the local costs of a bias? And how should policy distinguish between cases that call for correction, cases that call for redesigned environments, and cases that should simply be tolerated?
