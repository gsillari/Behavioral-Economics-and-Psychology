# Hellman 2020: Measuring algorithmic fairness
## Summary

[[Hellman 2020]] is a legal-philosophical paper about how algorithmic fairness should be measured. It focuses on a central conflict in the fairness literature: whether we should prioritize criteria tied to predictive parity and calibration or criteria tied to parity in false positives and false negatives. In the current vault, the paper matters as one of the clearest normative interpretations of the fairness-metrics debate.

Its core contribution is to argue that some commonly cited measures do not track fairness in the same way. Equal predictive accuracy is mainly about what one ought to believe given a score, whereas error-rate disparities matter more directly for action and for how burdens are distributed across groups. That makes the paper important not only for [[Algorithmic bias]], but also for the broader question of which statistical criteria are morally salient.

## Key claims

The first claim is that not all fairness metrics measure the same thing, even if they are often discussed as interchangeable.

The second claim is that predictive parity is conceptually closer to a norm about belief than to a norm about fair action.

The third claim is that parity in the ratio of false positives to false negatives has stronger normative significance because it tracks how decision errors burden groups differently.

The fourth claim is that legal and policy analysis should not assume that anti-discrimination law straightforwardly blocks all uses of protected-class information in efforts to improve fairness.

The fifth claim is that algorithmic-fairness debate needs both conceptual clarity and legal realism rather than metric selection by intuition alone.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Hellman 2020]] matters because it translates a technical metrics debate into a question about belief, action, and fairness. It matters for philosophy because it asks what kind of norm a statistical criterion expresses. It matters for law and politics because it explores how fairness arguments do and do not fit anti-discrimination doctrine. It matters for economics because it clarifies why institutional choices about error distribution cannot be reduced to overall accuracy.
