# Kahneman and Tversky 1972: Subjective probability
## Summary

[[Kahneman and Tversky 1972]] is the earliest major representativeness paper in the current vault. Before the label [[Heuristics and Biases]] becomes fully canonical in [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]], this paper already develops the key idea: people often judge the subjective probability of an event by the degree to which it resembles its parent population or the process that generated it.

Its importance is that it gives the representativeness branch an earlier and more precise starting point than the standard 1974 survey article alone. In the current vault, it should anchor [[Representativeness heuristic]] together with [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]], while also strengthening the more specific problem pages [[Base-rate neglect]] and [[Law of small numbers]].

## Key claims

The paper's first claim is that judgments of probability are often similarity-based rather than rule-based. People treat likelihood as if it were a matter of fit between a case and a prototype, or between a sample and the process from which it is drawn.

The second claim is that this mode of judgment creates recurrent departures from probability theory. If resemblance drives judgment, then prior probabilities can be underweighted, sample size can receive too little attention, and chance processes can be misunderstood as if small samples should already display the structure of the larger population.

The third claim is that these are not isolated slips. The same basic inferential style appears across judgments of category membership, predictions about outcomes, and assessments of stochastic processes. That gives representativeness the status of a genuine cognitive mechanism rather than a single task effect.

## Representativeness before the full heuristics-and-biases program

This paper matters because it shows that the representativeness branch has its own conceptual depth. In the current vault, [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] often functions as the opening source for judgment under uncertainty, but [[Kahneman and Tversky 1972]] is where one of the central mechanisms becomes visible in a more focused way.

The paper therefore helps clarify the internal architecture of the broader program. [[Representativeness heuristic]] is not just one example inside a trio. It is an early organizing idea in its own right, and many later patterns can be read as different expressions of that mechanism.

## Main inferential errors

The most important downstream errors in the current vault are [[Base-rate neglect]] and the [[Law of small numbers]]. The first arises when vivid case features or resemblance to a stereotype overwhelm background statistical information. The second arises when people expect small samples to look too much like the larger process from which they are drawn.

This matters structurally because it makes the representativeness branch more than one concept page. It now has a cleaner internal hierarchy: the general mechanism in [[Representativeness heuristic]], and more specific inferential failures in [[Base-rate neglect]] and [[Law of small numbers]].

## Relation to later sources

[[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] generalizes the argument into the larger heuristics-and-biases research program. [[Tversky and Kahneman 1981]] then pushes the representativeness branch further into [[Conjunction fallacy]] territory by showing how increasing descriptive specificity can increase judged plausibility while reducing formal probability. [[Kahneman and Frederick 2002]] later reframes the whole family through [[Attribute substitution]], giving representativeness a more explicit process interpretation.

In the current vault, this means the representativeness lineage now has a stronger developmental arc: early mechanism statement in 1972, broader programmatic placement in 1974, major extension in 1981, and process reinterpretation in 2002.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows behavioral economics beginning from a deep question about rational belief, not only from market anomalies or policy tools. It matters for economics because it challenges the assumption that people track probability in a disciplined statistical way. It matters for philosophy because it asks what it means to judge likelihood rationally and whether resemblance is ever a defensible substitute for inference. It matters for politics because citizens, experts, and institutions regularly rely on case-based impressions that can crowd out base rates and population reasoning.

This source is therefore especially useful for teaching. It helps students see that the critique of rationality in behavioral economics starts on the belief side as well as the preference side.

## Open questions

How much of representativeness should be understood as a prototype mechanism and how much as a family of related inferential shortcuts? When, if ever, can similarity provide useful probabilistic guidance rather than a misleading cue? And how should this early paper be positioned relative to the better-known 1974 article in the course-facing structure of the vault?
