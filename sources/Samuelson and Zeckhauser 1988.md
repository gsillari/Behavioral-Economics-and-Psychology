# Samuelson and Zeckhauser 1988: Status quo bias in decision making

## Summary

[[Samuelson and Zeckhauser 1988]] is the classic economics-facing paper on [[Status quo bias]]. William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser argue that when one option is framed as the current position or default, decision makers disproportionately stick with it. In the current vault, the paper matters because it helps explain why defaults are powerful well before the later nudge literature turns them into a central policy tool.

## Key claims

The paper's central claim is that people exhibit a substantial bias toward maintaining the status quo. This persistence is stronger than standard rational-choice models would predict if framing and labels were irrelevant to choice.

The paper also argues that the bias is visible in both experiments and consequential real choices, including faculty selections among health plans and retirement programs. That makes it useful in the current vault as a bridge from laboratory-style decision anomalies to administratively important choice environments.

Finally, the paper surveys several possible explanations, including rational calculations under uncertainty, cognitive misperception, and psychological commitment to existing states. In the current vault, that makes the source useful not only as a finding but as an early map of competing interpretations.

## Evidence and methods

The source combines decision experiments with naturally occurring cases in which a preexisting option is salient. Its main methodological value is that it isolates the status-quo label itself as a force in choice rather than treating inertia as a vague byproduct of preference or search cost alone.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows how a seemingly minor framing condition can alter major institutional decisions. It matters for economics because it complicates the idea that stable choice simply reveals stable preferences. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about when inertia is rational, when it is biased, and how much normative weight to give the current state of affairs. It matters for politics because default effects are central to pension design, insurance enrollment, consent rules, and many later debates in behavioral public policy.

## Open questions

How much of status quo bias should be explained by rational caution under uncertainty rather than by bias proper? When do defaults help by reducing burdens, and when do they distort welfare-relevant choice? And how tightly should the vault connect status quo bias to later work on defaults and paternalism?
