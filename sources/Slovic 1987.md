# Slovic 1987: Perception of risk
## Summary

[[Slovic 1987]] is a foundational overview of modern research on [[Risk perception]]. Its central claim is that public judgments of risk are not simply ignorant or noisy versions of expert probability estimates. They are structured by qualitative dimensions such as dread, catastrophic potential, controllability, familiarity, and perceived voluntariness. In the current vault, this matters because it gives the risk branch a proper conceptual center rather than leaving it distributed across later mechanism pages such as [[Affect heuristic]] and [[Dread risk]].

The paper is especially important because it codifies the psychometric approach to risk. Instead of asking only whether laypeople get probabilities right, Slovic shows that people evaluate hazards through a multidimensional map of social meaning, fear, unknownness, and control. That is a major shift for the vault because it turns risk judgment into a problem of perception, evaluation, and public legitimacy, not just one of statistical error.

## Key claims

The first claim is that expert and lay judgments of risk do not differ only in accuracy. They often differ in what they are attending to and in what they mean by a hazard's seriousness.

The second claim is that risk perception has stable qualitative dimensions. Hazards that are dreaded, catastrophic, uncontrollable, or unfamiliar tend to be perceived as especially threatening.

The third claim is that these judgments are not peripheral to policy. Public resistance, regulation, trust, and controversy depend on them.

The fourth claim is that risk cannot be understood adequately as objective expected harm alone. Perceived risk is partly social and psychological, and this has to be built into any serious account of public decision-making.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source deserves foundational status in the risk branch. [[Tversky and Kahneman 1973]] and [[Lichtenstein et al. 1978]] already give the vault classic availability-based distortions in judgments of frequency. [[Finucane et al. 2000]] adds the [[Affect heuristic]], and [[Gigerenzer 2004]] gives a vivid case of [[Dread risk]] after September 11. But without [[Slovic 1987]], those pages remain somewhat fragmented. This paper gives them a common conceptual background and makes clear that risk judgment is not an afterthought inside behavioral economics. It is one of the places where descriptive psychology, public policy, and normative conflict meet most sharply.

It also matters because it strengthens the policy branch without forcing everything into paternalism. The paper helps explain why risk communication, technological controversy, and regulatory legitimacy are hard even when formal cost-benefit arguments are available. That makes it an important bridge between [[Heuristics and Biases]] and [[Behavioral public policy]].

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows that "risk" is not a purely technical category. It matters for economics because market and policy responses depend on perceived risk, not only actuarial risk. It matters for philosophy because the paper raises questions about the authority of expert judgment, the normativity of public fear, and whether lay evaluations are biased, value-sensitive, or sometimes rationally responsive to dimensions experts ignore. It matters for politics because democratic conflict over technology, health, environment, and security is often a conflict over risk perception rather than over bare facts alone.
