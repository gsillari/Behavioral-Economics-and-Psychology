# Mercier and Sperber 2011: Why do humans reason? Arguments for an argumentative theory
## Summary

[[Mercier and Sperber 2011]] argues that the main function of human reasoning is argumentative rather than purely contemplative. On this view, reasoning did not evolve primarily to let solitary agents discover truth in a neutral way. Its core role is to produce and evaluate arguments in social settings. That claim matters in the current vault because it changes the structure of the rationality debate. Instead of asking only which benchmark should be used to judge thought, the paper asks what reasoning is for in the first place.

This source is especially important because it sits inside a full *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* exchange. The target article is followed by commentaries and the authors' response. In the current vault, four pieces of that discussion are especially useful. [[Wim De Neys]] argues that people often detect logical conflict even in nonargumentative tasks, which pushes back against too strong a social reading of reasoning. [[Jonathan St. B. T. Evans]] accepts that argumentation may be a major function of reasoning but argues that reasoning was likely co-opted for reflective thought and prospective decision-making too. Weber and Johnson's commentary on query theory suggests that people can also argue with themselves in ways that construct preferences intrapersonally. Mercier and Sperber's reply resists turning these concessions into a retreat: they maintain that the primary function of reasoning is still argumentative even if it has useful secondary roles in individual cognition.

## Key claims

The first claim is functional. Reasoning is better understood as an adaptation for social exchange, persuasion, and argument evaluation than as a general-purpose truth engine for isolated individuals.

The second claim is interpretive. Many classic failures of reasoning look less paradoxical once reasoning is seen as a tool for producing plausible justifications and for scrutinizing the arguments of others.

The third claim is asymmetrical. People are often better at evaluating the weaknesses in others' arguments than at neutrally inspecting the weaknesses in their own. This helps explain why reasoning can simultaneously look impressive in debate and biased in private belief formation.

The fourth claim, clarified by the surrounding discussion, is that the paper does not deny all individual uses of reasoning. Rather, it argues that such uses are often derivative, partial, or best understood as extensions of an underlying argumentative capacity.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source deserves a place in the spine of the vault because it reorients the rationality branch at the level of function. [[Simon 1955]] asks what rationality should look like for finite agents. [[Gigerenzer 2008]] asks whether ecological success matters more than conformity to formal norms. [[Mercier and Sperber 2011]] asks why reasoning exists at all. That is a different kind of intervention, and it makes the [[Rationality wars]] richer than a simple dispute over norms or performance.

It also matters because it connects several existing branches that otherwise risk staying too separate. It belongs near [[Fast and slow thinking]] because it questions the easy picture of reflection as a neutral corrective faculty. It belongs near [[Motivated reasoning]] and [[Confirmation bias]] because it helps explain why reflective thought can produce polished rationalization rather than correction. And it belongs near [[Myside bias]] because it gives a broader functional backdrop for why biased perspective-taking may persist even among sophisticated reasoners.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it reframes rationality as a social practice rather than only as an individual achievement. It matters for economics because market and policy reasoning often occurs through advocacy, justification, and contestation rather than through detached optimization. It matters for philosophy because it raises direct questions about the aim of reason, the relation between truth and persuasion, and whether argument can be epistemically valuable even when it is not neutral. It matters for politics because public reasoning, democratic disagreement, ideological defense, and institutional deliberation all look different once reasoning is understood as partly adapted for adversarial exchange.

## Open questions

How much of reasoning's individual problem-solving role can be treated as a later exaptation rather than a coequal function? Does the argumentative theory explain only biased reasoning, or also some of the strongest cases of collective intelligence? And how should PPE students judge institutions that rely on adversarial reasoning if the same faculty is both epistemically productive and systematically self-serving?
