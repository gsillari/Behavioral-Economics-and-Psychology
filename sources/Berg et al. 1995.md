# Berg et al. 1995: Trust, reciprocity, and social history

## Summary

[[Berg et al. 1995]] is the classic source for the experimental [[Trust game]], often called the investment game. In the basic design, a first mover can transfer some of an endowment to an anonymous second mover; the amount sent is multiplied; the second mover can then return some amount. Standard narrow self-interest predicts no return by the second mover and therefore no transfer by the first mover.

The empirical point is that subjects do transfer and return money even under anonymity, one-shot play, and controls designed to remove repeated-game reputation, contractual precommitment, and punishment. That makes the paper a central source for [[Trust]] and [[Reciprocity]] as behavioral primitives in economic interaction.

## What it adds to the wiki

This source adds a second experimental-game spine beside the [[Ultimatum game]]. Ultimatum bargaining is about unfair treatment and the willingness to punish it. The trust game is about vulnerability: one player deliberately gives another player the power to reciprocate or exploit. This distinction matters because trust is not just generosity and not just risk taking. It is a social relation in which the meaning of the other player's response matters.

The "social history" treatment is especially important conceptually. It shows that trust and reciprocity are not simply fixed traits; they can be strengthened by information about how others behaved in similar settings. That gives the vault a route from isolated laboratory games to norms, expectations, and institutional background.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Berg et al. 1995]] matters because trust is a central bridge between individual choice and institutional life. It matters for economics because anonymous one-shot interaction still shows transfers and returns that narrow self-interest does not predict. It matters for philosophy because trust involves vulnerability, obligation, and response to another agent rather than only payoff comparison. It matters for politics because institutions often function by reducing the need for trust, supporting it, or punishing its betrayal.
