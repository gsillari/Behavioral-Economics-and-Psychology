# Nickerson 1998: Confirmation bias
## Summary

[[Nickerson 1998]] is the major review source for [[Confirmation bias]] in the current vault. It surveys the tendency to seek, interpret, and remember evidence in ways that favor existing beliefs, expectations, or hypotheses.

In this wiki, the paper matters because it gives the judgment branch a durable concept that is adjacent to but not reducible to the classic Tversky-Kahneman heuristics. It helps connect heuristic error, hypothesis testing, belief perseverance, and motivated evidence processing.

## Key claims

The first claim is that confirmation bias appears in many guises rather than as one narrow experimental effect.

The second claim is that the bias can operate in evidence search, evidence interpretation, and memory.

The third claim is that the phenomenon is important both practically and theoretically because it shapes how people update beliefs under uncertainty.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source gives [[Nonstandard beliefs]] a stronger cross-cutting mechanism page. It also helps the vault distinguish between errors caused by shortcut-based intuitive judgment and errors caused by selective use of evidence after a view is already in place.

That makes it a useful companion to [[Kunda 1990]] and [[Epley and Gilovich 2016]]. Those later sources help explain how directional goals shape reasoning. [[Nickerson 1998]] provides the wider review frame showing how pervasive the phenomenon is.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because political and philosophical disagreement often turns less on missing information than on how information is filtered. It matters for economics because expectations and forecasts can become resistant to disconfirming evidence. It matters for philosophy because it pressures ideals of neutral evidence assessment. It matters for politics because ideological persistence, policy polarization, and institutional blind spots often have exactly this structure.
