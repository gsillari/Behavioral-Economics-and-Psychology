# Zaklan 2023: Coase and cap-and-trade
## Summary

[[Zaklan 2023]] is a modern empirical test of [[Coase theorem]] in a large cap-and-trade market. The paper asks whether emissions outcomes are independent of the initial allocation of allowances, as a strong Coasean benchmark would suggest.

In the current vault, this source matters because it gives the endowment-effect and entitlement branch a contemporary institutional application. Instead of laboratory mugs or marketplace tokens, the relevant endowment is the initial allocation of tradable emissions permits.

## Key claims

The first claim is that [[Coase theorem]] broadly holds at the sector level in the European carbon market.

The second claim is that deviations are more visible for smaller emitters, where transaction costs or behavioral frictions may matter more.

The third claim is that endowment-sensitive distortions need not destroy market performance in aggregate, but they can still matter in particular institutional margins.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source deepens the Coase branch by moving from abstract rights allocation to a concrete modern regulatory market. It matters for the endowment-effect branch because it asks a Coasean version of the same question: does initial entitlement alter behavior when trade is possible?

That makes the paper a valuable counterweight to simpler behavioral readings. It does not show that endowment effects are unreal. It shows that in some large markets, [[Coase theorem]] can still hold fairly well, especially when trading opportunities are strong enough and transaction costs are manageable.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it connects behavioral economics to environmental policy and institutional design. It matters for economics because it tests whether initial allocation affects outcomes in a major regulated market. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about when entitlement effects should be treated as normatively decisive and when they are institutionally absorbed. It matters for politics because cap-and-trade systems often use free allocation for political reasons, and this paper speaks directly to whether that choice compromises efficiency.
