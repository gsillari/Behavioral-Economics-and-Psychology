# Ross and Sicoly 1979: Egocentric biases in availability and attribution

## Summary

[[Ross and Sicoly 1979]] is the original article behind the blue-book chapter on egocentric biases in availability and attribution. Michael Ross and Fiore Sicoly argue that in joint activities people recall their own contributions more readily than those of others and correspondingly assign themselves more responsibility for the outcome. In the current vault, the paper matters because it extends the [[Availability heuristic]] branch into social judgment and attribution rather than leaving availability as a mainly statistical or risk-perception mechanism.

## Key claims

The paper's central claim is that availability can be egocentrically structured. One's own actions, statements, and efforts are often easier to recall than the comparable inputs of others.

The paper also argues that this retrieval asymmetry shapes attribution. When people allocate responsibility for a joint product, they often credit themselves more heavily because their own contributions are more cognitively available.

Finally, the paper shows that the bias can be attenuated when attention is redirected toward another participant's contributions. In the current vault, that makes the paper useful not only as a demonstration of error but as a clue about mechanism.

## Evidence and methods

The source reports five experiments across several natural and laboratory groups, including discussion groups, married couples, and teams. Its main value in the vault is to show that availability is not only about estimating frequencies of external events; it can also shape memory and responsibility judgments in interpersonal settings.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows how a cognitive mechanism travels from judgment under uncertainty into social explanation. It matters for economics because many cooperative and organizational settings require people to assess who contributed what. It matters for philosophy because it complicates naive ideas about fairness, memory, and responsibility. It matters for politics because disputes about collective effort, blame, and recognition often depend on exactly these egocentric retrieval asymmetries.
