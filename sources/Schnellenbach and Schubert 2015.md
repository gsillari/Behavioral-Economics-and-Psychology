# Schnellenbach and Schubert 2015: Behavioral political economy

## Summary

[[Schnellenbach and Schubert 2015]] is a broad survey of [[Behavioral political economy]]. Jan Schnellenbach and Christian Schubert argue that if behavioral economics is taken seriously in markets, it should also be taken seriously in politics, where feedback is weaker, incentives to become informed are often thinner, and institutional actors are no less behaviorally bounded than private ones.

In the current vault, the paper matters because it blocks an easy move from behavioral findings about citizens to benevolent-corrective government. It insists on motivational and behavioral symmetry across market and political arenas.

## Key claims

The paper's first central claim is methodological. Political economy should apply the same behavioral assumptions to voters, politicians, bureaucrats, and regulators that behavioral economics applies to consumers and firms.

The second claim is substantive. Many biases may be at least as strong in political settings as in markets because the political arena often offers weaker incentives for learning and more indirect feedback.

The third claim is cautionary. Behavioral findings do not automatically justify paternalistic intervention, because governments and political processes are themselves subject to bounded rationality, motivated reasoning, and institutional distortion.

## Evidence and scope

The source is a survey rather than a single experiment or model. Its value in the current vault is synthetic: it organizes the emerging field of behavioral political economy and links prospect-theory-style ideas, voter irrationality, social norms, paternalism, and public-choice concerns into one map.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it is one of the clearest bridges between behavioral economics and political theory. It matters for economics because it extends behavioral assumptions into political choice. It matters for philosophy because it complicates simple paternalist arguments by questioning the cognitive authority of the state. It matters for politics because it reframes behavioral economics as a theory not only of market error but also of political decision-making.

## Open questions

How far can behavioral findings travel from individual choice tasks to collective political outcomes? Does behavioral political economy mainly discipline paternalist optimism, or can it also generate positive theories of institutional design? And which behavioral mechanisms remain most robust in low-feedback political settings?
