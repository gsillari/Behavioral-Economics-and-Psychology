# Lewis 1969: Convention

## Summary

[[Lewis 1969]] is the canonical philosophical successor to Schelling's analysis of coordination. Where [[Schelling 1960 - Chapter 04 - Toward a Theory of Interdependent Decision]] shows that focal points, labels, and mutual expectations matter for coordinating action, Lewis turns that insight into a general theory of [[Convention]].

In this first pass, Chapters 1-3 have been ingested. They establish the coordination-game background, refine the definition of convention through [[Common knowledge]], and contrast convention with agreement, social contract, norms, rules, conformative behavior, and imitation.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Lewis 1969]] matters because it gives a philosophical microfoundation for conventions as solutions to recurrent coordination problems. That makes it an immediate bridge between game theory and social institutions. A convention is not merely a habit, a rule, or an agreement; it is a regularity sustained by expectations, preferences conditional on others' conformity, alternatives, and common knowledge.

This is important for behavioral economics and PPE because many institutional patterns are neither hard law nor private preference. They are stable because agents expect others to follow them and because each agent has reason to follow if others do. Lewis therefore provides one of the cleanest routes from [[Coordination games]] to later game-theoretic analyses of norms, language, money, traffic rules, bargaining, law, and social institutions.
