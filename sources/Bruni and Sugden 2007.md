# Bruni and Sugden 2007: The road not taken

## Summary

[[Bruni and Sugden 2007]] is a historical and methodological reconstruction of how economics separated itself from psychology, and of why that separation should not be treated as an obvious story of scientific progress. The paper matters because it argues that early neoclassical economics was not psychology-free at all. Before the [[Paretian turn]], it drew on what was then serious scientific psychology, especially psychophysics and the psychology of sensation. Behavioral economics is therefore not simply importing psychology into a discipline that had always excluded it. In one important sense, it is trying to reconnect with an older road in economics that was left behind.

The paper's central historical claim is that Pareto's reformulation of economics should be understood as a self-conscious attempt to turn economics into a separate science of rational choice, detached from psychology. Bruni and Sugden argue that this move was later completed and stabilized by Hicks, Allen, and Samuelson. They also argue that this history parallels later defenses of rational-choice theory against behavioral economics, especially those built around the idea of `discovered preference`: the claim that through experience and learning, agents eventually come to behave in accordance with stable, internally consistent preferences, while deviations are treated as temporary errors.

## Key claims

The first claim is that nineteenth-century neoclassical economics was more psychologically grounded than standard textbook history admits. Jevons, Edgeworth, and Pantaleoni did not merely decorate economics with loose mental language. They treated their theory as connected to the best psychology available to them, especially the psychophysics of sensation and diminishing sensitivity.

The second claim is that the [[Paretian turn]] was a real methodological rupture. Pareto explicitly tried to purge economics of psychological analysis and to redefine it as a science of `logical action`, grounded in rational choice rather than in sensation, pleasure, or mental process.

The third claim is that this older historical dispute closely resembles later methodological disputes over behavioral economics. Bruni and Sugden argue that modern `discovered preference` defenses of rational-choice theory replay many of Pareto's moves: they narrow the domain in which rational-choice theory is supposed to apply, and they interpret deviations as signs of incomplete learning rather than as evidence against the model.

The fourth claim is that these anti-psychological strategies face two recurring problems. They struggle to define the domain of economics in a stable and defensible way, and they struggle to explain why the underlying preferences they posit should have the strong consistency and stability properties required by rational-choice theory.

The fifth claim is that the `road not taken` was not obviously a dead end. If early economics had continued to engage more directly with psychology rather than separating itself from it, some of the concerns now central to behavioral economics might have become part of the discipline much earlier.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Bruni and Sugden 2007]] matters because it turns a familiar slogan into a real historical problem. The issue is not merely whether behavioral economics uses better psychology than standard economics. It is also whether the whole discipline once made a methodological choice to define itself against psychology, and whether that choice should still guide theory today. It matters for economics because it reframes revealed preference and rational-choice methodology as historically situated strategies rather than neutral starting points. It matters for philosophy because it asks what kind of science economics is meant to be. And it matters for politics and policy because later welfare and rationality debates inherit this older settlement about what counts as evidence of preference and agency.

## Open questions

How much of modern economics still relies on the Paretian aspiration to be a separate science of rational choice? Did revealed preference solve a scientific problem, or mainly redescribe old psychological questions in observational language? And if the anti-psychological turn was historically contingent, what should count as a legitimate role for psychology inside economics now?
