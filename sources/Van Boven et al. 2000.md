# Van Boven et al. 2000: Egocentric empathy gaps between owners and buyers
## Summary

[[Van Boven et al. 2000]] studies the social-perceptual side of the [[Endowment effect]]. The paper argues that owners and buyers do not merely value goods differently. They also systematically mispredict each other's valuations because they insufficiently take the other party's standpoint into account.

In the current vault, this source matters because it extends the endowment-effect branch beyond ownership-sensitive valuation alone. It adds a perspective-taking mechanism: gaps between buyers and sellers are sustained not only by different reference points, but also by [[Endowment effect]] in how each side imagines the other's evaluative position.

## Key claims

The first claim is that owners and buyers systematically misperceive each other's valuations.

The second claim is that these errors arise because people project too much from their own current standpoint.

The third claim is that the endowment effect is therefore partly a social-cognitive problem as well as a valuation problem.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source deepens the endowment-effect branch by adding a mechanism that is neither pure loss aversion nor simple elicitation sensitivity. It shows that ownership differences are reinforced by failures of perspective-taking between market sides.

That makes the paper especially useful alongside [[Kahneman et al. 1990]], [[Knetsch and Sinden 1984]], and the List papers. The earlier sources establish the valuation gap and then test its robustness in markets. [[Van Boven et al. 2000]] adds a different layer by asking why the two sides of exchange may remain cognitively stuck in different evaluative frames.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it connects behavioral economics to moral psychology and social cognition. It matters for economics because market exchange depends not only on valuation but on how traders model each other. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about egocentrism, standpoint, and interpersonal understanding. It matters for politics because many bargaining failures and distributive disputes depend on exactly this inability to inhabit the other side's perspective.

## Open questions

How much of the buyer-seller gap reflects valuation itself and how much reflects mistaken models of the other side? Do repeated bargaining interactions reduce egocentric empathy gaps in the same way that market experience may reduce the endowment effect?
