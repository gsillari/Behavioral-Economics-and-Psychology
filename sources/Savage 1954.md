# Savage 1954: The Foundations of Statistics

## Summary

[[Savage 1954]] is one of the foundational books for the benchmark side of behavioral economics because it builds a decision-theoretic account of rational choice under uncertainty out of personal probability, preference over acts, and utility. In the current vault, the book should now function as the canonical source for [[Subjective expected utility theory]], not merely as a background name attached vaguely to later discussions of ambiguity or Bayesian choice.

Its importance is architectural. Savage does not simply say that probabilities may be subjective. He embeds subjective probability in a larger theory of rational decision, centered on acts, states of the world, the sure-thing principle, utility, observation, and partition structure. That is why the book matters so much for the current vault: it makes the uncertainty-side benchmark as explicit as [[Expected utility theory]] already makes the risk-side benchmark.

## Structure and coverage

Savage's own 1972 preface is unusually helpful for organizing the book. He says that the first part, ending with Chapter 7, is a general introduction to the personalistic tradition in probability and utility, whereas the second part turns toward statistics proper and frequentist devices. That division is exactly the right one for the vault.

In the current ingest, the foundational core is the first seven chapters:

Chapters 8 and after are mapped in this source note as later statistical and minimax material, but they are not central to the present pass. The first-pass objective is to ground the vault's benchmark language for subjective expected utility, not to ingest the whole later personalistic critique of frequentist statistics.

## Key claims

The first claim is that statistics and rational choice under uncertainty should be grounded in decision rather than in a purely verbal or purely frequency-based account of probability. Savage's framework begins from acts, consequences, and states of the world, not from long-run frequencies alone.

The second claim is that personal probability can be disciplined without requiring objective frequencies. Chapters 2-4 show how subjective probability can be built behaviorally out of preferences over acts, while still allowing serious criticism of oversimplified or symmetry-driven uses of the personalistic view.

The third claim is that utility under uncertainty is not merely utility over lotteries. Chapter 5 extends utility to acts in a broader decision-theoretic structure and introduces the small-world idea that later becomes crucial for interpreting the scope and limits of the theory.

The fourth claim is that observation and evidence enter the framework through changes in the informational structure of acts. Chapters 6-7 matter because they show how value of information, admissibility, sufficient statistics, likelihood ratios, and sequential procedures appear inside a personalistic decision framework rather than outside it.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Savage 1954]] matters because it clarifies what later behavioral critique is actually criticizing. Much of the behavioral literature is not reacting only to generic rational choice or to von Neumann-Morgenstern expected utility under risk. It is reacting to a stronger uncertainty benchmark in which agents can be represented as if they carried a coherent personal probability over states and evaluated acts through expected utility. Ellsberg's challenge, debates over ambiguity, and later arguments about the limits of sharp probabilities are all easier to understand once Savage is explicit.

The book also matters because it complicates any simple opposition between economics and psychology. Savage is not doing descriptive psychology, but neither is he merely offering a formal trick. He is proposing a disciplined bridge from uncertainty, belief, and choice to decision and statistics.
