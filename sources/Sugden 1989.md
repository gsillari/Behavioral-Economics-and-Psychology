# Sugden 1989: Spontaneous Order

## Summary

[[Sugden 1989]] is a compact and important statement of the spontaneous-order branch of game-theoretic social theory.
The paper starts from an informal driftwood custom in a Yorkshire fishing village and asks how a rule assigning de facto property rights can arise without design, persist without formal enforcement, and acquire moral force.
In the current vault, this is the clearest source for connecting [[Spontaneous order]], [[Convention]], [[Evolutionary game theory]], and Humean justice.

The paper's central claim is not that evolved conventions are necessarily efficient or just.
Its stronger and more interesting claim is that order can arise because some behavioral patterns are self-replicating.
Conventions survive when they are self-enforcing and evolutionarily stable, not because they have been collectively chosen, deduced by ideal reason, or optimized for social welfare.

## Key claims

The first claim is that many market institutions are spontaneous orders.
Buyers and sellers find one another, business districts emerge, informal markets survive despite legal suppression, and property-like expectations can exist before or outside state enforcement.
This matters because it shifts the explanation of institutions away from explicit design and toward self-sustaining convention.

The second claim is that classical game-theoretic rationality is too thin to explain conventions.
If players in a game such as Chicken are treated as perfectly rational but inexperienced, there may be no uniquely rational solution.
Conventions use information that abstract rational-choice models often bracket out: common human experience, prominence, analogy, precedent, and shared expectations.
This connects Sugden directly to [[Schelling 1960]] and [[Lewis 1969]].

The third claim is that conventions can be analyzed through evolutionary stability.
Sugden adapts the biological idea of an evolutionarily stable strategy to human conventions by substituting utility for fitness and learning or imitation for natural selection.
In his formulation, a convention is an ESS in a game with multiple ESSs: one of several possible self-enforcing rules that could stabilize.
This is the paper's direct bridge to [[Evolutionary game theory]].

The fourth claim is anti-Panglossian.
Evolutionary stability does not imply Pareto efficiency.
Some inefficient conventions can take root more easily because they are prominent, versatile, or spread by analogy from neighboring contexts.
This point is crucial for the vault because it prevents spontaneous order from becoming a simple apology for whatever institutions happen to exist.

The fifth claim is Humean.
Conventions can become norms when people come to believe that they ought to maintain the patterns that already structure mutual expectations.
Sugden draws explicitly on Hume's account of justice and property: principles of justice can arise from convention and later acquire moral force from inside the convention.
That does not show that the convention is morally justified from an external standpoint.
It explains why the convention can be experienced as binding.

## Spontaneous order, convention, and justice

The paper gives [[Spontaneous order]] a precise place in the vault.
Spontaneous order is not merely "order without government."
It is order generated by self-reinforcing conventions: patterns that replicate because people expect others to follow them and because successful patterns are repeated, imitated, and extended by analogy.

The link to [[Convention]] is direct.
Sugden's conventions are self-enforcing equilibria selected by experience, prominence, analogy, and evolutionary stability.
The driftwood rule, first-possession rules, queuing, and informal property conventions all illustrate this mechanism.

The link to [[Evolutionary game theory]] is also direct.
Sugden does not treat evolution as a metaphor for progress.
He uses evolutionary stability to explain why some conventions persist and why mixed-strategy equilibria may be unstable in games such as Chicken.
Deviant behavior is not merely error; if successful, it can be repeated and imitated.

The link to justice is Humean and deliberately limited.
Rules of property and justice may emerge as conventions, and moral resentment can grow around breaches of those conventions.
But this is an internal explanation of moral force, not an external proof that the convention is just.
Sugden explicitly warns that a convention may benefit one group more than another and may still generate resentment against breaches from within the convention.

## Why it matters here

This source is a missing hinge between the current Schelling-Lewis-Skyrms branch and the older Hume-Hayek tradition.
It explains how [[Convention]] can become institutionally important, why [[Evolutionary game theory]] matters for social order, and why emergent order should not be confused with efficient or normatively justified order.

It also strengthens [[Robert Sugden]]'s profile in the vault.
Sugden is already present through regret theory, the Paretian-turn history, and the later opportunity-based critique of behavioral welfare economics.
This paper adds the missing convention/cooperation/institutional branch of his work.

## Why it matters for PPE

For economics, the paper matters because it treats market institutions and property-like expectations as possible spontaneous orders rather than only as products of law, contract, or explicit optimization.
It also shows why efficiency is not the right default explanation of institutional survival.

For philosophy, it matters because it connects Humean justice, Hayekian spontaneous order, Schelling-style prominence, Lewisian convention, and evolutionary game theory.
It gives PPE students a way to see why convention theory is not just a theory of arbitrary social habits but a theory of how normatively charged social order can arise.

For politics, it matters because it cuts both ways.
Spontaneous order can explain how social order survives without central design, but it can also explain how inefficient or unequal conventions become stable.
The paper therefore supports institutional humility without collapsing into institutional conservatism.
