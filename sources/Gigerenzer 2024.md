# Gigerenzer 2024: The rationality wars
## Summary

[[Gigerenzer 2024]] is a reflective intervention in the [[Rationality wars]] by one of the central figures on the ecological-rationality side. Rather than presenting a new empirical program, the paper looks back on the conflict over heuristics, bias, and rationality and clarifies how Gigerenzer understands the dispute in personal and intellectual terms. In the current vault, the source matters because it sharpens the self-understanding of the ecological-rationality branch and helps distinguish substantive disagreement from disciplinary misunderstanding.

The paper is especially useful because it does not simply restate the older positive case from [[Gigerenzer 2008]]. It reflects on how the debate developed, which claims were misunderstood, and why benchmark choice remains the central issue. That gives the vault a more mature debate source: not only a defense of ecological rationality, but also a retrospective account of what the rationality wars were about.

## Key claims

The first claim is that the rationality wars were never only about whether humans make mistakes. They were also about what should count as a proper standard of rationality and which tasks are legitimate tests of reasoning. Gigerenzer argues that the heuristics-and-biases tradition too often treated one family of formal norms as if it were the only standard worth using.

The second claim is that ecological rationality should not be confused with a blanket defense of intuition or a denial of error. The point is instead that simple heuristics can perform well when matched to the informational structure of real environments. In the current vault, that keeps the source closely tied to [[Ecological rationality]] and away from any caricature in which Gigerenzer merely denies bias findings.

The third claim is that the rationality wars were intensified by rhetoric, disciplinary boundaries, and selective readings of the opposing side. That makes the paper a strong complement to [[Samuels et al. 2002]] and [[Hortal 2025]]. Samuels emphasizes that some disputes dissolve once standards are clarified. Hortal emphasizes epistemological pluralism. Gigerenzer adds the personal, historical, and polemical dimension from inside the debate itself.

## Evidence and methods

This is a reflective and programmatic article rather than a new experimental or field paper. Its evidence is conceptual, autobiographical, and historiographic. Gigerenzer revisits the framing of the debate, the interpretation of heuristics, and the role of benchmark choice in how rationality findings are received.

That matters in the vault because not every important source needs to add a new mechanism. Some sources help by clarifying how existing mechanism disputes should be interpreted. [[Gigerenzer 2024]] belongs in that second category. It is therefore best used to deepen [[Rationality wars]], [[Ecological rationality]], and the policy-facing pages that depend on rationality standards.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Gigerenzer 2024]] matters because it turns an abstract theoretical conflict into a live dispute about standards, institutions, and intellectual framing. It matters for economics because benchmark choice shapes whether heuristics appear as failures or as adaptive tools. It matters for philosophy because the article foregrounds normativity, pluralism, and the risk of treating one evaluative framework as universally authoritative. It matters for politics because many behavioral-policy arguments depend on contestable judgments about what counts as rational judgment in the first place.

The paper is especially helpful as a teaching source because it gives students a sense of why the debate became so influential. It shows that the rationality wars are not only a technical disagreement about experiments. They are also a disagreement about who gets to define rationality for the social sciences.
