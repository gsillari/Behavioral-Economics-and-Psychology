# Kuran and Sunstein 1999: Availability cascades and risk regulation
## Summary

[[Kuran and Sunstein 1999]] argues that public concern about risk can escalate through self-reinforcing social and informational processes called [[Availability cascades]]. In the current vault, the paper matters because it extends the [[Availability heuristic]] from individual judgment to collective attention, moral pressure, media amplification, and legal response.

The paper is especially useful because it turns a cognitive mechanism into an institutional one. What is cognitively salient to individuals can become politically dominant through social transmission, reputational incentives, and legal mobilization.

## Key claims

The first claim is that public attention can be governed by cascade dynamics rather than by objective hazard frequency alone. Vivid incidents and alarming narratives can spread through informational and reputational channels.

The second claim is that these cascades can lead to distorted regulation. Policy may become highly responsive to salient risks while neglecting more statistically important but less dramatic harms.

The third claim is that the legal and political problem is not only bad individual judgment. It is the social aggregation of biased attention into institutional response.

## Why this source matters in the vault

This source belongs squarely in the risk-perception and policy branch. [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] gives the classic individual-level availability mechanism. [[Finucane et al. 2000]] and [[Gigerenzer 2004]] move that branch toward public risk perception. [[Kuran and Sunstein 1999]] goes one step further by showing how salience can scale into regulation and public controversy.

That makes it especially valuable for PPE students. It links cognitive psychology to media dynamics, collective behavior, and legal institutions rather than stopping at laboratory inference.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows how a judgment bias can become a political phenomenon. It matters for economics because regulatory attention is a scarce resource that can be misallocated by salience. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about public reason, moral panic, and the legitimacy of regulation under distorted attention. It matters for politics because democratic institutions must often respond to risks whose visibility is socially produced rather than proportionate to underlying danger.
