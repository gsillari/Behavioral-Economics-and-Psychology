# Van Huyck et al. 1990: Tacit coordination games, strategic uncertainty, and coordination failure

## Summary

[[Van Huyck et al. 1990]] is a central experimental source for [[Coordination games]]. Its point is deliberately sharp: even when players have aligned interests, complete information, common knowledge of the payoff table, and no incentive to defect from the efficient outcome once it is expected, tacit coordination can still fail.

The experimental game is a minimum-effort coordination game. Each subject chooses an integer from 1 to 7. Payoffs rise with the group minimum but fall with one's own effort. The all-7 outcome is payoff-dominant and Pareto-best; the all-1 outcome is secure because choosing 1 protects a player against the worst possible group minimum. All equal-action profiles are strict Nash equilibria, so ordinary equilibrium analysis does not select a unique outcome.

The first-period data already undermine simple deductive equilibrium selection. Only 31 percent of subjects chose the payoff-dominant action 7, only 2 percent chose the secure action 1, and the actual minimum was never above 4. Subjects' predictions were dispersed and mostly anticipated disequilibrium rather than a shared equilibrium. In repeated group play, the striking result is convergence toward the secure but inefficient outcome: by period 10, 72 percent of subjects chose 1, and all seven baseline experiments had a minimum of 1.

The paper therefore gives the wiki an experimental bridge from [[Schelling 1960]] and [[Lewis 1969]] to laboratory game theory. Schelling and Lewis show why focal points, common knowledge, and convention matter for coordination. Van Huyck, Battalio, and Beil show that when no focal or conventional equilibrium is available, strategic uncertainty can push groups toward safety rather than efficiency.

## Main contribution

The main contribution is the distinction between two kinds of coordination failure.

One failure is disequilibrium: players fail to forecast the minimum correctly, so individual choices are not mutual best responses.

The deeper failure is inefficient equilibrium selection: players may eventually coordinate, but on a Pareto-dominated equilibrium. In the baseline treatment, repeated experience does not solve the problem by discovering the payoff-dominant equilibrium. It teaches many subjects that high effort is risky under the minimum rule, and the group converges toward the lowest secure outcome.

This matters because the game has no prisoner's-dilemma-style conflict and no hidden information. The failure is produced by strategic uncertainty: uncertainty about which equilibrium others will try to implement, and about whether enough others will risk trying for the efficient outcome.

## Experimental variations

The paper uses several treatments to separate mechanisms.

In treatment B, the cost of choosing a high number is removed, so 7 becomes a dominant strategy. Most subjects then move toward 7, showing that they understand the payoff structure when the coordination risk is removed.

When the original payoff table is restored in treatment A', however, groups again move toward the secure low outcome. Prior experience with the dominant-strategy version does not make the high-effort equilibrium self-sustaining once strategic uncertainty returns.

The group-size treatment is especially important. With fixed pairs, subjects often converge to the payoff-dominant equilibrium. With larger groups, the minimum rule magnifies even small doubts about others' choices, making the secure action attractive. This gives a concrete experimental mechanism for why coordination problems become harder as the number of required co-coordinators increases.

The monitoring treatment, where the whole distribution of actions is revealed, does not solve the collective problem. It may help subjects best-respond sooner, but it does not prevent convergence to the secure low outcome. More information about the distribution is not enough when strategic uncertainty still makes high effort fragile.

## What it adds to the wiki

For [[Coordination games]], this source adds the empirical counterpart to the Schelling-Lewis-Skyrms branch. Coordination is not merely a matter of finding a salient focal point or stabilizing a convention. In some games, no salient solution is available, and repetition can stabilize an inefficient equilibrium.

For [[Experimental games]], it widens the methodological map. Experimental games are not only tools for measuring fairness, trust, reciprocity, or punishment. They can also test equilibrium selection, strategic uncertainty, and the conditions under which rationally defensible equilibria are behaviorally plausible.

For [[Common knowledge]], the paper is a useful limit case. Common knowledge of the rules and payoffs does not by itself generate common expectations about which equilibrium will be played. The epistemic condition helps sustain a convention once a convention exists, but it does not automatically solve equilibrium selection.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Van Huyck et al. 1990]] matters because it gives a disciplined laboratory model of institutional failure without bad preferences, selfish defection, or informational asymmetry. Groups can fail because each person reasonably fears that others will not coordinate on the efficient action.

That makes the paper useful for thinking about economic development, organizational routines, policy implementation, political mobilization, and social conventions. The problem is not always incentives. Sometimes the hard part is making the efficient equilibrium credible enough that people are willing to act as if others will act too.
