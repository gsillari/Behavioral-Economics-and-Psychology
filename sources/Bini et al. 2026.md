# Bini et al. 2026: Behavioral economics of AI
## Summary

[[Bini et al. 2026]] studies whether large language models exhibit systematic behavioral biases in economic and financial decisions and whether those biases can be reduced. The paper, titled "Behavioral Economics of AI: LLM Biases and Corrections," analyzes prominent model families including ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Llama across versions and scales. In this vault, it anchors the new AI branch by treating LLMs as decision-making agents that can be benchmarked with tools from [[Behavioral Economics]], [[Heuristics and Biases]], and [[Expected utility theory]].

## Key claims

The paper claims that LLM behavior is not random in economic and financial tasks. Instead it exhibits systematic patterns. In preference-based tasks, more advanced or larger models become more human-like, which in the paper's framing often means less consistent with [[Expected utility theory]]. In belief-based tasks, advanced large-scale models often generate more rational responses. The paper also claims that behavioral patterns differ across model families and that prompting models to make rational decisions can reduce biases.

## Evidence and methods

The paper adapts a broad set of experiments from cognitive psychology and experimental economics to multiple LLM families. According to the abstract and introduction, the authors compare older and newer versions within model families and also compare larger and smaller scale variants. They study both cognitive-psychology tasks designed to reveal human biases and financial decision tasks involving forecasting and investment judgments. The paper reports cross-family heterogeneity as well as differences between preference-based and belief-based responses.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper shows that behavioral economics is no longer only about human agents. It matters for economics because LLMs are starting to mediate forecasting, investment, and advice, for politics because public and private institutions may rely on these systems in high-stakes settings, and for philosophy because it forces the question of what it means to call a nonhuman system biased, rational, or corrigible. It helps position behavioral economics as part of a broader inquiry into decision-making agents and the governance of their errors.

## Open questions

One open question is whether LLM behavioral biases should be interpreted as imitation of human response patterns, artifacts of training data, or emerging decision heuristics of their own. Another is how stable the paper's findings will remain across rapidly changing model generations after January 2026. A third is whether prompt-based corrections are robust enough for deployment or merely temporary surface fixes.
