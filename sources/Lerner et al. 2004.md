# Lerner et al. 2004: Heart strings and purse strings
## Summary

[[Lerner et al. 2004]] studies how incidental emotions carry over into economic valuation. In the current vault, its most important contribution is to show that the [[Endowment effect]] is not fixed across emotional states: disgust can eliminate it, and sadness can even reverse it.

This matters because the paper links the valuation branch to the emotions branch much more directly than the current wiki previously did. Instead of treating emotion as a background influence on choice in general, the source shows that specific emotions can reshape buying, selling, and choice prices for the same object.

## Key claims

The first claim is that incidental emotions influence economic decisions even when those emotions are normatively unrelated to the valuation task itself.

The second claim is that different negative emotions do not simply push behavior in one shared direction. Disgust and sadness have different carryover effects on valuation.

The third claim is that these emotion-specific effects can alter one of the best-known valuation patterns in behavioral economics. In the current vault, this is especially important because it shows that the [[Endowment effect]] is not only about stable reference points or ownership, but also about transient emotional state.

## Emotions and valuation

This source is valuable because it prevents the valuation branch from becoming too static. [[Knetsch and Sinden 1984]] and [[Kahneman et al. 1990]] show stable-looking disparities in willingness to pay and willingness to accept. [[Lerner et al. 2004]] adds that those disparities can themselves shift with incidental emotion.

That matters conceptually because it complicates simple welfare readings. If valuation can be moved by irrelevant sadness or disgust, then the interpretation of buying and selling prices becomes more fragile. In the current vault, that links the paper not only to [[Endowment effect]] but also to [[Constructed preferences]] and the broader question of how settled preferences really are.

## Why it matters for the emotions branch

The paper should also deepen [[Emotions in decision-making]]. That page had been mainly field-oriented and somewhat general. This source adds a sharper experimental demonstration that emotions of the same valence can produce different economic effects, which is stronger and more specific than a generic “mood matters” claim.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this paper matters because it shows how valuation can be shaped by contingent emotional states rather than by stable preference alone. It matters for economics because it complicates the interpretation of expressed value. It matters for philosophy because it raises hard questions about which valuations should count as authoritative when emotion is transient and irrelevant to the choice object. It matters for politics and policy because emotionally charged environments may systematically distort willingness to buy, sell, or accept compensation.

## Links into the wiki

The main target pages are [[Emotions in decision-making]] and [[Endowment effect]]. The source also belongs near [[Loss aversion]] and [[Constructed preferences]], because it shows that valuation anomalies need not come only from stable reference dependence. At the topic level, it slightly strengthens both [[Choice, Preference, and Decision Under Risk]] and [[Time, Attention, and Choice Architecture]], though it belongs primarily on the emotions side.

## Open questions

How stable are these emotion-specific valuation effects outside laboratory settings? Should the vault eventually distinguish incidental emotions from integral emotions more clearly? And how should policy interpret valuations produced under transient emotional carryover?

## Bibliographic reference
