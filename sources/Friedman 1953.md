# Friedman 1953: Essays in positive economics

## Summary

[[Friedman 1953]] is the top-level work note for *Essays in Positive Economics*. In the current vault, the book matters mainly as a methodological benchmark rather than as part of behavioral economics proper. Its importance lies in the way it clarifies the ambition of economics as a predictive positive science and provides a classic defense of judging theories by predictive performance rather than by the realism of their assumptions.

For the current wiki, the central reusable unit is the essay [[Essays in Positive Economics - Essay - The Methodology of Positive Economics]]. That essay matters because later behavioral economics can be read partly as a challenge to methodological complacency about unrealistic assumptions, and partly as a redirection of what good prediction and good explanation should look like in economics. [[Berg and Gigerenzer 2010]] is especially useful here because it argues that this methodological break was incomplete: much behavioral economics still keeps Friedman's `as-if` logic while presenting itself as psychologically richer and more realistic.

## Main claims

At the work level, the book's importance is that it defends economics as a positive science aimed at prediction and explanatory power rather than direct moral guidance. It also helps fix the background against which later methodological disputes in economics unfold.

For the current vault, that means the work belongs mainly near pages on method, benchmark choice, and the status of rational-choice modeling. It should function as a foil and reference point, not as the center of the wiki.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this work matters because it is one of the clearest twentieth-century statements of how economics often understands itself as a science. It matters for economics because it defends predictive success as the central standard for theory assessment. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about explanation, realism, idealization, and the relation between positive and normative claims. It matters for politics because policy advice often depends on whether economics is treated mainly as a predictive tool or as a more behaviorally realistic account of human choice.
