# Guth et al. 1982: An experimental analysis of ultimatum bargaining

## Summary

[[Guth et al. 1982]] is the original experimental source for the [[Ultimatum game]]. It matters because it makes a very simple game-theoretic prediction empirically visible and then shows that the prediction is descriptively fragile. In the canonical two-player version, the proposer makes a take-it-or-leave-it division and the responder either accepts or rejects. Backward induction plus narrow material self-interest predicts that the proposer should offer the smallest positive amount and the responder should accept it.

The experimental result is important not because subjects are confused by the rules, but because the game is simple enough to make that explanation weak. Proposers often avoid the subgame-perfect selfish prediction, and responders are willing to reject low offers. The paper therefore gives the vault its first experimental-game anchor for the idea that [[Fairness]] and social meaning can enter even one-shot strategic interaction.

## What it adds to the wiki

This source should anchor [[Ultimatum game]] as a benchmark experiment rather than as a loose example. It also strengthens [[Game theory]] by showing why behavioral economics does not merely add psychological color to strategic choice: it pressures the payoff interpretation used by standard solution concepts. If agents care about insult, fairness, or reciprocity, then the material-payoff game is not yet the full behavioral game.

The paper also helps distinguish the social-preference branch from the judgment-and-bias branch. The failure here is not primarily a mistaken probability judgment. It is a problem about what players value and how they interpret the act of making or rejecting an offer.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Guth et al. 1982]] matters because it makes strategic rationality, fairness, and institutional design meet in the simplest possible bargaining setting. It matters for economics because it challenges the descriptive reach of narrow self-interest and backward induction. It matters for philosophy because it raises the question whether refusing an unfair offer is irrational, expressive, punitive, or norm-enforcing. It matters for politics and policy because bargaining power and perceived fairness shape compliance, conflict, and legitimacy.
