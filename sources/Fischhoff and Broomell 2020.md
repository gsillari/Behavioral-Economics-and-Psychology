# Fischhoff and Broomell 2020: Judgment and decision making
## Summary

[[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]] is a high-level review of judgment and decision making that deserves weight in the current vault because it organizes the mature field without flattening it. The paper frames decision science as the interaction of three kinds of work: analysis of the decisions people face, description of their natural responses, and interventions designed to help them do better. It then reviews the three core elements of decision making itself: judgment, preference, and choice.

In the current wiki, that makes the paper less a source for one narrow concept than a grounding review across several existing ones. It helps explain why belief, preference, and decision-process pages belong together without collapsing into one undifferentiated catalog of biases. It also helps the vault connect basic research to applications in health, public policy, intelligence analysis, and risk management.

## Key claims

The first claim is that judgment and decision making works best when analytical and behavioral research are coupled rather than separated. Researchers need task analysis in order to know what would count as good performance, but they also need realistic studies of how people actually construe and respond to those tasks.

The second claim is that judgment research turns on both accuracy and consistency. People may be inaccurate, but they may also think about uncertainty in ways that do not fit the formal standards researchers bring to the task. That matters because the review does not treat bias as a simple matter of people failing obvious norms. It also treats task interpretation and benchmark choice as part of the problem.

The third claim is that preference research is structurally different from judgment research. There is no accuracy criterion for what people should want, but there are still consistency standards drawn from utility theory. This matters because the review explicitly links preference anomalies to the difficulty of eliciting stable preferences in abstract, unfamiliar, or morally charged tasks, and therefore to the later [[Constructed preferences]] branch in the vault.

The fourth claim is that choice research depends heavily on modeling and on sensitivity to task structure. The review discusses cumulative prospect theory, decision by sampling, description-versus-experience effects, repeated decisions, and dynamic tasks in order to show that choice is not just a matter of stating a preference. It is also a matter of how judgment and preference are combined under specific informational and temporal conditions.

The fifth claim is that behavioral heterogeneity and application are central, not peripheral. Individual differences, life-span changes, calibration training, diagnostic decisions, risk analysis, and policy communication are all treated as part of the field's proper scope. That makes the paper especially useful for a PPE-oriented wiki that wants to connect theory to institutions.

## Evidence and methods

This is a synthetic review article rather than a new experimental paper. Its method is to reconstruct the field's intellectual foundations, then organize more recent research around the elements of decision making and the settings in which those elements matter.

The paper is especially useful because it does not present a single master theory. Instead, it gives the vault a disciplined architecture:

- judgment research studies prediction, inference, calibration, and consistency
- preference research studies utility, elicitation, and the instability or construction of what people want
- choice research studies how judgments and preferences are combined under task-specific conditions
- intervention research studies how decision aids, training, feedback, and institutional design can improve decisions

That architecture is a good fit for the current wiki because many of the relevant concept pages already exist. The value of this source is therefore mainly integrative and grounding.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, [[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]] matters because it shows that behavioral economics is not just a sequence of famous demonstrations. It matters for economics because it clarifies how judgment, preference, and choice each put pressure on simplified models of agency. It matters for philosophy because it keeps questions of accuracy, consistency, welfare, and benchmark choice in view at the same time. It matters for politics and public policy because it treats interventions, expert judgment, risk communication, and institutional decision support as intrinsic parts of the field rather than as downstream add-ons.

Pedagogically, the paper is valuable because it helps students see one continuous arc from laboratory tasks to real institutions. In the current vault, it should therefore strengthen the spine pages more than it should generate many new standalone concepts.

## Links into the wiki

The paper should ground [[Nonstandard beliefs]] by clarifying why belief research is organized around accuracy and consistency standards and by showing that alternative inferential standards and task construal matter. It should ground [[Preference]] by clarifying why preference anomalies often expose not only odd tastes but also hard measurement problems and unstable elicitation contexts. It should ground [[Nonstandard decision-making]] by showing how modeling, repeated decisions, and intervention design fit around the core choice problem rather than around isolated biases.

It also belongs in [[Judgment, Belief, and Inference]] and [[Scope and Method of Behavioral Economics]] because it gives the field a mature methodological self-description. In the policy branch, it should support [[Behavioral public policy]] and [[Risk perception]] by showing that applied decision science includes decision aids, expert elicitation, risk analysis, and communication as well as nudges or defaults. At the synthesis level, it complements [[Camerer and Loewenstein 2004]]: Camerer and Loewenstein map behavioral economics as a research program, while Fischhoff and Broomell map judgment and decision making as a mature analytical-behavioral field.

## Open questions

How far should the vault treat decision science as the broader frame inside which behavioral economics occupies one important region? When do violations of formal standards reveal deep irrationality, and when do they reveal badly matched tasks or unrealistic elicitation demands? And how should interventions be evaluated when they improve outcomes but leave underlying decision competence unchanged?
