# Evolutionary psychology

## Core idea

[[Evolutionary psychology]] is a research program that explains features of human cognition and behavior in terms of evolved psychological adaptations. In the current vault, the concept now has two roles. [[Samuels et al. 2002]] places it inside the broader dispute over how rational human beings really are. [[Cosmides and Tooby 2000]] gives it a concrete reasoning case: performance on the [[Wason selection task]] changes when the task is framed as social exchange and cheater detection.

## Key distinctions

In the present cluster, evolutionary psychology is important not as a general theory of mind but as a position in the rationality wars. It contrasts with the [[Heuristics and Biases]] tradition by arguing that some findings taken to show irrationality may instead reflect the mismatch between laboratory task formats and the forms of information-processing for which human cognition evolved. That makes it related to, but not identical with, later ideas about ecological fit.

The main distinction is between domain-general and domain-specific accounts of reasoning. A domain-general account treats reasoning as the operation of content-free procedures such as logic, Bayesian updating, or general-purpose heuristics. Cosmides and Tooby argue that at least some reasoning is handled by specialized mechanisms whose design reflects recurrent adaptive problems, such as social exchange. This is why the page belongs near [[Fast and slow thinking]] but should not be collapsed into it. Evolutionary psychology asks what a reasoning mechanism is for; dual-process theory asks how intuitive and reflective processing are organized.

## Evidence and debate

[[Cosmides and Tooby 2000]] is now the strongest concrete source. It argues that social exchange created selection pressures for specialized reasoning machinery able to detect cheaters: agents who take benefits without satisfying requirements. The [[Wason selection task]] becomes their experimental probe. Abstract conditional rules usually produce poor performance, but social-contract versions often produce much higher performance, including in unfamiliar cultural scenarios. Cosmides and Tooby interpret this as evidence for domain-specific social-contract algorithms rather than mere familiarity or clearer general reasoning.

[[Samuels et al. 2002]] then gives the meta-level caution. The chapter argues that the dispute between evolutionary psychology and heuristics-and-biases is often overstated because the two sides rely on different standards of assessment. In the current vault, this makes evolutionary psychology less a settled rival doctrine and more a lens through which to reinterpret what bias findings do and do not establish.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because judgments about human rationality affect how strongly we think institutions should intervene, correct, or redesign choice environments. If some apparently irrational behavior reflects adaptive design under ecologically appropriate representations, then the policy implications of behavioral findings become less straightforward. The Wason/Cosmides-Tooby sequence is useful pedagogically because it shows this in miniature: the same task can look like a failure of logic, a confirmation bias, or a specialized competence depending on how the problem is represented.

## Open questions

How much of the conflict with heuristics-and-biases is really about standards of assessment, and how much is about substantive disagreement over cognitive architecture? How much of the social-contract effect should be attributed to cheater-detection adaptations rather than pragmatic reasoning schemas? Which later sources should be used to connect this debate more directly to ecological rationality?
