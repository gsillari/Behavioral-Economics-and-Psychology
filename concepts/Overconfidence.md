# Overconfidence

## Core idea

[[Overconfidence]] is the tendency to overestimate one's own ability, the precision of one's information, or the likelihood of favorable outcomes. In the current vault, the branch now has two complementary anchors: [[Lichtenstein et al. 1982 - Calibration of Probabilities (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 22)]] for the classic literature on confidence calibration, and [[DellaVigna 2009]] for later field evidence from self-control, corporate decisions, employee stock options, and financial trading.

## Key distinctions

The concept differs from [[Projection bias]], where people mispredict future tastes, and from [[Law of small numbers]], where they misread short sequences of evidence. Overconfidence is specifically a belief about one's own competence, information quality, or control. The calibration literature matters here because it shows that overconfidence can also be measured as a mismatch between stated confidence and realized truth frequencies, not only as self-enhancing self-assessment. [[Langer 1982 - The Illusion of Control (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 16)]] is useful here because it marks one important nearby form of distorted confidence: people may not only overestimate how well they will do, but also misperceive whether the outcome is controllable at all. DellaVigna also notes that naivete about future self-control can be understood as one form of overconfidence, because it involves overestimating one's future discipline.

## Evidence and debate

[[Lichtenstein et al. 1982 - Calibration of Probabilities (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 22)]] is important because it gives the concept a strong earlier benchmark in the study of confidence judgments. It reviews how people attach subjective probabilities to propositions and how those judgments compare with subsequent truth frequencies. [[Langer 1982 - The Illusion of Control (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 16)]] adds a related but distinct pattern: people may also display excessive confidence by acting as though chance events were partly governed by personal skill or effort. [[DellaVigna 2009]] then highlights several field domains in which overconfidence matters. Consumers can be overconfident about future self-control, CEOs about their managerial ability, and investors about the precision of their private information. These patterns help explain phenomena such as value-destroying mergers, option-exercise decisions, excess trading, and some asset-pricing anomalies. The debate centers on measurement: because overconfidence is latent, researchers must infer it from behavior that may also admit other explanations.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because overconfidence can produce costly errors in settings where people make infrequent, high-stakes decisions. It is important for financial regulation, corporate governance, retirement savings, and consumer protection. The neighboring branch of [[Illusion of control]] matters for similar reasons because people may take on risk not only by overestimating their skill, but also by overestimating whether skill is relevant in the first place. It also matters for policy design because some interventions that look paternalistic may instead be responses to predictable self-misprediction.

## Open questions

How should overconfidence be distinguished empirically from private information or strategic signaling? When does feedback reduce overconfidence, and when does it reinforce it? Which institutional settings are most exposed to overconfidence among powerful actors rather than ordinary consumers?
