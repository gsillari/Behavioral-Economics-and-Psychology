# Prospect Theory

## Core idea

[[Prospect Theory]] is the central behavioral theory of choice under risk in the current vault. Its core claim is that people do not normally evaluate risky options as if they had stable utilities over final states. Instead, they evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point, distinguish sharply between gains and losses, and respond to risk differently depending on how outcomes are coded.

That is why the page matters so much. [[Prospect Theory]] is the point where behavioral economics stops being only a collection of anomalies and becomes a compact positive model of risky choice.

## Key distinctions

Three distinctions keep the concept clear.

First, [[Prospect Theory]] is the main behavioral alternative in the vault to [[Expected utility theory]]. Expected utility models evaluate risky options through utilities over final states. Prospect theory instead evaluates gains and losses relative to a benchmark.

Second, the theory is not identical to any one of its famous ingredients. [[Reference dependence]], [[Loss aversion]], and [[Framing effects]] are all central nearby pages, but prospect theory is the architecture that connects them.

Third, the theory is not the same thing as the broader [[Heuristics and Biases]] program. That earlier program is about judgment under uncertainty in general. Prospect theory is narrower and more model-like: it is specifically about risky choice.

## The problem the theory solves

The theory begins from a pressure point in classical decision theory. [[Allais 1953]] and the [[Allais paradox]] show that actual risky choice often violates the predictions of [[Expected utility theory]]. In the current vault, that anomaly matters because it forces a question: should the benchmark be defended as merely normative, adjusted from within, or replaced by a more psychologically realistic model?

[[Prospect Theory]] is Kahneman and Tversky's most influential answer to that question. It preserves the ambition to model risky choice systematically, but changes the object being modeled. The key move is away from final states and toward psychologically coded gains and losses.

## How the theory works

The cleanest way to read the theory is as a three-part structure.

### 1. Reference points

Outcomes are evaluated relative to a benchmark rather than in absolute terms. That is why [[Reference dependence]] is indispensable. The same objective outcome can look like a gain or a loss depending on whether the relevant baseline is the status quo, an expectation, an entitlement, or a recent outcome.

### 2. Value over gains and losses

The second element is the value function. It is usually concave for gains, convex for losses, and steeper in the loss domain. That asymmetry is what gives the theory its distinctive motivational force. In the current vault, [[Loss aversion]] is the main page that develops this part of the structure. The nearby page [[Risk attitudes]] is useful here because it clarifies what prospect theory is and is not changing. Expected-utility theory already has a formal way to represent risk aversion through utility curvature. Prospect theory's novelty is that curvature is now benchmark-relative and combined with gain-loss coding and decision weights.

### 3. Decision weights

The third element is the treatment of risk itself. Prospect theory does not assume that objective probabilities translate directly into psychological impact. Instead, choices depend on decision weights, which helps explain certainty effects and the unstable relation between formal probability and actual response.

That point is easy to understate. Decision weights are not just another name for subjective probabilities. In the prospect-theory tradition they are descriptive impact weights: they capture how much a possibility enters into choice, not simply what probability the agent reports for that possibility. That is why small probabilities can be overweighted in some settings and moderate-to-high probabilities can be underweighted without the theory claiming that people literally believe the numbers themselves are different.

Together, these three elements explain the classic reflection pattern: people often prefer certainty in gains but can become more risk seeking in losses.

## From 1979 to cumulative prospect theory

This distinction needs to be explicit because the original paper and the mature theory are not the same thing.

[[Kahneman and Tversky 1979]] is the breakthrough paper. It supplies the basic architecture of reference points, gain-loss coding, asymmetric valuation, and decision weights. But the original formulation was limited. It was built mainly for simple risky prospects with a small number of outcomes, and its separable weighting scheme could generate problems such as violations of stochastic dominance.

For an advanced undergraduate, stochastic dominance is the key technical phrase to unpack here. A prospect stochastically dominates another when it gives outcomes that are at least as good in every relevant respect and strictly better in some cases, so choosing the dominated option looks normatively hard to defend. The trouble with the 1979 version is that its weighting scheme can, in sufficiently complex cases, assign choice values in a way that breaks that intuitive ordering.

[[Tversky and Kahneman 1992]] is the major repair and extension. Cumulative prospect theory replaces the original weighting structure with cumulative, rank-dependent weighting over ordered outcomes. In the current vault, that matters because it makes the theory more general, better behaved, and easier to use as the mature version of the behavioral model rather than only as an initial breakthrough.

So the theory should be taught here as a two-stage development:
- [[Kahneman and Tversky 1979]] as the foundational break with expected utility
- [[Tversky and Kahneman 1992]] as the main formal repair and extension

## Main branches in the vault

### 1. Framing and risky choice

The most direct pedagogical companion is [[Tversky and Kahneman 1981 (Framing)]]. It is the clearest short presentation in the vault of how gain-loss coding changes risk attitudes. In teaching terms, it is often the best entry point into the theory’s basic logic.

### 2. Valuation and entitlement

Prospect theory does not stay confined to lottery problems. In the valuation branch, [[Reference dependence]] and [[Loss aversion]] connect the theory to [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]], the [[Endowment effect]], and later valuation anomalies. This is the branch where the theory most directly pressures standard welfare and valuation analysis.

### 3. Consumer behavior and sunk costs

[[Thaler 1980]] is one of the earliest economics-facing uptake points in the vault. It shows how prospect-theoretic ideas can be used to rethink consumer behavior rather than only laboratory choice under risk. [[Arkes and Blumer 1985]] then gives a neighboring anomaly through the [[Sunk cost effect]], which fits naturally with the theory’s concern for realized losses and reluctance to accept them.

### 4. Finance

The strongest finance extension is [[Benartzi and Thaler 1995]]. Through [[Myopic loss aversion]], the theory helps explain the [[Equity premium puzzle]] by combining prospect-theoretic loss sensitivity with frequent evaluation horizons. This is one of the clearest cases where the theory generates a serious economics application rather than just a psychological observation.

### 5. Politics and political economy

The vault now also gives the theory a PPE-facing political branch. [[Mercer 2005]] reviews its uptake in political science, [[Levy 1997]] tests its use in international relations, and [[Schnellenbach and Schubert 2015]] places it inside [[Behavioral political economy]]. That branch matters because it shows that prospect theory is not only a theory of consumer or investor behavior. It is also a theory of how threatened losses, declining positions, and coded gains shape political action.

### 6. Field relevance

[[DellaVigna 2009]] matters because it helps show that prospect-theoretic ideas travel beyond textbook experiments. In the current vault, it is one of the main reasons the theory connects to real institutional settings rather than remaining a classroom model of risky choice alone.

### 7. Intertemporal choice

The current vault now also treats [[Loewenstein and Prelec 2007 - Anomalies in Intertemporal Choice - Evidence and an Interpretation (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 15)]] as an important outward extension. It argues that many anomalies in choice over time can be organized by importing prospect-theory-style ideas such as reference dependence and gain-loss valuation into intertemporal choice. That matters because it shows how prospect theory became not only a model of risky choice, but a template for reconstructing other branches of behavior.

## Evidence and debate

The core evidence comes from the classic choice problems in [[Kahneman and Tversky 1979]] and the especially clear framing demonstrations in [[Tversky and Kahneman 1981 (Framing)]]. In the current vault, those are still the main teaching sources for the theory itself.

The main debates are about interpretation and scope.

One debate concerns the status of decision weights. Are they best read as transformed probabilities, as attention-like weights, or as a more general descriptive device?

Another concerns reference points. How stable are they outside experimental tasks, and how should we identify them in real institutions?

A third concerns the relation between the 1979 and 1992 versions. How much of the original theory is foundational intuition, and how much should now be taught only through cumulative prospect theory?

Finally, there is the larger rationality question. Should prospect-theory departures from expected utility be interpreted as evidence of irrationality, or as evidence that the classical benchmark is descriptively too thin for finite human choosers?

## Practical or policy relevance

The theory matters because institutions do not present options as bare final states. They present them as gains, losses, defaults, reforms, protections, cuts, risks, and opportunities. That means prospect theory has direct implications for communication, consumer protection, benefit design, taxation, retirement saving, and policy reform more broadly.

Its PPE value is especially strong because it also scales upward. The same gain-loss logic can help explain why politically beneficial reforms generate backlash, why declining positions may induce risk acceptance, and why formally equivalent options are not always psychologically equivalent to citizens, leaders, or voters.

## Open questions

How stable are reference points across domains and institutions? Which parts of prospect theory are best treated as core theory and which as surrounding interpretation? And how far can the theory travel from individual risky choice into organizational and political settings without becoming too loose to test?
