# Transaction costs

## Core idea

[[Transaction costs]] are the costs of identifying parties, gathering information, bargaining, monitoring agreements, and enforcing them. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Coase 1960]], where transaction costs explain why legal rules and institutional arrangements matter for resolving social conflicts.

## Key distinctions

Transaction costs are not simply production costs. They are the frictions involved in reaching and sustaining agreements. In the current vault, they also matter because they separate the idealized world of the [[Coase theorem]] from the real institutional world that policy must address. Once these costs are nontrivial, law and regulation can no longer be treated as irrelevant.

## Evidence and debate

[[Coase 1960]] makes transaction costs the hinge concept between abstract efficiency arguments and real institutional analysis. The main debate is how broadly the term should be understood and how much explanatory work it can do. In a behavioral context, the concept is especially useful because many cognitive and motivational frictions look like additional transaction costs or barriers to effective bargaining.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many policies implicitly assume that citizens, firms, and affected parties can bargain cheaply when conflicts arise. If transaction costs are high, private ordering may fail even when a textbook benchmark says bargaining should solve the problem. That makes transaction costs central to law, regulation, consumer protection, environmental policy, and behavioral interventions alike.
