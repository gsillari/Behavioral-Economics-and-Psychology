# Risk perception

## Core idea

[[Risk perception]] refers to how people judge the seriousness, acceptability, and meaning of hazards, not only their formal probability and expected harm. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Slovic 1987]]. The key lesson is that public judgments of risk are structured by dimensions such as dread, catastrophic potential, controllability, familiarity, and unknownness. That makes risk perception a broader and more foundational concept than any single bias page in the branch.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Availability heuristic]], which explains some risk judgments through ease of recall and salience. It differs from [[Affect heuristic]], which emphasizes global positive or negative feeling as a shortcut for judging risks and benefits. It differs from [[Risk as feelings]], which is narrower and more process-oriented: it focuses on emotional reactions at the moment of risky choice rather than the full structure of hazard judgment. It also differs from [[Dread risk]], which names a particularly potent class of feared hazards. Risk perception is the umbrella concept that helps organize these more specific mechanisms. It is also not identical to objective risk assessment. The point of the page is precisely that perceived risk and expert risk are often structured differently.

## Evidence and debate

[[Slovic 1987]] is foundational because it synthesizes the psychometric approach to risk. The source argues that laypeople do not simply misunderstand probability. They often attend to socially and morally salient dimensions that expert analysis treats differently or downplays. [[Slovic et al. 1982 - Facts versus Fears (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 33)]] usefully deepens that branch inside the blue-book tradition itself by framing perceived risk as a conflict between quantitative assessment and the qualitative features that matter to the public. In the current vault, this makes the page a useful hub for several later branches. [[Availability heuristic]] and [[Probability neglect]] help explain how some hazards become cognitively dominant. [[Affect heuristic]] helps explain why risks and benefits can be read off from a single evaluative impression. [[Dread risk]] captures the especially powerful response to catastrophic hazards. [[Disaster insurance]] then connects the branch to preparedness and institutional response.

[[Loewenstein et al. 2007 - Risk as Feelings (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 19)]] adds an important complementary layer. The psychometric tradition explains why hazards acquire different qualitative profiles in public judgment; the risk-as-feelings approach explains how those emotional reactions can directly shape choice at the moment of decision. That makes the current vault's risk branch more complete. It now has one line of work on how people understand hazards and another on how fear, vividness, and arousal steer risky behavior.

[[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]] adds a complementary grounding layer. It shows how risk perception sits inside the broader practice of cost, risk, and benefit analysis, where judgment, value assumptions, and communication all matter. The review is especially useful because it does not treat dread and uncertainty as automatically irrational. It explicitly notes that these qualitative dimensions may reflect concerns that public policy should take seriously. Read together, [[Slovic et al. 1982 - Facts versus Fears (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 33)]], [[Slovic 1987]], [[Loewenstein et al. 2007 - Risk as Feelings (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 19)]], and [[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]] give the risk branch a clearer internal sequence: early conflict between public fears and expert fact language, mature psychometric mapping of the dimensions of risk, an affective account of risky choice itself, and later decision-science reflection on analysis and communication.

The main debate is whether these patterns should be treated as distortions from rational judgment or as a broader conception of what it means to judge risk well. For a PPE-oriented wiki, that debate is central rather than optional.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because risk regulation, crisis communication, public trust, and technological controversy all depend on perceived risk as well as measured risk. Institutions that ignore this difference often fail not only politically but substantively, because public response changes behavior, compliance, and legitimacy. [[Slovic et al. 1982 - Facts versus Fears (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 33)]] is especially useful here because it shows how easily policy can misfire when experts treat disagreement as ignorance rather than as a different structure of concern. [[Loewenstein et al. 2007 - Risk as Feelings (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 19)]] sharpens the point by showing that policy must address not only what people think about risk but also what they feel in risky situations. [[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]] then treats communication as part of risk governance rather than as an afterthought once the analysis is complete.

## Open questions

How should institutions respond when public risk perception diverges sharply from expert assessment? Which parts of perceived risk track morally relevant features that formal models neglect? And when should behavioral economics treat fear as a distortion rather than as an important signal about vulnerability and trust?
