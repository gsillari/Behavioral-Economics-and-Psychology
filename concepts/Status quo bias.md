# Status quo bias

## Core idea

[[Status quo bias]] is the tendency to stick with the current option or default more often than standard models of stable preference would predict. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Samuelson and Zeckhauser 1988]], which shows that merely placing an option in the status-quo position can substantially raise its likelihood of being chosen.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from simple habit or rational inertia because the bias claim is comparative: the current option gets extra weight because it is current, not only because it is substantively best. It also differs from [[Endowment effect]], though the two are related. The endowment effect concerns ownership-sensitive valuation, whereas status quo bias concerns persistence in choice among options, especially when one is framed as the default or existing arrangement.

## Evidence and debate

[[Samuelson and Zeckhauser 1988]] argues that people disproportionately remain with existing options in both experiments and consequential institutional choices. The paper is especially useful in the current vault because it predates the later nudge literature while already making clear why defaults matter. Debate then turns on explanation: some of the effect may reflect rational caution, switching costs, informational asymmetry, or anticipated regret, while some may reflect framing, loss aversion, or simple inertia.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because default rules are one of the most powerful instruments in [[Behavioral public policy]] and one of the most important subclasses of [[Nudge]]. Pension enrollment, organ donation, insurance, privacy settings, and public-program take-up can all be strongly shaped by status-quo effects. For PPE students, the page matters because it sits directly at the boundary between descriptive bias and institutional design.

## Open questions

When should persistence in the status quo be treated as bias rather than as sensible caution? How much of the effect comes from current entitlements, transaction costs, or anticipated regret? And how aggressive should policy be in using defaults once status-quo bias is acknowledged?
