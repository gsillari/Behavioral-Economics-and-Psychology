# Myopic loss aversion

## Core idea

[[Myopic loss aversion]] is the idea that people evaluate risky outcomes narrowly or frequently and weigh losses more heavily than equal-sized gains. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Benartzi and Thaler 1995]], which uses this combination to explain the [[Equity premium puzzle]]. [[Rabin and Thaler 2001]] broadens the concept beyond finance by using narrow evaluation and loss aversion to explain why people reject small favorable gambles while accepting aggregated portfolios of similar risks.

## Key distinctions

This concept is not just [[Loss aversion]] in finance. The specifically myopic part is essential: if investors evaluate outcomes infrequently over longer horizons, short-run losses occur less often and the force of loss aversion falls. It also differs from standard risk aversion because the mechanism depends on [[Reference dependence]], [[Mental accounting]], and the frequency or narrowness with which temporary setbacks are coded as losses.

## Evidence and debate

[[Benartzi and Thaler 1995]] introduces the concept as a behavioral-finance explanation of why equities must historically pay such a large premium to induce participation. In the current vault, the importance of the paper is that it transports prospect-theory reasoning into asset pricing.

[[Rabin and Thaler 2001]] then uses the same logic as an alternative to expected-utility explanations of small-stakes risk aversion. The key contrast is [[Calibration|calibration]]: wealth-curvature explanations imply absurd large-stakes aversion, while myopic loss aversion explains small-stakes rejection through local gain-loss coding and narrow bracketing. The main debate is whether short or narrow evaluation is psychologically realistic, institutionally induced, or too strong an assumption to carry the whole explanatory burden.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many households experience investment decisions through frequent account checking, short-run return reporting, and salient losses in retirement or savings accounts. If evaluation frequency changes risk-taking, then reporting rules, pension dashboards, default fund design, and financial communication can alter behavior without changing underlying asset payoffs.
