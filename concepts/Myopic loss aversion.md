# Myopic loss aversion

## Core idea

[[Myopic loss aversion]] is the idea that investors evaluate outcomes over short horizons and weigh losses more heavily than equal-sized gains. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Benartzi and Thaler 1995]], which uses this combination to explain the [[Equity premium puzzle]].

## Key distinctions

This concept is not just [[Loss aversion]] in finance. The specifically myopic part is essential: if investors evaluate outcomes infrequently over longer horizons, short-run losses occur less often and the force of loss aversion falls. It also differs from standard risk aversion because the mechanism depends on [[Reference dependence]] and the frequency with which temporary setbacks are coded as losses.

## Evidence and debate

[[Benartzi and Thaler 1995]] introduces the concept as a behavioral-finance explanation of why equities must historically pay such a large premium to induce participation. In the current vault, the importance of the paper is that it transports prospect-theory reasoning into asset pricing. The main debate is whether short evaluation horizons are psychologically realistic, institutionally induced, or too strong an assumption to carry the whole explanatory burden.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many households experience investment decisions through frequent account checking, short-run return reporting, and salient losses in retirement or savings accounts. If evaluation frequency changes risk-taking, then reporting rules, pension dashboards, default fund design, and financial communication can alter behavior without changing underlying asset payoffs.

## Open questions

How much of the effect comes from psychology and how much from institutional design? Are investors consistently myopic, or only in periods of volatility and stress? What kinds of policy or platform design amplify or soften myopic loss aversion?
