# Ordinal utility theory

## Core idea

[[Ordinal utility theory]] is the view that consumer choice requires only an ordering of alternatives, not a cardinal measure of psychic satisfaction. In the current vault, the main anchors are now [[Hands 2010]], [[Bruni and Sugden 2007]], and [[Moscati 2022 - History of Utility Theory (in Heilmann and Reiss 2022, ch. 2)]]. Together they matter because they reconstruct this move historically rather than treating it as a purely technical refinement. The move to ordinalism was a rejection of hedonistic and introspective cardinal utility, but it was not a simple elimination of every psychological element from consumer theory.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from the older cardinal and hedonistic utility tradition because it does not require utility numbers to measure pleasures and pains. It also differs from [[Revealed preference theory]], which tries to go further by grounding consumer theory in observable choice rather than in utility language at all.

[[Hands 2010]] and [[Bruni and Sugden 2007]] are especially useful on this distinction. They argue that the standard story gets the ordinal revolution wrong when it says psychology was simply driven out. What was attacked most directly was hedonistic introspection. Ordinalism still preserved purposive choice, preference, and other ideas that remained psychologically meaningful even if they were no longer tied to cardinal feeling.

## Why it matters here

This concept matters because it clarifies the background that later behavioral economics inherits. Many behavioral critiques take standard consumer theory as the model of a psychology-free benchmark. Hands shows that this is historically misleading. The benchmark had already been reconstructed through selective acceptance and rejection of different psychologies.

It also matters because it clarifies why [[Revealed preference theory]] was seen by some economists as a further methodological step. If ordinal utility still retained what Samuelson called vestiges of utility, then revealed preference could present itself as a more thoroughly observational and operational alternative, and therefore as more closely aligned with [[Behaviorism]]. [[Bruni and Sugden 2007]] helps place this contrast inside the wider [[Paretian turn]], where economics progressively redefined itself as a science of rational choice rather than one of psychologically grounded value theory.

## Open questions

How much psychology remained inside ordinal utility theory once hedonism was removed? Did ordinalism solve a scientific problem, a methodological problem, or both? And should the vault treat ordinal utility theory mainly as a technical benchmark, or also as a historically specific compromise about what kinds of psychology economics would tolerate?
