# Disaster insurance

## Core idea

[[Disaster insurance]] refers to the problem of how individuals, firms, and states insure against low-probability, high-loss events. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Kunreuther 1996]], which argues that preparedness and insurance demand are often behaviorally fragile rather than well-aligned with actuarial reasoning.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from ordinary insurance demand because catastrophic risks are especially vulnerable to salience, myopia, and post-disaster overreaction. It also differs from [[Dread risk]]: dread concerns the psychology of feared hazards, while disaster insurance concerns the institutional and market problem of how protection is purchased and structured before losses occur.

## Evidence and debate

[[Kunreuther 1996]] argues that people often underinsure before disasters and become more attentive afterward, creating an unstable cycle of preparedness. Debate concerns how much of this pattern reflects behavioral bias, affordability constraints, expectations of aid, or rational adaptation to uncertain risk information.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because disaster losses are one of the clearest areas where behavioral economics meets public policy, regulation, and market design. If individuals predictably underprepare for catastrophic loss, then insurance design, mandates, subsidies, and public communication become central institutional questions.
