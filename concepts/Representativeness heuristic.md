# Representativeness heuristic

## Core idea

[[Representativeness heuristic]] is the tendency to judge probability, frequency, category membership, or causal fit by similarity to a prototype rather than by formal statistical reasoning. In the current vault, it is one of the deepest mechanisms in the early [[Heuristics and Biases]] program, not just one item in a familiar trio.

That is why the page matters. Representativeness is one of the main ways intuitive plausibility comes apart from probability. It helps explain why vivid cases overpower base rates, why small samples are treated as more diagnostic than they are, and why coherent descriptions can seem more likely than formally broader alternatives.

## Key distinctions

Three distinctions keep the concept clear.

First, representativeness works by resemblance, fit, or coherence, not by explicit probability calculation. That is what separates it from [[Availability heuristic]], which depends on recall and salience, and from [[Anchoring]], which depends on a starting value or adjustment problem.

Second, the heuristic is broader than any one downstream bias. In the current vault, [[Base-rate neglect]], [[Law of small numbers]], and [[Conjunction fallacy]] are best read as different ways the same similarity-based inferential style can defeat formal reasoning.

Third, representativeness is not only an error label. The later reinterpretation in [[Kahneman and Frederick 2002]] matters because it reframes the mechanism through [[Attribute substitution]]: a hard question about probability is answered by an easier question about resemblance or narrative fit.

## How the heuristic works

The cleanest way to read the heuristic is as a substitution sequence:

1. A person is asked a hard question about probability, frequency, or category membership.
2. A vivid case or description is compared to a stereotype, prototype, or familiar pattern.
3. The felt fit between case and category is treated as if it were evidence of likelihood.
4. Statistical constraints such as base rates, sample size, or logical inclusion then lose weight.

What makes the heuristic so important is that it can operate in multiple directions. A person can judge:
- an instance by its fit with a category
- a sample by its fit with a population
- an outcome by its fit with a generating process
- a scenario by its narrative coherence

That is why the representativeness branch becomes so expansive so quickly in the original literature.

## Main error families in the vault

### 1. Base rates versus case fit

The clearest route from the general mechanism to a concrete inferential error is [[Base-rate neglect]]. In this family, background frequency information is underweighted because individuating description feels more diagnostic. In the current vault, [[Tversky and Kahneman 1982 - Evidential Impact of Base Rates (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 10)]] is especially valuable because it isolates this evidential failure directly rather than only treating it as one example among many.

The base-rate branch is now richer than simple neglect. [[Ajzen 1977]] and [[Bar-Hillel 1980]] show that base rates gain influence when they are perceived as causal, specific, or relevant to the target case. [[Fischhoff and Bar-Hillel 1984]] then shows that base rates gain influence when representativeness weakens: if a description does not clearly point to one category, people use base rates more. This makes base-rate neglect a graded competition between statistical background and case-based representativeness rather than an all-or-nothing failure.

### 2. Samples and the law of small numbers

The second major family is the [[Law of small numbers]]. Here the similarity relation is between a small sample and the larger process from which it is drawn. People expect even short runs to look representative of the broader generating structure, which creates gambler's-fallacy reasoning, overinference from streaks, and exaggerated confidence in thin evidence.

### 3. Conjunction and narrative coherence

The third family is the [[Conjunction fallacy]]. This is where representativeness becomes a direct formal violation rather than only a loose inferential tendency. In the current vault, [[Tversky and Kahneman 1981]] and especially [[Tversky and Kahneman 1983]] show how a more detailed and coherent description can feel more plausible even though it is formally less probable than one of its components.

## Development of the literature in the vault

The literature now has a clear internal arc.

[[Kahneman and Tversky 1972]] is the early focused mechanism paper. It shows that subjective probability is often judged by resemblance to a parent population or generating process.

[[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] then places representativeness inside the wider [[Heuristics and Biases]] architecture. This is the canonical source for why the heuristic became one of the program’s central mechanisms.

[[Bar-Hillel 1982 - Studies of Representativeness (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 5)]] is important because it gives the branch internal theoretical depth. Bar-Hillel helps show that representativeness can be studied as a psychologically tractable relation rather than just as a post hoc bias label.

[[Tversky and Kahneman 1982 - Evidential Impact of Base Rates (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 10)]] sharpens the evidential side of the mechanism by showing more precisely how descriptive case information crowds out background frequencies.

[[Tversky and Kahneman 1981]] and [[Tversky and Kahneman 1983]] then carry the branch into conjunction reasoning and extensional norm violation, where descriptive coherence and formal probability diverge especially sharply.

Finally, [[Kahneman and Frederick 2002]] gives the later process reinterpretation through [[Attribute substitution]], making explicit the idea that the difficult question about likelihood is answered by an easier one about resemblance.

## Evidence and debate

The concept remains central because it captures a durable pattern in intuitive judgment, but the debate is not trivial.

One question is whether the heuristic should be treated mainly as a statistical error pattern or as a broader process of intuitive classification and coherence judgment.

Another is whether some classic tasks overstate bias because of wording, pragmatics, or experimenter interpretation.

A third is how far representativeness can be operationalized cleanly. That is where the Bar-Hillel chapter is useful in the current vault: it pushes back against the idea that representativeness is too vague to be analytically useful.

In the current structure, representativeness should be read both as part of the background to [[Nonstandard beliefs]] and as a bridge between the classic heuristics tradition and later process-based interpretations of intuition.

## Practical or policy relevance

Representativeness matters for policy because citizens and institutions often judge people, cases, and risks by how typical they seem. That can distort perceptions of crime, fraud, unemployment, market behavior, medical diagnosis, or public risk whenever stereotype-like fit crowds out base-rate information. It also matters for why coherent public stories can overpower statistical context and why more detailed scenarios can feel more credible than formally broader ones.

## Open questions

How should institutions present statistical context in ways that compete effectively with vivid prototypes? When does similarity-based reasoning perform well enough to count as a practical shortcut rather than a bias? And should representativeness be taught mainly as a family of probability errors, or as a broader process of intuitive substitution and narrative fit?
