# Behavioral law and economics

## Core idea

[[Behavioral law and economics]] applies behavioral findings about actual human judgment and choice to legal analysis. In the current vault, the deepest conceptual background for that move is now [[Simon 1955]], because [[Herbert A. Simon]] makes clear why legal and economic analysis should not assume agents with unlimited information and unlimited computational power. The anchor behavioral law source remains [[Jolls et al. 1998]], which translates that broader realism into the now-familiar trio of [[Bounded rationality]], bounded willpower, and [[Social preferences]]. In the current vault, that self-control branch is handled mainly through [[Time inconsistency]]. The main benchmark background source is still [[Coase 1960]], which clarifies the classical law-and-economics focus on rights, bargaining, and [[Transaction costs]].

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from standard law and economics because it does not assume that legal actors behave as fully rational, fully self-controlled, narrowly self-interested maximizers. [[Coase 1960]] is useful here because it shows what the nonbehavioral benchmark looks like at its strongest: legal analysis centered on bargaining possibilities, rights allocation, and [[Transaction costs]]. The concept also differs from [[Behavioral public policy]], which is the broader policy branch now represented in the vault. Behavioral law and economics is an earlier and somewhat wider frame: it covers positive analysis of how law affects behavior, prescriptive analysis of what rules should be adopted, and normative analysis of the goals of the legal system.

## Evidence and debate

The debate is easiest to follow when it is broken into four connected branches rather than read as one long correction to standard law and economics.

The first branch is the move from the classical benchmark to the behavioral actor. [[Coase 1960]] gives the benchmark background: legal analysis should compare institutions in light of rights allocation, bargaining possibilities, and [[Transaction costs]]. [[Simon 1955]] and [[Simon 1979]] add the deeper challenge by arguing that realistic analysis must start from limited search, bounded computation, and organizational decision procedures rather than from frictionless maximization. [[Jolls et al. 1998]] is the decisive bridge source because it translates that realism into legal analysis through [[Bounded rationality]], bounded willpower, and [[Social preferences]]. In the current vault, that is the moment when behavioral law and economics becomes a genuine program rather than a set of scattered anomalies.

The second branch concerns legal design under error and paternalism. [[Jolls and Sunstein 2006]] matters because it shows that law can sometimes try to reduce predictable error through procedural or substantive design, which is why [[Debiasing]] belongs inside this page rather than only in the older heuristics literature. [[Sunstein and Thaler 2003]], [[Camerer et al. 2003]], and later policy-facing work can then be read as more interventionist descendants of that move. But the branch is contested. [[Lecouteux 2022 - Behavioral Welfare Economics and Consumer Sovereignty (in Heilmann and Reiss 2022, ch. 4)]] makes clear why the legal version of the welfare problem is so difficult: once observed choice no longer cleanly reveals welfare, familiar appeals to [[Consumer sovereignty]] weaken. [[Bernheim 2025]] responds by insisting on a narrower notion of mistake, while [[Sugden 2018]] objects more fundamentally to reconstructing an inner rational chooser whose preferences law may then privilege. In the current vault, this is the main normative fault line inside behavioral law and economics.

The third branch concerns institutional competence and formalized judgment. [[Meehl 1954]] is the historical anchor because it first poses the general problem of whether explicit predictive procedures outperform informal expertise. [[Dawes et al. 1989]] gives the later comparative lesson through [[Clinical versus actuarial judgment]]: across many domains, explicit predictive procedures often do outperform discretionary expertise. [[Kleinberg et al. 2018]] updates that lesson for criminal procedure by showing that algorithms can outperform judges in bail decisions, but only if institutions are explicit about what they are optimizing and how they handle selective labels and fairness tradeoffs. [[Angwin et al. 2016]] adds the public-law warning that the same systems may also be opaque, proprietary, and difficult to contest. [[Kleinberg et al. 2016]], [[Hellman 2020]], and [[Hedden 2021]] then show that even the language of fairness is internally contested because different [[Statistical fairness criteria]] capture different moral concerns. [[Obermeyer et al. 2019]] adds the [[Target variable problem]], and [[Wang et al. 2024]] broadens that into a critique of [[Predictive optimization]] itself. Taken together, these sources make algorithmic governance a central contemporary branch of behavioral law and economics rather than a side application.

The fourth branch concerns markets, experience, and the limits of revealed welfare. [[Bar-Gill et al. 2023]] is central here because it shows that legal concern is not limited to public algorithms; private firms can also use data and personalization to exploit behavioral vulnerabilities, now tracked under [[Algorithmic harm]]. [[Ariely et al. 2003]] and [[Ariely et al. 2006]] add a different pressure point by suggesting that observed demand may fail to reveal stable welfare because preferences can be constructed, anchored, or uncertain. [[List 2003]] and [[List 2004]] then qualify that whole branch by showing that some entitlement-sensitive anomalies shrink with market experience. [[Zaklan 2023]] adds a modern Coasean application in which initial allocations matter less in aggregate than a simple entitlement story would suggest, while still leaving room for friction-sensitive deviations. That is why this page should not read as if behavioral evidence always overrides market and institutional learning. The legal question is comparative: which anomalies are robust enough, and in which environments, to justify redesign?

The underlying debate across all four branches is the same one. Should behavioral findings merely add correction terms to standard law and economics, or do they require a deeper revision of legal design, welfare analysis, and institutional comparison? In the current vault, Simon, Bernheim, and Sugden all push toward the deeper reading, but in importantly different ways.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many legal institutions already assume answers to behavioral questions whether they acknowledge it or not. Disclosure rules assume something about attention and inference. Consumer protection assumes something about error and exploitation. Family law, contract law, savings policy, and administrative regulation all depend on whether people are treated as fully self-directed choosers or as agents facing predictable cognitive and motivational frictions. Behavioral law and economics makes those assumptions visible.

## Open questions

How far should legal analysis move away from the neoclassical baseline once behavioral departures are admitted? When do behavioral findings justify new legal defaults or consumer protections, and when do they merely complicate prediction? Is behavioral law and economics best treated as a correction to standard law and economics or as a rival framework?
