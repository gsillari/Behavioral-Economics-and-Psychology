# Behavioral law and economics

## Core idea

[[Behavioral law and economics]] applies behavioral findings about actual human judgment and choice to legal analysis. In the current vault, the deepest conceptual background for that move is now [[Simon 1955]], because [[Herbert A. Simon]] makes clear why legal and economic analysis should not assume agents with unlimited information and unlimited computational power. The anchor behavioral law source remains [[Jolls et al. 1998]], which translates that broader realism into the now-familiar trio of [[Bounded rationality]], bounded willpower, and [[Social preferences]]. In the current vault, that self-control branch is handled mainly through [[Time inconsistency]]. The main benchmark background source is still [[Coase 1960]], which clarifies the classical law-and-economics focus on rights, bargaining, and [[Transaction costs]].

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from standard law and economics because it does not assume that legal actors behave as fully rational, fully self-controlled, narrowly self-interested maximizers. [[Coase 1960]] is useful here because it shows what the nonbehavioral benchmark looks like at its strongest: legal analysis centered on bargaining possibilities, rights allocation, and [[Transaction costs]]. The concept also differs from [[Behavioral public policy]], which is the broader policy branch now represented in the vault. Behavioral law and economics is an earlier and somewhat wider frame: it covers positive analysis of how law affects behavior, prescriptive analysis of what rules should be adopted, and normative analysis of the goals of the legal system.

## Evidence and debate

[[Coase 1960]] provides the benchmark background: legal-economic analysis should compare institutional arrangements in light of bargaining possibilities and [[Transaction costs]]. [[Simon 1955]] provides the deeper conceptual background by arguing that rational choice should be modeled for real agents who search imperfectly and compute under constraint. [[Simon 1979]] deepens that lesson by insisting that decision procedures inside organizations belong at the center of economic analysis rather than at its margins. [[Jolls et al. 1998]] then becomes the first explicitly behavioral law anchor source, arguing that legal analysis is incomplete if it ignores cognitive limits, self-control failures, and fairness-related motives. [[Jolls and Sunstein 2006]] adds an important second step by showing that law need not merely accommodate or fence off those failures. It can sometimes try to reduce them through procedural and substantive design, which is why [[Debiasing]] belongs inside this branch rather than only in the older heuristics literature.

[[Dawes et al. 1989]] adds a complementary comparative-institutional lesson through [[Clinical versus actuarial judgment]]: explicit predictive procedures often outperform discretionary expertise, which matters wherever law delegates judgment to professionals or officials. [[Kleinberg et al. 2018]] then modernizes exactly that lesson inside criminal procedure by comparing judges with machine-learning predictions in bail decisions. Its importance is not only that an algorithm may predict better, but that legal institutions must specify what they are optimizing and how to evaluate predictive tools when liberty, crime, fairness, and selective observation all matter at once. [[Angwin et al. 2016]] adds the public-facing legal challenge from the other side: a predictive tool may be influential, proprietary, hard to contest, and racially disparate in its errors. [[Kleinberg et al. 2016]], [[Hellman 2020]], and [[Hedden 2021]] then add the formal and jurisprudential side of the same problem by showing that [[Statistical fairness criteria]] can conflict and that legal institutions still need a theory of which criteria matter and why. [[Obermeyer et al. 2019]] adds a different but equally important lesson: legal and policy scrutiny cannot stop at error rates if the system is optimizing the wrong proxy, now tracked under [[Target variable problem]]. [[Wang et al. 2024]] broadens that lesson into a challenge to [[Predictive optimization]] as a model of decision-making. In the current vault, those papers together make [[Algorithmic bias]] foundational for the legal-governance side of the AI branch. [[Bar-Gill et al. 2023]] adds a different legal-economic branch from consumer markets. Its key point is that algorithms can also create harm by exploiting absent information and behavioral biases through individualized pricing and product targeting, giving the vault a distinct home under [[Algorithmic harm]]. [[Ariely et al. 2003]] adds an important later pressure point by suggesting through [[Constructed preferences]] and [[Coherent arbitrariness]] that observed demand may not reveal stable underlying welfare in any simple sense. [[Ariely et al. 2006]] sharpens that concern for experiential goods through [[Preference uncertainty]], suggesting that even sampled experiences may not generate settled tastes that law or policy can simply read off from choice. [[Lecouteux 2022 - Behavioral Welfare Economics and Consumer Sovereignty (in Heilmann and Reiss 2022, ch. 4)]] helps state the legal version of that problem more explicitly by linking those behavioral difficulties to the weakening of [[Consumer sovereignty]] and the rise of [[Behavioral welfare economics]]. [[Bernheim 2025]] then adds a more explicit legal-policy caution: once welfare becomes contestable, legal institutions still need a disciplined account of what counts as mistake rather than simply inferring that instability licenses correction. [[Sugden 2018]] presses even harder by arguing that law and policy should resist the move from unstable or context-sensitive choices to an inner rational agent whose preferences deserve priority. In his alternative frame, now tracked under [[Opportunity criterion]], regulation should be judged more by whether it expands opportunities for mutually advantageous interaction than by whether it corrects citizens toward a reconstructed true preference ordering. [[List 2003]] and [[List 2004]] add a different kind of pressure point: entitlement-sensitive anomalies such as the [[Endowment effect]] may shrink with market experience, which matters for how confidently law should generalize from lab evidence to actual exchange environments. [[Zaklan 2023]] adds a modern Coasean application through [[Coase theorem]], showing that initial allocations in a cap-and-trade system may matter less in aggregate than a simple entitlement story would suggest, while still leaving room for friction-sensitive deviations. In the current vault, later sources such as [[Sunstein and Thaler 2003]], [[Camerer et al. 2003]], and [[Madrian 2014]] can be read as more policy-specific descendants of that broader move.

Debate centers on whether behavioral findings should merely modify standard law-and-economics models at the margins or instead require a deeper rethinking of legal design, welfare analysis, and paternalism. Simon matters here because he pushes toward the deeper reading: the issue is not only that some actors err, but that the benchmark actor may have been misdescribed from the beginning.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many legal institutions already assume answers to behavioral questions whether they acknowledge it or not. Disclosure rules assume something about attention and inference. Consumer protection assumes something about error and exploitation. Family law, contract law, savings policy, and administrative regulation all depend on whether people are treated as fully self-directed choosers or as agents facing predictable cognitive and motivational frictions. Behavioral law and economics makes those assumptions visible.

## Open questions

How far should legal analysis move away from the neoclassical baseline once behavioral departures are admitted? When do behavioral findings justify new legal defaults or consumer protections, and when do they merely complicate prediction? Is behavioral law and economics best treated as a correction to standard law and economics or as a rival framework?
