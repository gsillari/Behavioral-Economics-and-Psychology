# Evolutionary game theory

## Core idea

[[Evolutionary game theory]] studies how strategies spread, persist, or disappear in populations when payoffs affect reproduction, imitation, learning, or cultural transmission. In the current vault, [[Skyrms 2014]] is the anchor source. Its importance is that it shifts the question from "Which strategy would ideally rational agents choose?" to "Which strategies can emerge and stabilize under a dynamic process?"

This matters for behavioral economics and PPE because many social patterns are not best understood as explicit contracts or as one-shot rational choices. Fair division, costly rejection, cooperation, ownership, and convention can be treated as population-level outcomes of repeated interaction, correlation, imitation, mutation, recombination, learning, and local structure.
[[Sugden 1989]] adds an earlier and more institution-facing version of this point. Sugden uses evolutionary stability to explain how conventions can emerge and persist as [[Spontaneous order]], substituting utility for biological fitness and learning or imitation for natural selection.

## Key distinctions

The first distinction is between static equilibrium and dynamic explanation. A Nash equilibrium says that no player has a unilateral incentive to deviate. Evolutionary analysis asks whether a population state is stable under a process that increases the share of more successful strategies.

The second distinction is between random and correlated interaction. Under random pairing, evolutionary and classical game theory often align. Under correlated pairing, like may interact with like through kinship, partner choice, repeated interaction, local clustering, signaling, or group structure. That can support cooperation, altruism, fair division, or conventions that the uncorrelated game would not select.

The third distinction is between biological and cultural evolution. Biological evolution works through differential reproduction. Cultural evolution may work through imitation, reinforcement, learning, transmission, mutation-like trial, and recombination of strategies. Skyrms uses the same abstract dynamics to make both intelligible, while keeping the mechanisms distinct.

The fourth distinction is between evolutionary explanation and normative justification. Showing how a norm could evolve does not show that it is morally justified. Skyrms is explicit that his project is explanatory rather than a replacement for normative social-contract theory.

## Evidence and debate

[[Skyrms 2014 - Chapter 01 - Sex and Justice]] uses sex ratios and bargaining to introduce evolutionary stability and the replicator dynamics. Equal division is not uniquely selected by static Nash analysis, but it can become the unique evolutionarily stable pure strategy under the Darwinian veil of ignorance. The complication is that polymorphic traps can also be stable.

[[Skyrms 2014 - Chapter 02 - Fairness and Commitment]] applies evolutionary reasoning to the [[Ultimatum game]]. It shows why evolution need not eliminate costly punishment or non-sequentially-rational strategies. This matters because ultimatum-game behavior should not be read simply as irrationality; it may reflect evolved or culturally stabilized norm responses.

[[Skyrms 2014 - Chapter 03 - Mutual Aid]] makes correlation central. Cooperation can evolve when cooperators are more likely to meet cooperators, whether through kinship, partner choice, reciprocal altruism, local interaction, or group structure. This chapter is the main bridge to [[Cooperation]].

[[Skyrms 2014 - Chapter 04 - Correlated Convention]] applies the same dynamic logic to [[Coordination games]] and [[Convention]]. Correlated equilibria can become conventions when cues, roles, and learning break symmetry. Ownership is treated as one important case: possession can become a role cue that coordinates conflict.

[[Sugden 1989]] is a crucial precursor to this branch. It defines a convention as an evolutionarily stable strategy in a game with multiple ESSs and uses Chicken to show why conventions tend to exploit asymmetries. It also emphasizes that evolutionary stability is not the same as efficiency: a convention can be stable because it spreads quickly or analogically, not because it is best.

The main debate this opens for the vault is how to connect evolutionary dynamics with behavioral game evidence. Evolutionary game theory can explain why fairness and cooperation are possible, but cross-cultural evidence still shows path-dependence and variation. It should therefore not be used to smuggle in a universal fairness instinct.

## Practical or policy relevance

Evolutionary game theory matters for institutional analysis because institutions often stabilize patterns of interaction rather than merely constrain isolated choices. It gives the vault a language for asking when cooperation, fair division, property-like expectations, or conventions are dynamically stable, fragile, inefficient, or culturally path-dependent.

It is especially useful in PPE because it connects political theory's social contract, economics' strategic models, and philosophy's analysis of norms and conventions without assuming that social order begins from explicit consent.

## Open questions

When should a behavioral regularity be explained by evolved or culturally transmitted dynamics rather than by stable social preferences? How much correlation is needed to sustain cooperation in realistic institutions? When do conventions solve coordination problems, and when do they lock populations into inefficient equilibria?
