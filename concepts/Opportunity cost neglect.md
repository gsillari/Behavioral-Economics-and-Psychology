# Opportunity cost neglect

## Core idea

[[Opportunity cost neglect]] is the tendency to underrepresent or ignore the alternative uses of money, time, or resources that a focal choice displaces. In the current vault, the main source is [[Frederick et al. 2009]], which shows that consumers often do not spontaneously generate those forgone alternatives unless they are made salient. [[Thaler 1980]] matters here as an earlier bridge source because it already treats underweighting of opportunity costs as one of the recurring departures from standard consumer theory.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Mental accounting]]. Mental accounting is a broader claim about how people code and separate gains, losses, and budgets. Opportunity cost neglect is narrower: the problem is that the displaced alternative often never becomes explicit in the first place.

It also differs from [[Limited attention]], though it sits close to it. Limited attention is the wider idea that people underprocess non-salient information. Opportunity cost neglect is one particularly important case where the neglected information consists of forgone uses of money or resources rather than a tax, fee, probability, or attribute.

## Evidence and debate

[[Frederick et al. 2009]] shows that describing the nonpurchase option as keeping money for other purchases, or making leftover cash explicit, reduces purchase rates and shifts choice toward cheaper options. The paper matters because these effects persist even when participants are pushed to think more carefully, suggesting that effort alone does not guarantee spontaneous representation of outside options.

[[Thaler 1980]] is useful as background because it shows that economists entering the behavioral tradition had already identified opportunity-cost underweighting as a meaningful departure from standard consumer theory. The later Frederick et al. paper sharpens that older point into a more specific and experimentally tractable mechanism: the neglected opportunity cost is often not computed because the relevant alternatives are never actively generated.

The main debate is how to classify the effect. It can be read as a species of [[Limited attention]], as a framing effect in consumer choice, or as a gap in internal accounting that sits near [[Mental accounting]]. In the current vault, the cleanest treatment is to see it as a contained but important leaf concept under [[Nonstandard decision-making]].

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many real decisions look cheaper or more attractive when the forgone alternative stays implicit. Consumer disclosures, reminders, savings tools, and even classroom economic instruction can try to improve choice by making the displaced alternative more visible. For PPE students, the page is especially helpful because it connects a textbook economic norm, opportunity cost, to a specific behavioral finding about why actual choosers often fail to apply it.
