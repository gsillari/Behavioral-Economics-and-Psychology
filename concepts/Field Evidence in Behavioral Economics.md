# Field Evidence in Behavioral Economics

to be REVIEWED

## Core idea

[[Field Evidence in Behavioral Economics]] refers to empirical work showing that the kinds of departures from the standard model identified by behavioral economics also appear in real-world settings, not only in laboratory experiments. In the current vault, the anchor sources are [[DellaVigna 2009]], which organizes this evidence into preference-side mechanisms, [[Nonstandard beliefs]], and [[Nonstandard decision-making]] across a wide range of economic and political domains, [[Madrian 2014]], which connects those patterns to policy-relevant interventions, and [[Barnett et al. 2005]], which adds a methodological warning about [[Regression to the mean]] when interpreting repeated-measures evidence.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from a simple catalogue of behavioral theories. Its focus is not primarily on what mechanisms have been proposed, but on where those mechanisms show up outside the lab and how strongly they matter in practice. It also differs from purely policy-oriented behavioral work because field evidence can be used for several purposes at once: to test the external validity of lab findings, to revise models of markets and institutions, and to identify situations in which policy intervention may or may not be justified. [[Madrian 2014]] sharpens that last use by showing how field-relevant frictions can be mapped to different intervention margins. [[Barnett et al. 2005]] adds a different distinction that matters just as much for this branch: some apparent effects may be inferential artifacts rather than genuine evidence of behavioral change, especially when researchers focus on extreme baseline observations.

## Evidence and debate

[[DellaVigna 2009]], [[Madrian 2014]], [[Barnett et al. 2005]], and [[Hossain and List 2009]] are the current anchor sources.
DellaVigna surveys evidence on [[Time inconsistency]], [[Reference dependence]], [[Social preferences]], [[Overconfidence]], [[Projection bias]], the [[Law of small numbers]], [[Limited attention]], [[Framing effects]], [[Menu effects]], interpersonal influence, and [[Emotions in decision-making]], among other themes.
A key contribution is that the survey does not stop at individual behavior; it also considers how firms, employers, investors, and politicians respond strategically to predictable behavioral patterns, a move captured in [[Strategic response to behavioral anomalies]].
Madrian then turns to policy design and shows how real-world frictions such as inattention, self-control problems, and context dependence can motivate different kinds of interventions, including reminders, defaults, and redesigned incentives.
Barnett and coauthors add an important methodological caution: when researchers select unusually high or low cases and then observe later outcomes, some apparent improvement or deterioration may reflect [[Regression to the mean]] rather than a behavioral mechanism or successful intervention.
[[Hossain and List 2009]] adds a different kind of field evidence: a natural experiment in a Chinese factory showing that [[Loss-framed incentives]] can change productivity. This matters because it connects [[Loss aversion]] and [[Reference dependence]] directly to organizational design rather than only to choice tasks or financial behavior. [[Laibson and List 2015]] adds a concise didactic lesson that is useful for the current vault: market outcomes can be shaped by boundedly rational behavior when enough market participants, or enough marginal traders, are affected. Their examples of bubbles and arbitrage limits help explain why field evidence matters for markets rather than only for individuals.

In the current vault, the field-evidence architecture is most useful when organized comparatively:

- strongest preference-side anchors: [[Time inconsistency]], [[Reference dependence]], [[Social preferences]]
- strongest belief-side anchors: [[Overconfidence]], [[Projection bias]], [[Law of small numbers]]
- strongest decision-process anchors: [[Limited attention]], [[Framing effects]], [[Menu effects]], [[Emotions in decision-making]]
- equilibrium-level extension: [[Strategic response to behavioral anomalies]]

The main debate raised by this page is therefore not only whether field evidence confirms a unified behavioral-economics framework.
It is also how strong the identification and measurement standards must be before those empirical patterns should guide theory and policy, and which mechanisms are robust enough to move from interesting evidence to real policy relevance.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because it gives [[Behavioral public policy]] and the broader field stronger empirical footing.
If behavioral deviations appear repeatedly in high-stakes domains such as savings, finance, voting, labor markets, and consumer choice, then behavioral economics is harder to dismiss as a laboratory curiosity.
At the same time, field evidence can discipline overreach by showing where effects are strong, where they attenuate, and where markets or experience may partially offset them.
[[Madrian 2014]] adds a further payoff: evidence from the field helps determine whether the main problem is [[Limited attention]], [[Time inconsistency]], [[Reference dependence]], or something else, and therefore which policy tools are likely to be effective.
[[Barnett et al. 2005]] adds a methodological discipline to that optimism by showing how badly evidence can mislead when [[Regression to the mean]] is ignored.

The page is therefore not just about "more evidence."
It is about sorting mechanisms by practical significance and using that sorting to decide which findings should carry the most weight in policy, welfare analysis, and course design.

## Open questions

Which behavioral mechanisms travel most reliably from the lab to the field? When do experience, competition, and institutional design dampen behavioral effects, and when do they amplify them? How should field evidence change the balance between descriptive explanation and paternalistic intervention? When does field evidence justify talking about [[Internalities]] rather than simply low-information or low-incentive environments? And which influential behavioral findings are most vulnerable to [[Regression to the mean]] or related methodological threats?
