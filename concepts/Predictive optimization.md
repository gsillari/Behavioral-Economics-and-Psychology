# Predictive optimization

## Core idea

[[Predictive optimization]] is the use of predictive models to make or structure decisions about individuals by optimizing around forecasted future outcomes. In the current vault, the concept is anchored by [[Wang et al. 2024]], with important concrete support from [[Obermeyer et al. 2019]] and a system-level extension in [[Narayanan 2026]]. It differs from using prediction merely as advice or measurement. The prediction is built directly into a decision rule that allocates burdens, opportunities, or resources.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Clinical versus actuarial judgment]], which is the broader comparison between human and formal prediction. Predictive optimization is a more ambitious institutional strategy: it treats predictive accuracy as a basis for decision-making design rather than only as a comparative benchmark.

It also differs from [[Algorithmic bias]]. Predictive optimization can be criticized even when a system is not obviously biased in the ordinary disparity sense, because the broader institutional practice may still be illegitimate, unaccountable, or built around the wrong target.

## Evidence and debate

[[Wang et al. 2024]] gives the strongest articulation in the current vault. It argues that predictive optimization is a distinctive class of systems whose promises of accuracy, efficiency, and fairness often fail on their own terms. [[Narayanan 2026]] builds on this by arguing that predictive systems are best evaluated as algorithmic bureaucracies rather than as models in isolation. That moves the critique from target choice and legitimacy to the full decision process: value specification, policy formation, human adjudication, contestability, explanations, and the social setting in which the tool operates. [[Obermeyer et al. 2019]] shows one especially clear mechanism behind that failure: optimization around a bad proxy target can magnify inequality even when the system appears to perform well. [[Kleinberg et al. 2024]] adds a deeper behavioral mechanism through [[Inversion problem]]: even if a model predicts observed behavior well, an institution may still be trying to recover something else such as preference, welfare, or reflective judgment. Nearby sources such as [[Kleinberg et al. 2018]] and [[Ludwig et al. 2024]] help explain why predictive systems are institutionally attractive in the first place, which makes the critique more rather than less important.

The main debate is whether these systems are best understood as useful but risky tools, or as a category of institutional decision-making that often demands a much higher burden of justification than developers provide.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because predictive optimization increasingly appears in criminal justice, health care, education, finance, and public administration. Once institutions begin to optimize decisions around predicted outcomes, questions about target choice, fairness, legitimacy, contestability, and feedback effects move to the center.
