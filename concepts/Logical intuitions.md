# Logical intuitions

## Core idea

[[Logical intuitions]] are intuitive sensitivities to logical or normative conflict in reasoning tasks. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[De Neys 2012]], which argues that people can often detect that a heuristic response conflicts with a better answer even when they fail to override the heuristic response.

[[Wason 1968]] is now useful as the older background case. The [[Wason selection task]] shows how subjects can fail to select the falsifying not-Q case even after prompts that seem designed to make falsification salient. Later work on logical intuitions asks a more refined question: are subjects wholly blind to the logical conflict, or do they register some conflict while still giving the wrong response?

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from the simple claim that intuition is always heuristic and error-prone. It also differs from full reflective reasoning, where the correct norm is explicitly applied and the tempting answer is successfully overridden. Logical intuitions mark an intermediate case: the agent may register conflict without fully resolving it.

## Evidence and debate

[[De Neys 2012]] uses conflict-detection findings to argue that many biased responses are not wholly blind. This matters because it complicates a standard reading of [[Heuristics and Biases]] according to which norm sensitivity only appears when slow, reflective correction takes over. The debate is whether conflict detection really shows intuitive logical competence, or whether it reflects a weaker, less content-rich sense that something is wrong.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because judgments that look irrational from the outside may feel conflicted from the inside. That has consequences for education, nudging, and institutional design. Interventions may work differently depending on whether people lack the norm entirely or already sense the conflict but fail to act on it.

## Open questions

How robust are logical intuitions across tasks and populations? Do they indicate genuine implicit norm competence or only generic uncertainty? And how should they affect the way we classify biased responses as irrational?
