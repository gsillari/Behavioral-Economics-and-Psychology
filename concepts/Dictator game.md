# Dictator game

## Core idea

The [[Dictator game]] is a simple allocation experiment in which one player unilaterally divides a surplus and the recipient has no power to reject. Its role in the wiki is comparative: it helps interpret the [[Ultimatum game]] by removing the responder's strategic veto.

If proposers in ultimatum games offered fair splits only because they feared rejection, giving should disappear when rejection is impossible. [[Camerer and Thaler 1995]] explains why the evidence is more interesting: dictator giving usually falls, but it often remains positive and is highly sensitive to anonymity, social distance, framing, and what the allocator thinks the situation means.

## What it clarifies

The dictator game separates social preference from strategic precaution, but it does not give a pure readout of altruism. Giving can reflect generosity, fairness norms, self-image, experimenter demand, reluctance to violate manners, or discomfort with inequality. That is why this page should not become a list of dictator-game variants. Its conceptual point is that removing strategic punishment makes the interpretation of [[Social preferences]] sharper and also more fragile.

[[Fehr and Schmidt 1999]] uses dictator evidence as one test for [[Inequity aversion]], but the paper also notes limits: a simple inequity-aversion model does not automatically explain every dictator result. That matters because the dictator game is often where social-preference models become empirically delicate.

[[Henrich et al. 2004]] places the dictator game inside a wider cross-cultural measurement strategy. Used beside the [[Ultimatum game]], it helps ask whether positive transfers reflect fear of rejection, sharing norms, altruism, entitlement, or local construals of windfall allocation.
