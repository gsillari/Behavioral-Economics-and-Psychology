# Consumer sovereignty

## Core idea

[[Consumer sovereignty]] is the classical welfare-economics idea that what is good for consumers should be determined by their own preferences rather than by the evaluator's substantive view of the good. In practice, that often means reading welfare from choice, demand, or preference satisfaction. In the current vault, [[Lecouteux 2022 - Behavioral Welfare Economics and Consumer Sovereignty (in Heilmann and Reiss 2022, ch. 4)]] is now the clearest overview of how behavioral economics pressures that ideal.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Revealed preference theory]], even though the two are historically close. Revealed preference is a methodological and theoretical strategy for inferring preference from observed choice. Consumer sovereignty is the welfare and normative idea that those preferences deserve deference.

It also differs from [[Behavioral welfare economics]], which begins when the sovereignty ideal becomes harder to maintain because observed choice no longer seems to track welfare cleanly.

Economists were drawn to consumer sovereignty because it combines two attractive commitments. It is informationally modest, in that it does not require policymakers to know people's good better than they do themselves. And it is normatively anti-paternalist, in that it tells analysts to respect the chooser's own priorities even when those priorities differ from elite or official judgments.

## Why it matters here

This concept matters because a large part of the vault's policy branch turns on whether and when institutions should defer to observed choice. Behavioral evidence about framing, self-control, preference construction, and uncertainty does not automatically destroy consumer sovereignty, but it weakens simple versions of it. Once behavior is unstable, the live question is no longer just whether to respect "the consumer's preference," but which choice under which conditions deserves normative authority. That is why the concept belongs near both the preference branch and the behavioral-policy branch.

## Open questions

How much instability in observed choice is enough to weaken consumer sovereignty? Can the ideal be revised without being abandoned? And when policy departs from consumer sovereignty, what principle should take its place?
