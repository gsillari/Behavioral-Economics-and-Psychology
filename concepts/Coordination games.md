# Coordination games

## Core idea

[[Coordination games]] are strategic situations in which agents can do well only if their actions fit together. Their problem is not always selfishness, temptation, or conflict. Sometimes the main problem is convergence: each player must choose in a way that matches what the other player expects, while knowing that the other player is trying to do the same.

[[Schelling 1960 - Chapter 04 - Toward a Theory of Interdependent Decision]] makes this the central lesson of [[Schelling 1960]]. Pure coordination is the opposite limiting case from pure conflict. In pure conflict, one player's gain is the other's loss. In pure coordination, the players' interests are aligned, but they may still fail if they cannot find the same action, signal, meeting place, boundary, convention, or rule.

[[Lewis 1969]] is the immediate philosophical successor. Lewis takes Schelling's focal-point insight and turns it into a general theory of [[Convention]]: recurrent coordination problems can become stable social regularities when conformity, expectations of conformity, and conditional reasons to conform are mutually known.

## Why coordination is not trivial

Schelling's crucial move is to show that a shared interest does not eliminate the strategic problem. Two people who need to meet without communication, drivers who need to avoid collision, dancers adjusting to unfamiliar music, or an audience deciding whether to keep applauding all face the same kind of issue: each must act with an eye to what will be mutually recognized.

This is why focal clues matter. A salient time, place, label, boundary, or convention can coordinate expectations even without explicit communication. The clue works not because it mechanically causes behavior, but because each player can see it as something the other player may also see as a natural point of convergence.

## Labels and rationality

The chapter is important for the wiki because it changes how game theory should be read. If a normal-form representation strips away the labels and symbolic details of a situation, it may remove information that rational players should use. Schelling's point is therefore not just descriptive. He argues that a normative theory of correct play cannot ignore details that help players jointly avoid worse outcomes.

This makes coordination games a useful bridge between formal rationality and behavioral interpretation. The issue is not simply that people are biased by salience. Salience can be part of the rational structure of the game when it helps agents form mutually consistent expectations.

## From focal points to convention

Lewis deepens the Schelling branch by asking when a solution to a coordination problem becomes a convention. The answer is not merely "when people repeatedly do it." A convention requires a regularity that agents expect one another to follow, alternatives that could also have solved the problem, and enough [[Common knowledge]] to make conformity self-sustaining.

This is the bridge to social institutions. A convention can stabilize traffic practices, linguistic usage, bargaining practices, or informal rules without being reducible to law, morality, explicit agreement, or imitation. It is equilibrium-like, but it is also socially and epistemically structured.

## Relation to cooperation and bargaining

Coordination is close to [[Cooperation]], but it is not the same mechanism. Cooperation often concerns incentives to contribute or defect. Coordination concerns whether agents can make their actions mesh. A group can fail to coordinate even when everyone wants the same outcome; it can also coordinate on a bad or fragile equilibrium.

Schelling also treats coordination as central to mixed-motive games. Bargaining contains conflict over distribution, but it also contains a shared interest in finding an agreement better than no agreement. That means coordination of expectations remains present even where players compete.

## Open questions

When should coordination be treated as a formal equilibrium-selection problem, and when should it be treated as a social or cultural problem of convention and shared salience? How often do institutional failures come from bad incentives rather than failed coordination? When does a focal point solve a problem, and when does it lock agents into an inferior convention?
