# Convention

## Core idea

A [[Convention]] is a stable social regularity that solves a recurrent [[Coordination games|coordination problem]]. Its force does not come primarily from coercion, moral obligation, or explicit agreement. It comes from mutually reinforcing expectations: each person has reason to conform because others are expected to conform, and others have reason to conform because that expectation is shared.

[[Lewis 1969]] is the anchor source for this concept in the current vault. It develops the philosophical theory of convention as a direct successor to [[Schelling 1960]]. Schelling shows that focal points, labels, salience, and mutual perception can select one coordination equilibrium among many. Lewis turns that insight into a general account of how conventions stabilize recurrent social interaction.

[[Skyrms 2014 - Chapter 04 - Correlated Convention]] adds the evolutionary version of the story. A convention can emerge not only because agents recognize a focal point or share common knowledge, but because learning and population dynamics amplify a symmetry-breaking cue until a correlated equilibrium becomes a stable custom.

## The Lewis structure

The first element is a recurrent coordination problem. A population repeatedly faces situations in which agents need their actions to fit together, and more than one pattern of fitting together is possible.

The second element is a regularity of behavior. People generally conform to one pattern rather than another.

The third element is expectation. People expect others to conform to the regularity, and those expectations help make conformity individually rational.

The fourth element is alternative possibility. The regularity is conventional partly because another regularity could have solved the same problem if expectations and precedent had settled differently.

The fifth element is [[Common knowledge]]. The relevant conformity, expectations, and conditional preferences must be mutually accessible enough to sustain the pattern. This is what makes convention more than mere habit or repeated behavior.

## What convention is not

Lewis's Chapter 3 is important because it prevents conceptual sprawl. Convention is not simply agreement. Agreements can create conventions, but conventions can arise without explicit agreement through precedent, salience, and repeated coordination.

Convention is also not identical with a rule or a norm. Rules can be written and enforced; norms can involve obligation, approval, blame, or sanction. A convention may be rule-like or norm-supported, but its distinctive mechanism is coordination through mutual expectations.

Convention is not mere imitation either. Imitation may spread a pattern, but convention requires that following the pattern be tied to the expectation that others will follow it too.

## Why it matters for institutions

This concept is a precursor to game-theoretic analysis of social institutions. Many institutions work because agents expect one another to act in certain ways and because those expectations make conformity sensible. Traffic practices, linguistic meanings, bargaining practices, money, political procedures, legal defaults, and informal social rules all become easier to analyze once conventions are understood as equilibrium-like regularities sustained by common expectations.

For behavioral economics, the point is subtle but important. Salience and labels are not always biases or superficial framing effects. In coordination settings, they can be part of the rational structure of the situation because they help agents converge.

Skyrms broadens this institutional point by showing how conventions can arise through [[Evolutionary game theory]]. Ownership, for example, can function as a role cue in a hawk-dove contest: "fight as owner, yield as intruder" is a correlated convention that can stabilize conflict over resources. This does not make property morally justified by itself, but it explains how property-like expectations can emerge before formal law.

## Open questions

When should an institution be analyzed as a convention rather than as a norm, rule, contract, or coercive structure? How much common knowledge is needed for a convention to exist? When do conventions solve coordination problems, and when do they lock a population into inferior equilibria?
