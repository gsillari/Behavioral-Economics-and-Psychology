# Equilibrium selection

## Core idea

[[Equilibrium selection]] is the problem of explaining which equilibrium agents will actually play when a game has several self-enforcing outcomes. It is different from equilibrium existence. A model may show that many outcomes would be stable if expected; it has not yet explained why one of them becomes expected.

This concept sits at the center of the coordination branch. In [[Coordination games]], players may share an interest in coordinating, but still need some reason to converge on one pattern rather than another. In mixed-motive games, they may also disagree about which coordinated outcome is best. [[Camerer 2003 - Chapter 07 - Coordination]] is the current wiki's main empirical map of this problem.

The conceptual sequence is Schelling -> Lewis -> experimental behavioral game theory. [[Schelling 1960]] introduces focal points as tacit selection devices. [[Lewis 1969]] explains how recurrent selections can become [[Convention|conventions]]. [[Van Huyck et al. 1990]] and Camerer show experimentally that selection is often fragile: people can coordinate on secure but inefficient equilibria, fail to use payoff dominance, or follow principles that depend on framing, communication, history, and group structure.

## Selection principles and empirical anchors

Equilibrium selection is not governed by one universal rule. Camerer's Chapter 7 is useful because it matches principles with experimental settings.

- [[Focal points]] and common ground work in matching games and visual assignment tasks, where payoffs alone leave many equivalent equilibria.
- Payoff dominance works when the efficient equilibrium is psychologically safe or salient, but it is not reliable when the efficient choice exposes players to strategic risk.
- Risk dominance and security matter in stag-hunt and weak-link games, where players may prefer a lower but safer equilibrium to a high payoff that depends on everyone else choosing high too.
- Communication helps when it selects a unique equilibrium and gives assurance. It can be weaker in battle-of-the-sexes games because talk can intensify conflict over whose preferred equilibrium should be selected.
- External assignment or authority can select an equilibrium in pure matching games, but it loses force when it competes with equality, payoff dominance, or another salient rule.
- Precedence and path dependence matter in median-action and continental-divide experiments, where early behavior can become a durable anchor.
- Simplicity matters because formally equivalent games can be played differently when one representation is easier to reason through.
- Loss avoidance and [[Mental accounting]] matter in preplay auction and entry-fee experiments, where a prior payment can make low coordination feel like a loss to avoid.
- Level-1 or diffuse-prior reasoning performs strongly in Haruvy and Stahl's comparison of selection principles, often better than payoff dominance, risk dominance, or maximin.

The lesson is not pluralism for its own sake. It is empirical discipline. Selection principles should be tied to the kinds of games and representations in which they actually predict behavior.

## Relation to institutions

Equilibrium selection is one plausible function of social institutions. Institutions do not only support [[Cooperation]] by changing incentives, monitoring behavior, or sanctioning defectors. They can also make one equilibrium salient, credible, public, legitimate, or expected.

This matters for conventions, law, organizational routines, standards, traffic rules, monetary practices, and political procedures. Such institutions may solve a selection problem by telling people not simply what is good, but what others will treat as the thing to do.

The same mechanism can also be dangerous. If history or institutional design selects a bad equilibrium, coordination can stabilize an inferior pattern. This is the darker side of convention and path dependence.

## Relation to behavioral economics

Equilibrium selection is a behavioral problem because formal structure alone often underdetermines prediction. Players use labels, histories, analogies, categories, assignments, communication, expectations, and representations. Those features are not mere psychological noise. In many games they are the material from which equilibrium expectations are built.

This is why equilibrium selection belongs near [[Behavioral game theory]] and [[Experimental games]]. The issue is not whether people are irrational because they fail to pick the economist's favorite equilibrium. The issue is which cues and principles people can mutually rely on when rational choice leaves several self-enforcing possibilities.
