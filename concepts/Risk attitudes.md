# Risk attitudes

## Core idea

[[Risk attitudes]] refers to how an agent evaluates known-risk prospects such as lotteries with given probabilities. In the standard expected-utility framework, the central representation device is the curvature of the utility function: concavity represents risk aversion, convexity represents risk seeking, and linearity represents risk neutrality. In the current vault, [[von Neumann and Morgenstern 1944 - Appendix - The Axiomatic Treatment of Utility]] supplies the canonical risk-side utility representation, while [[Stefansson 2022 - The Economics and Philosophy of Risk (in Heilmann and Reiss 2022, ch. 3)]] gives the compact philosophical overview.

[[Rabin and Thaler 2001]] is now the key source for the main pressure point. Expected utility can formally represent risk aversion, but ordinary small-stakes risk aversion cannot plausibly be explained by global concavity of utility over lifetime wealth. When that curvature is [[Calibration|calibrated]] to familiar small gambles, it implies absurd aversion to enormously favorable large gambles.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Ambiguity aversion]]. Risk attitudes concern preferences over outcomes when probabilities are known. Ambiguity aversion concerns discomfort with uncertainty about the probabilities themselves.

It also differs from [[Subjective expected utility theory]]. That concept concerns the uncertainty-side benchmark in which acts are evaluated through personal probabilities over states of the world. Risk attitudes belong primarily to the risk-side branch of [[Expected utility theory]], now anchored in the vault by [[Von Neumann-Morgenstern utility theory]].

It also differs from [[Prospect Theory]]. Prospect theory does not simply replace one risk attitude with another. It changes the evaluative architecture by introducing reference points, gain-loss coding, and decision weights. That means risk attitudes in expected utility and risk attitudes in prospect-theoretic settings should not be treated as identical objects.

It also differs from [[Loss aversion]]. Risk aversion in the expected-utility sense is usually represented by concavity over final wealth. Loss aversion is reference-dependent and asymmetrical: losses hurt more than equal gains help. [[Rabin and Thaler 2001]] matters because it argues that many ordinary cases economists call risk aversion are better explained by loss aversion plus [[Mental accounting]] than by wealth curvature.

## Why it matters here

This concept matters because many debates in risky choice turn on a point that is easy to understate: expected utility can represent very different attitudes toward risk, but it does so through a specific formal device. That helps explain both its appeal and its limits. The VNM appendix also makes clear that the numerical representation depends on preferences over probabilistic alternatives, not on a direct psychological meter of satisfaction.

The Rabin-Thaler calibration critique is especially important because it does not merely add one more anomaly. It shows why the standard explanation of moderate-stakes risk aversion is structurally miscalibrated. If a person rejects small favorable gambles because marginal utility of wealth falls steeply over small wealth changes, the same curve says that the person should reject extremely attractive larger gambles. That is why the page should teach risk attitudes in two steps: expected utility gives the formal representation; behavioral economics asks whether that representation has the right psychological mechanism.
