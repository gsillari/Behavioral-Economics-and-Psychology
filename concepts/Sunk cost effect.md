# Sunk cost effect

## Core idea

[[Sunk cost effect]] is the tendency to continue an endeavor once resources have already been invested in it, even when those past costs should not matter for the present decision. In the current vault, the anchor experimental source is [[Arkes and Blumer 1985]], but [[Thaler 1980]] is now an important earlier economics-facing formulation of the problem.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from rational persistence. Continuing a project can be sensible if future benefits still outweigh future costs. The sunk cost effect arises when past expenditure itself becomes a reason to continue. It also differs from simple habit or commitment because the key driver is the felt relevance of an irrecoverable prior investment. In the current vault, the effect sits between [[Expected utility theory]], which treats sunk costs as normatively irrelevant, and [[Prospect Theory]], which helps explain why people may resist realizing losses.

## Evidence and debate

[[Thaler 1980]] matters because it identifies sunk-cost sensitivity as a recurring consumer-choice error and tries to model it through prospect-theory-style psychic accounting rather than simply treating it as noise. That makes the paper an important conceptual predecessor for the later bias literature. [[Arkes and Blumer 1985]] provides the anchor evidence. The paper combines field and questionnaire studies to show that prior investment increases willingness to persist. The most famous field case involves theater subscriptions: people who paid more for a series attended more often, even though the ticket cost was already unrecoverable. Debate concerns the mechanism. Some explanations stress loss aversion and the reluctance to realize a sure loss, while others stress self-justification, waste aversion, or norms of consistency and commitment.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because sunk-cost thinking appears in domains far beyond small consumer decisions. It can shape business investment, household consumption, military escalation, infrastructure policy, and bureaucratic project continuation. The practical lesson is not that all persistence is irrational, but that institutions often need procedures that separate future-oriented evaluation from the emotional and symbolic weight of past expenditure.

## Open questions

Which mechanism matters most: loss aversion, waste aversion, self-justification, or social signaling? When do experience and accountability reduce the sunk cost effect, and when do they intensify it? What kinds of institutional design best limit sunk-cost persistence without weakening responsible long-term commitment?
