# Behavioral welfare economics

## Core idea

[[Behavioral welfare economics]] is the attempt to rethink welfare analysis once actual choice no longer looks like a transparent guide to what people value. In the current vault, [[Lecouteux 2022 - Behavioral Welfare Economics and Consumer Sovereignty (in Heilmann and Reiss 2022, ch. 4)]] now gives the cleanest overview of that project, [[Bernheim 2025]] gives one of its strongest internal restrictions, [[Rabin 1998]] introduces the key distinction between decision utility and [[Experienced utility]], and [[O'Donoghue and Rabin 1999]] supplies a canonical self-control case in which current choice may conflict with the chooser's own temporally extended welfare.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Behavioral public policy]]. Public policy is the larger intervention branch. Behavioral welfare economics is the narrower question of what should count as welfare once behavior displays inconsistency, context sensitivity, self-control failure, or unstable preference.

It also differs from the older ideal of [[Consumer sovereignty]]. Classical consumer sovereignty assumes that welfare is tracked by the satisfaction of the consumer's own preferences as revealed in choice. Behavioral welfare economics begins when that inference becomes doubtful.

That gives the field a three-step structure. Standard welfare economics says that observed choice normally reveals what is good for the chooser. Behavioral welfare economics begins when observed choice looks too unstable, impulsive, reason-dependent, or context-sensitive to play that role cleanly. The hard part comes next: once revealed choice is no longer enough, analysts need some replacement account of welfare, such as informed preference, reflective preference, experienced utility, or protected opportunity.

## Why it matters here

This concept matters because much of the vault's policy debate turns on it, even when the phrase is not used explicitly. If preferences are partly [[Constructed preferences|constructed]], if people display [[Preference uncertainty]], if choice depends on available reasons, or if observed choice depends heavily on framing and timing, then policy cannot simply defer to current choice without further argument. [[Rabin 1998]] adds the experienced-utility problem: people may choose one thing while later experiencing, remembering, or endorsing something else. [[O'Donoghue and Rabin 1999]] makes the timing problem especially vivid: eating, smoking, saving, or delaying a task may look like ordinary preference satisfaction or like a present self imposing costs on a later self. But the page also matters because it blocks an equally quick move in the opposite direction: once observed choice becomes unreliable, analysts still need a defensible account of what should replace it. That is where the Bernheim and Sugden cautions become central.

For that reason, behavioral welfare economics is not just "welfare economics plus biases." It is a dispute about whether a choice pattern records a mistake, a conflict between short-run and long-run aims, an unsettled valuation, or simply an analyst's overconfident benchmark. That is why the concept sits so close to both [[Behavioral public policy]] and the philosophy of preference.

## Open questions

When should unstable or self-defeating choice count as evidence of welfare failure? What can replace revealed preference without licensing unconstrained paternalism? And how should behavioral welfare economics distinguish between mistake, conflict in values, and ordinary context sensitivity?
