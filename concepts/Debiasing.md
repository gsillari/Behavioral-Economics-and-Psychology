# Debiasing

## Core idea

[[Debiasing]] is the branch of behavioral economics concerned not only with diagnosing systematic error, but with reducing it. In the current vault, the concept begins from [[Fischhoff 1982 - Debiasing (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 31)]], where Fischhoff treats correction as both an intervention problem and a way of learning what kind of process produced the error in the first place.

The concept matters because it turns behavioral economics from a descriptive critique into a design program. Once a judgment or choice pattern is called biased, the next question is no longer only whether the pattern violates a benchmark. It is whether the pattern can be improved, by whom, and at what cost.

## Key distinctions

Three distinctions organize the concept in the current vault.

First, debiasing is not the same thing as simply identifying bias. Diagnosis comes first, but debiasing asks what follows once a deviation has been found.

Second, [[Soll et al. 2015]] gives the cleanest modern split between interventions that modify the decision maker and interventions that modify the environment. Education, rules, and decision aids belong to the first family. Information design, defaults, and institutional structure belong to the second.

Third, [[Jolls and Sunstein 2006]] shows that institutions can either insulate outcomes from error or try to reduce the error itself. That distinction matters because legal and policy design need not always take bias as fixed.

## Evidence and debate

[[Fischhoff 1982 - Debiasing (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 31)]] is the foundational source because it frames correction as a systematic research problem. If warning, reframing, training, or redesigned tasks reduce an error, that tells us something about the mechanism behind it. [[Nisbett et al. 1982 - Improving Inductive Inference (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 32)]] complements this by showing that some inferential improvements depend on better representations and statistical understanding rather than on exhortation alone.

[[Arkes 1991]] then adds an important complication. Many judgment errors are costs of otherwise adaptive systems, not sheer defects. That means the right debiasing strategy depends on the kind of error involved and on what useful function the underlying process normally performs.

[[Soll et al. 2015]] brings the literature together in a form the vault can use structurally. Its core contribution is the person-versus-environment distinction, along with the related idea of decision readiness. Debiasing succeeds or fails partly because some agents are not in a position to use the corrective information or tools they are given.

[[Jolls and Sunstein 2006]] carries the concept into law and public institutions. It shows that debiasing can be procedural or substantive, and that legal design can try to improve reasoning rather than merely constrain outcomes. At the same time, it makes clear that debiasing through law risks overshooting, manipulation, and new distortions for agents who were not erring to begin with.

## Why it matters for behavioral public policy

The concept matters for [[Behavioral public policy]] because it opens a policy space that is neither simple laissez-faire nor simple paternalistic blocking. Some interventions aim to help citizens judge more accurately or choose more consistently without eliminating choice altogether. That is why debiasing belongs next to nudges, disclosure, risk communication, and institutional design rather than being reduced to any one of them.

It also matters because it sharpens policy evaluation. A debiasing intervention should be judged not only by whether it moves behavior, but by whether it improves judgment quality, avoids manipulation, and respects the comparative competence of citizens and institutions.

## Why it matters for ecological rationality

The concept also matters for [[Ecological rationality]], because the ecological branch complicates any flat idea that debiasing simply means moving people closer to one formal benchmark. [[Arkes 1991]] already points in this direction by treating some errors as the costs of adaptive systems. The Gigerenzer-side ecological program strengthens the warning: in some environments, the right response is not to "correct" a heuristic toward formal optimization, but to improve the fit between agent, cue structure, and task. The Smith-side ecological program adds a different caution: institutions and norms may already embody useful correctives that should not be casually displaced by top-down redesign.

So the debiasing branch and the ecological branch should not be treated as simple enemies. They disagree about what counts as error and about what kind of improvement matters, but they also overlap whenever better environments, better cue structures, or better institutional scaffolds make judgment more reliable.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many real interventions are best understood as debiasing attempts: improved warnings, structured elicitation, checklists, better displays, defaults, decision aids, procedural safeguards, and legally mandated disclosures. The same logic also reaches into newer branches such as AI-supported decision-making, where the question is often not whether a model changes behavior, but whether it improves judgment without creating new distortions.
