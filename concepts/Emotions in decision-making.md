# Emotions in decision-making

## Core idea

[[Emotions in decision-making]] captures the idea that transient emotional and affective states can systematically shape economic choices. In the current vault, the concept is anchored by [[DellaVigna 2009]] as a field-evidence survey, strengthened directly by [[Lerner et al. 2004]], which shows that incidental emotions can alter buying, selling, and choice prices in a controlled valuation setting, and now broadened by [[Loewenstein 2007 - Out of Control - Visceral Influences on Behavior (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 18)]] and [[Loewenstein et al. 2007 - Risk as Feelings (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 19)]], which connect emotion to hot-state motivation and risky choice.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Projection bias]], where current states distort beliefs about future tastes, though the two can overlap. It also differs from [[Visceral influences on behavior]], which includes hunger, craving, pain, and bodily urgency as well as emotion. And it differs from [[Risk as feelings]], which is narrower and focuses on affective responses at the moment of risky choice. [[DellaVigna 2009]] shows that emotional influences are not only laboratory curiosities; they can be observed in real field settings. [[Lerner et al. 2004]] adds an important internal distinction: emotions with the same negative valence can have different economic effects, so the category should not collapse into a generic "bad mood" story.

## Evidence and debate

[[DellaVigna 2009]] highlights evidence that weather-induced mood affects stock returns and college enrollment decisions, while emotionally charged settings can alter short-run violent behavior. The paper also notes that some other behavioral patterns, such as gift exchange and food-related projection bias, may themselves be mediated by emotional states.

[[Lerner et al. 2004]] matters because it gives the current vault a more specific experimental result: disgust and sadness do not simply intensify or reduce valuation uniformly. They reshape buying and selling prices differently, and can even reverse the usual [[Endowment effect]]. [[Loewenstein 2007 - Out of Control - Visceral Influences on Behavior (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 18)]] broadens the mechanism further by arguing that the relevant class of influences is not limited to named emotions. Craving, hunger, pain, and arousal can all overpower cooler plans and therefore belong inside a behavioral account of choice. [[Loewenstein et al. 2007 - Risk as Feelings (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 19)]] then extends the branch to risky choice by arguing that emotional reactions to danger can diverge from cognitive risk assessment and directly steer behavior.

Read together, these sources give the page a cleaner internal structure. [[DellaVigna 2009]] shows field relevance. [[Lerner et al. 2004]] isolates specific emotion effects in valuation. [[Loewenstein 2007 - Out of Control - Visceral Influences on Behavior (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 18)]] explains hot-state motivation more broadly. [[Loewenstein et al. 2007 - Risk as Feelings (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 19)]] connects that affective machinery to decisions under risk.

Debate centers on interpretation: field studies often capture a net effect relative to alternative activities, while lab studies isolate more specific affective channels. A second debate concerns scope. Should behavioral economics treat emotion as one perturbation among others, or as part of a more general theory of motivation and choice?

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because emotional states can influence decisions even when fundamentals are unchanged. For policy and institutional design, that raises questions about timing, context, and whether behavior should be measured in emotionally neutral conditions or in the states where real choices occur. It also matters for law and regulation whenever temporary arousal or mood can predictably distort high-stakes behavior. The Lerner paper adds that even willingness to buy, sell, or accept compensation may vary with incidental emotion. The Loewenstein chapters make the policy stakes broader still: some decisions are distorted not by mild mood but by intense hot states or by fear at the moment of risk.

## Open questions

Which emotional effects are robust in field settings rather than only in controlled experiments? How should policy account for short-run emotional states without treating them as the whole of the person? When do lab and field estimates answer different but equally important policy questions?
