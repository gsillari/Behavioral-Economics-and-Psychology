# Behavioral game theory

## Core idea

[[Behavioral game theory]] is the use of experimental and psychological evidence to revise the behavioral assumptions of [[Game theory]] while retaining the formal representation of strategic interaction. In [[Camerer 2003 - Chapter 01 - Introduction]], Camerer presents it as an extension of analytical game theory, not as an anti-formal replacement.

The key contrast is between games and game theory. A game represents players, strategies, information, order of moves, and payoffs. Analytical game theory derives what players with specified rationality and beliefs would do. Behavioral game theory asks what people actually do, identifies regularities, and then builds those regularities back into models.

## What it adds

Behavioral game theory adds several kinds of behavioral content to standard strategic analysis.

First, players may have richer utility than money-payoff maximization. [[Camerer 2003 - Chapter 02 - Dictator, Ultimatum, and Trust Games]] shows this through [[Social preferences]], [[Fairness]], [[Altruism]], [[Reciprocity]], [[Inequity aversion]], and [[Trust]].

Second, players may have limited strategic reasoning. The beauty-contest example in Camerer's introduction shows that people often reason one or two steps ahead rather than iterating indefinitely to equilibrium.

Third, players learn. In coordination and beauty-contest settings, behavior may begin far from equilibrium and move toward a pattern over time. Learning is therefore not an afterthought; it is often part of the explanation.

Fourth, equilibrium selection may depend on history, salience, communication, analogy, and convention. The continental-divide example shows how groups can converge to different equilibria from small initial differences.

## Relation to rationality

Behavioral game theory occupies a moderate position in the [[Rationality wars]]. It does not say that game theory is false because people deviate from standard predictions. It says that a sharp prediction made from unrealistic assumptions is a useful diagnostic. When the prediction fails regularly, the task is to revise the assumptions: utility, beliefs, reasoning, learning, or the representation of the situation.

This makes behavioral game theory especially important for [[Bounded rationality]]. It treats bounded reasoning as modelable rather than as mere noise.
