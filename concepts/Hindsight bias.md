# Hindsight bias

## Core idea

[[Hindsight bias]] is the tendency to see past outcomes as having been more foreseeable, more obvious, or more inevitable than they actually were before the fact. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Fischhoff 1982 - Heuristics and Biases in Hindsight (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 23)]], which shows that known outcomes reshape retrospective judgment rather than merely updating it.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from simple learning from evidence. In ordinary belief revision, people receive new information and update accordingly. In hindsight bias, by contrast, the known outcome distorts how the earlier uncertainty itself is remembered and assessed. It also differs from [[Overconfidence]], although the two are related: hindsight can make people seem more prescient than they were and can encourage later overconfidence about future prediction.

## Evidence and debate

The anchor chapter argues that once people know what happened, they systematically overstate how predictable it was. That matters because case studies, postmortems, and historical explanations are then judged from a falsely flattened view of past uncertainty. In the current vault, the bias belongs naturally inside [[Nonstandard beliefs]] because it concerns distorted retrospective belief rather than preference or choice alone. It also matters for [[Heuristics and Biases]] because it shows that the classic program did not concern only future judgment, but also the reconstruction of past reasoning.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because institutions constantly evaluate the past: financial crises, wars, policy failures, legal decisions, disasters, and regulatory errors. If hindsight bias is strong, then blame, learning, and reform can all become distorted. Past actors may be judged as if the right path was obvious, while the real informational difficulty of the original decision disappears from view.
