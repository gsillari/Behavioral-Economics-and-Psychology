# Regression to the mean

this is a STUB

## Core idea

[[Regression to the mean]] is the statistical tendency for unusually high or low measurements to be followed by measurements closer to the average when outcomes are noisy. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Barnett et al. 2005]], which explains why this matters for empirical inference and why it can be mistaken for a real effect.

## Key distinctions

Regression to the mean is not the same as a causal response, learning effect, or behavioral correction. It can arise even when nothing meaningful has changed except random measurement error or temporary fluctuation. It also differs from simple mean reversion in economic time series: the concept here is specifically about the inferential danger created when researchers select cases because they are extreme at baseline and then observe less extreme values later.

## Evidence and debate

[[Barnett et al. 2005]] presents the concept as a general problem in repeated-measures research. In the context of this vault, that matters because field evidence in behavioral economics often tracks high-need individuals, unusual outcomes, or intervention targets selected for extreme baseline behavior. The main debate is not whether regression to the mean exists, but how much it threatens substantive interpretation in practice and which design choices are enough to separate real behavioral effects from statistical artifacts.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because behavioral interventions are often evaluated on populations selected for unusually poor outcomes, such as low savers, heavy borrowers, or underperforming groups. Without careful design, later improvement may be attributed to a nudge, reminder, or institutional reform when part of the movement would have happened anyway. That makes regression to the mean important for both empirical credibility and policy legitimacy.
