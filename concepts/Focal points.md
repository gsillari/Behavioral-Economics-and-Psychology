# Focal points

## Core idea

[[Focal points]] are salient solutions that help agents coordinate when a game has more than one possible equilibrium and communication, contracts, or explicit authority do not fully determine what to do. A focal point is not merely what one person likes, notices, or prefers. It is a cue that each person can expect the others to recognize as a plausible basis for joint convergence.

[[Schelling 1960 - Chapter 04 - Toward a Theory of Interdependent Decision]] is the foundational source. Schelling's central insight is that tacit coordination often depends on labels, precedents, places, boundaries, symmetry-breaking details, and shared imagination. These features may look psychologically messy from the standpoint of a stripped-down normal form, but in the actual coordination problem they can be rationally useful because they help players form mutually consistent expectations.

[[Lewis 1969]] is the immediate philosophical successor. Lewis turns Schelling's focal-point insight into a theory of [[Convention]]: a once-focal solution can become a recurrent regularity when people expect others to conform and have conditional reasons to conform because of those expectations.

[[Camerer 2003 - Chapter 07 - Coordination]] adds the experimental unpacking. Matching games, visual assignment games, sender-receiver games, and communication experiments show that focality is structured. It depends on common ground, distinctiveness, recognizability, equality, assignment, precedent, and the absence or presence of rival selection principles.

## What focality is not

Focality is not the same as private salience. In matching experiments, what people pick when they are asked for a personally prominent answer can differ from what they pick when they are trying to coordinate with someone else. The coordination task asks, in effect, "what will we both see as mutually usable?"

Focality is also not the same as [[Common knowledge]]. Common knowledge can make a cue usable, but common knowledge of the payoff table alone does not select an equilibrium. The cue must also say something about which equilibrium should be expected.

Focality is not yet [[Convention]]. A focal point can solve a one-shot tacit coordination problem. A convention is the stabilized, recurrent version of such a solution, embedded in expectations and repeated practice.

## Evidence and debate

Camerer's Chapter 7 shows why focal points should be treated as a serious equilibrium-selection mechanism rather than as an informal anecdote. In pure matching games, subjects coordinate above chance because some answers are mutually prominent. In visual assignment games, equality, accession, and closeness can function as selection rules. In Bacharach and Bernasconi's analysis, cues work when they are distinctive enough to break symmetry but not so subtle that others may miss them.

The evidence also shows limits. External assignment can select an outcome when no stronger rival principle is present, but it weakens when it competes with equality or payoff dominance. Communication can create a focal equilibrium, but in mixed-motive settings it can also reopen distributional conflict. Focality is therefore not a universal solution; it is one family of selection mechanisms among others.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, focal points matter because they connect rational choice to social meaning. Traffic practices, meeting places, bargaining positions, legal defaults, public ceremonies, categories, flags, brands, organizational routines, and linguistic meanings can matter because people expect others to treat them as shared cues.

This is also why focal points belong near institutions. Some institutions work by changing incentives; others work by making one pattern of action salient, credible, and mutually expected. Focal points are one route by which social order can arise before formal enforcement.
