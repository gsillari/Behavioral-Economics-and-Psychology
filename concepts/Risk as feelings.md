# Risk as feelings

## Core idea

[[Risk as feelings]] is the idea that risky choice is often governed by emotional reactions to danger rather than by detached evaluation of probabilities and consequences. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Loewenstein et al. 2007 - Risk as Feelings (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 19)]], which argues that fear, vividness, anticipation, and physiological arousal can shape decisions under risk independently of more deliberative cognitive assessment.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Risk perception]], which is the broader umbrella for how people understand and evaluate hazards. It differs from [[Affect heuristic]], which emphasizes the use of an overall positive or negative feeling as a shortcut for judging risks and benefits. [[Risk as feelings]] is narrower and more process-oriented: it focuses on the emotional reactions experienced at the moment of risky choice and on their divergence from calculated judgment.

## Evidence and debate

[[Loewenstein et al. 2007 - Risk as Feelings (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 19)]] challenges cognitive-consequentialist models that expect stable risk attitudes across contexts. The chapter argues that emotional responses vary with vividness, imaginability, immediacy, and arousal, so risk taking should also vary across domains more than formal models predict. In the current vault, this helps connect [[Risk perception]] and [[Emotions in decision-making]] more tightly. The psychometric tradition associated with [[Slovic 1987]] explains why hazards differ qualitatively in the public mind; the risk-as-feelings perspective explains how those affective reactions can directly steer choice.

The main debate is normative. Are these feelings distortions from better judgment, or do they register morally and politically relevant aspects of risk that purely formal models overlook?

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many public controversies about risk are not fights between probability and ignorance. They are conflicts between analytic assessments and emotionally charged experiences of danger. That matters for medical decisions, financial risks, terrorism, environmental hazards, and crisis communication. For policy, it implies that risk governance cannot treat feeling as an afterthought once analysis is complete.
