# Limited attention

## Core idea

[[Limited attention]] is the idea that decision-makers do not process all available information fully, especially when some attributes are opaque, non-salient, or crowded out by competing stimuli. In the current vault, the anchor sources are [[DellaVigna 2009]], which surveys field evidence on attention failures, [[Madrian 2014]], which links those failures to policy design, and [[Frederick et al. 2009]], which gives a focused consumer-choice example through [[Opportunity cost neglect]].

## Key distinctions

The concept differs from lack of information: people may observe a feature and still underprocess it. It also differs from [[Menu effects]], where the main challenge is choosing from many options, and from [[Framing effects]], where different presentations of equivalent information change decisions. Inattention is fundamentally about salience, cognitive scarcity, and the partial processing of information.

## Evidence and debate

[[DellaVigna 2009]] summarizes field evidence showing underweighting of shipping costs in auctions, non-salient taxes, complex ranking information, earnings news released at distracting times, information about linked firms, and demand shifts far into the future. These studies suggest that salience and competing stimuli matter for what people notice and price into decisions. [[Frederick et al. 2009]] adds a useful leaf case: consumers often fail to represent the other purchases that a focal expenditure displaces unless those outside options are explicitly cued. That makes [[Opportunity cost neglect]] a natural neighboring page rather than a wholly separate branch. Debate continues over whether limited attention should be modeled behaviorally or through more formal notions of rational inattention.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many interventions in [[Behavioral public policy]] aim to redirect attention rather than to change preferences. Reminders, salient disclosures, simplified forms, and choice architecture all operate partly by changing what people notice and process. [[Madrian 2014]] is especially useful here because it shows how policy design can start from the diagnosis that the core friction is attention, not incentives alone.
