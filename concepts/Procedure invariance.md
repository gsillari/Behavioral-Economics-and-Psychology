# Procedure invariance

## Core idea

[[Procedure invariance]] is the principle that normatively equivalent methods of eliciting preference should yield the same underlying ordering or valuation. In the current vault, the anchor sources are [[Slovic 1995]] and [[Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006]], which present violations of this principle as one of the clearest signs that preferences may be [[Constructed preferences|constructed]] rather than simply revealed.

## Key distinctions

This concept is a benchmark principle rather than an empirical phenomenon. [[Preference reversals]] and some forms of [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]] matter because they appear to violate it. Procedure invariance therefore sits at the intersection of descriptive anomaly and normative expectation: it tells us what rational-choice theory expects from elicitation, and the construction-of-preference literature shows where actual behavior diverges.

A concrete example helps. Suppose a person is offered two gambles and prefers gamble A to gamble B when asked to choose between them. If the same person then assigns a higher monetary value to B than to A when asked to price them, procedure invariance has failed. The options themselves have not changed; only the elicitation task has changed. In standard rational-choice terms, that should not reverse the underlying ordering.

## Evidence and debate

[[Slovic 1995]] argues that systematic changes across elicitation procedures are too important to be dismissed as measurement noise. [[Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006]] then places that point inside a broader research program on valuation, context, and response mode. [[Grether and Plott 2006 - Economic Theory of Choice and the Preference Reversal Phenomenon (in Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006, ch. 5)]] matters because it shows how the issue enters economic theory directly. [[Tversky et al. 2006 - Contingent Weighting in Judgment and Choice (in Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006, ch. 6)]] strengthens the mechanism story by arguing that some violations arise because different procedures induce different attribute weights. [[Hsee et al. 2007 - Preference Reversals Between Joint and Separate Evaluations of Options (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 9)]] adds the later evaluability-based extension.

The joint-versus-separate-evaluation result is especially useful pedagogically. Some attributes are hard to assess on their own but easy to compare side by side. In separate evaluation, a person may rely on whatever attribute is most immediately evaluable, while in joint evaluation a previously neglected attribute becomes salient and can reverse the ranking. Debate therefore concerns whether violations of procedure invariance show unstable underlying preference, poor elicitation methods, multi-attribute tradeoffs that are differentially weighted by task, or some combination of these.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because public institutions often need to choose an elicitation procedure. If the procedure helps determine the preference that is expressed, then legal valuation, compensation, cost-benefit analysis, and consumer policy cannot be treated as neutral mirrors of what people already want.

## Open questions

How much violation of procedure invariance should a theory tolerate before it stops being descriptively useful? Are some elicitation procedures normatively superior even if no procedure is fully neutral?
