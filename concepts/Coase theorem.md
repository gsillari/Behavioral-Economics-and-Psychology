# Coase theorem

## Core idea

[[Coase theorem]] is the result associated with [[Coase 1960]] that, in the absence of [[Transaction costs]], bargaining over rights can lead to efficient outcomes regardless of the initial allocation of those rights. In the current vault, it matters as a benchmark for later behavioral and institutional critiques rather than as a literal description of how real policy problems usually work. [[Zaklan 2023]] now gives the page a modern empirical test through the more specific Coasean-independence claim in a carbon market: when trade is sufficiently fluid, final outcomes should be largely independent of the initial allocation of rights.

## Key distinctions

The theorem is often misread as a blanket defense of laissez-faire. In the current vault, the more important distinction is between the idealized zero-transaction-cost case and the real world that Coase actually cared about. The theorem is a limiting benchmark; the broader argument is that once bargaining is costly, rights, institutions, and legal rules matter. That is exactly what makes the page relevant to later behavioral work.

## Evidence and debate

[[Coase 1960]] is the anchor source. The main debate is not whether the theorem is logically coherent, but what follows from it. Some readers take it as showing that law often does not matter much. Others, more faithfully to Coase, take it as showing why law matters whenever bargaining is difficult, information is imperfect, or parties are numerous. In the current vault, that second reading is more useful because it creates a bridge to behavioral and policy-oriented work. [[Zaklan 2023]] adds an empirical application in which the specific Coasean-independence property broadly holds at the sector level, but appears more fragile for smaller firms where trading frictions or behavioral distortions may matter more.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because arguments about regulation, nuisance, environmental harm, and market design often begin by asking whether private bargaining could solve the problem. The theorem provides a benchmark for that question, while also helping explain why policy analysis must look carefully at institutions whenever bargaining is costly or unrealistic.
