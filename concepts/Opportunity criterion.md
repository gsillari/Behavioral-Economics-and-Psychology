# Opportunity criterion

## Core idea

[[Opportunity criterion]] is Sugden's proposal that institutions should be evaluated by the opportunities they create for individuals to engage in mutually beneficial interaction, rather than by how well they maximize a reconstructed welfare function for a chooser's supposed inner rational self. In the current vault, the main sources are [[Sugden 2018]], especially [[Sugden 2018 - Chapter 05 - Opportunity]], with important philosophical setup in [[Sugden 2018 - Chapter 03 - The Contractarian Perspective]] and policy application in [[Sugden 2018 - Chapter 07 - Regulation]].

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from the usual welfare logic in [[Behavioral public policy]]. It does not begin by asking what policy makers should do to help people satisfy their true preferences more fully. It begins by asking whether institutions expand the space of opportunities for voluntary, mutually advantageous cooperation.

It also differs from [[Libertarian paternalism]] and [[Asymmetric paternalism]]. Those views still work largely within a framework in which policy aims to improve outcomes for choosers by their own lights, even if softly or modestly. The opportunity criterion resists that whole reconstruction of latent welfare and offers a different normative benchmark.

## Evidence and debate

[[Sugden 2018 - Chapter 05 - Opportunity]] is the central source because it gives the explicit criterion. [[Sugden 2018 - Chapter 03 - The Contractarian Perspective]] matters because it explains why Sugden thinks a citizen-centered, mutually advantageous framework is normatively superior to a planner's standpoint. [[Sugden 2018 - Chapter 07 - Regulation]] matters because it shows how the criterion bears on actual policy arguments.

The main debate is whether this criterion can really replace behavioral welfare analysis or only constrain it. Supporters can say that it avoids the fictional move from unstable preferences to an inner rational agent. Critics can say that opportunity itself needs interpretation and that policy still has to decide which opportunities matter, for whom, and under what background conditions.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because it offers one of the strongest alternatives in the vault to the idea that behavioral economics naturally points toward paternalist welfare correction. It is especially useful for reading disputes over regulation, consumer protection, and market design. In those settings, the opportunity criterion asks whether policy expands the scope for mutually beneficial action rather than merely whether it corrects individual error.

## Open questions

Can opportunity be measured or compared without collapsing back into ordinary welfare analysis? How much background equality is needed before an opportunity-based defense of markets becomes persuasive? And is the criterion best understood as a replacement for paternalist behavioral policy or as a constraint on it?
