# Reason-based choice

## Core idea

[[Reason-based choice]] is the idea that people often choose by looking for reasons that justify one option over another, rather than by simply consulting a stable pre-existing preference ordering. In the current vault, [[Rabin 1998]] makes this concept important because it belongs to the more radical part of the psychology-and-economics program: some choices are not best modeled as maximizing fixed, coherent, accurately perceived preferences.

The key point is not that reasons are irrational. It is that the availability of reasons can change choice. The same option may become more attractive when the menu, frame, comparison set, or institutional context supplies an intelligible justification for choosing it.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Constructed preferences]], but it belongs inside that larger branch. Constructed preferences says that preferences can be partly formed in the act of choosing. Reason-based choice identifies one mechanism: people search for defensible grounds, such as dominance, compromise, salient attributes, or avoidance of responsibility.

It also differs from ordinary [[Framing effects]]. Framing changes how a decision is represented. Reason-based choice explains why some representations matter: they provide or remove reasons that make one choice feel justified.

It differs from [[Menu effects]] because not all menu effects are reason-based. But compromise effects, attraction effects, deferral, and choice overload often work partly by changing which option has the best available justification.

## Evidence and debate

[[Rabin 1998]] treats reason-based choice as part of the evidence that economics cannot always assume stable preferences waiting to be maximized. People may delay choosing when no option is easy to justify, choose a compromise because it has a defensible middle-ground rationale, or shift choice when a decoy makes one option easier to defend.

Melioration belongs near this concept but does not need its own page yet. The useful idea is that people may improve locally or choose according to short-run comparative reasons without maximizing global welfare. That mechanism helps explain why repeated choices can look purposeful while still failing to implement the agent's larger interests.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because institutional design often changes not only incentives or information, but the reasons available to choosers. Defaults, menus, disclosures, and comparison sets can make some actions feel justified and others feel arbitrary. That is why reason-based choice belongs close to [[Behavioral public policy]] even when it is not explicitly paternalist.

## Open questions

When are reasons evidence of real values, and when are they post-hoc justifications? How should economists model choices when reasons are menu-dependent? Can institutions help people find better reasons without simply steering them toward a designer's preferred option?
