# Internalities

## Core idea

[[Internalities]] are harms that a person's present choices impose on that same person's future self, in a way that is often compared to how externalities describe harms imposed on others. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Madrian 2014]], which uses the concept to explain why present bias and related behavioral frictions can matter for policy analysis even when there is no obvious external harm. [[O'Donoghue and Rabin 1999]] supplies one of the key behavioral mechanisms behind this idea by modeling how present-biased choices can conflict with what the same person would prefer from a more distant temporal standpoint.

## Key distinctions

The concept differs from standard externalities because the relevant conflict is within the person across time rather than between different people. [[Coase 1960]] is useful as the benchmark contrast: standard law and economics focuses on interpersonal conflicts, rights assignment, and [[Transaction costs]], whereas internalities shift attention to conflict within the agent. It also differs from a simple claim that people make mistakes, because internalities matter specifically when current choices predictably undermine future welfare in ways the person may later regret. In the current vault, this makes internalities an important bridge between behavioral evidence and policy design: they help explain why public intervention may be considered even when the primary costs are borne by the chooser's future self.

## Evidence and debate

[[Madrian 2014]] is the current anchor source. The paper uses present bias as the leading example, linking internalities to behavior such as undersaving or unhealthy consumption that creates future costs. [[O'Donoghue and Rabin 1999]] clarifies why this is not just ordinary impatience. Their distinction between immediate costs and immediate rewards shows how the present self can either postpone costly actions that would benefit the future self or indulge too early in tempting actions that impose delayed costs. Their distinction between sophistication and naivete also matters for policy: a sophisticated chooser may demand commitment because she expects future failure, while a naive chooser may repeatedly overestimate future discipline. [[DellaVigna 2009]] provides an important empirical backdrop by surveying field evidence on [[Time inconsistency]] and related frictions that later policy discussions reinterpret through the language of internalities. [[Thaler and Sunstein 2021 - Chapter 04 - When Do We Need a Nudge?]] is a useful neighboring unit because it makes clear that nudges are often most attractive where attention is scarce, self-control is weak, and delayed consequences are difficult to register. [[Sunstein 2025]] extends the concept into the AI setting by arguing that personalized choice engines might help people overcome internalities more effectively than generic nudges or warnings, precisely because they can be tailored to individual vulnerabilities and goals. [[Ludwig et al. 2025]] pushes further by arguing that algorithmic thought partners may help people identify what they themselves would endorse under more considered judgment. That makes [[Internalities]] a natural part of the proposed program in [[Behavioral economics 2.0]], but it also sharpens the same old worry: if future-self protection is personalized by algorithms, who decides what counts as the person's better self? The concept therefore sits close to disputes over paternalism, welfare measurement, and the legitimacy of behaviorally informed intervention.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because it gives [[Behavioral public policy]] a language for discussing self-harming patterns without pretending they fit neatly into the standard externality framework. If internalities are real and important, interventions such as defaults, commitment devices, reminders, or targeted incentives may be justified even when harms to third parties are limited. The concept is especially relevant to savings, health, addiction, and other domains where present-biased choices can have substantial delayed consequences.

## Open questions

When should policy treat a within-person conflict as an internality rather than as a legitimate preference? How can institutions identify future welfare losses without overstepping into paternalistic overconfidence? Which interventions address internalities most effectively while preserving agency?
