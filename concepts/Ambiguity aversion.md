# Ambiguity aversion

## Core idea

[[Ambiguity aversion]] is the tendency to prefer options with known probabilities over otherwise similar options whose probabilities are unknown or ill specified. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Ellsberg 1961]], but the benchmark being challenged should now be stated more precisely. Ellsberg pressures the uncertainty-side classical benchmark collected under [[Subjective expected utility theory]], whose canonical source is [[Savage 1954]].

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from standard [[Risk attitudes|risk aversion]]. Risk aversion concerns dislike of variance when probabilities are known. Ambiguity aversion concerns discomfort with uncertainty about the probabilities themselves.

It also differs from a simple mistake in calculation. The point of the Ellsberg problem is not only that people compute badly. It is that they may reject the assumption that every uncertain situation can be represented by one precise personal probability function and then evaluated through expected utility. In the current vault, that assumption is now anchored explicitly in [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 02 - Preliminary Considerations on Decision in the Face of Uncertainty]] and [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 03 - Personal Probability]].

[[Stefansson 2022 - The Economics and Philosophy of Risk (in Heilmann and Reiss 2022, ch. 3)]] is especially useful because it keeps separate risk, uncertainty, and unawareness rather than treating them as interchangeable.

## Evidence and debate

[[Ellsberg 1961]] introduces canonical choice problems in which many people prefer known-risk urns to ambiguous urns. In the current vault, the importance of those problems is now more precise than it used to be. They challenge the Savage benchmark for uncertainty, especially the idea that uncertain acts can be represented through one coherent personal probability structure that is ready for choice.

[[Kahneman and Tversky 1982 - Variants of Uncertainty (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 35)]] broadens the context by arguing that uncertainty itself is psychologically heterogeneous and may not fit one single scale of belief. The debate is therefore not only whether people are biased. It is whether the benchmark is too strong, whether ambiguity sensitivity is a rational response to second-order uncertainty, or whether it marks a limit in the intended scope of classical decision theory.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many important choices involve ambiguity rather than measurable risk: novel technologies, macroeconomic shocks, geopolitical crises, and public-health emergencies often lack stable known probabilities. For PPE students, ambiguity aversion matters because it links economics, political judgment, and philosophy of uncertainty.

It also matters because it sharpens a benchmark question. If policy analysts assume that rational agents should always act as if one precise personal probability is available, then ambiguity-averse behavior looks defective. If that requirement is too strong, the same behavior may instead look like appropriate caution.
