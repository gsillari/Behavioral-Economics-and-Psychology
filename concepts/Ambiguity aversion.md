# Ambiguity aversion

## Core idea

[[Ambiguity aversion]] is the tendency to prefer options with known probabilities over otherwise similar options whose probabilities are unknown or ill specified. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Ellsberg 1961]], which shows that many agents distinguish sharply between risk and ambiguity rather than treating both as ordinary expected-value problems.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from standard [[Risk attitudes|risk aversion]]. Risk aversion concerns dislike of variance when probabilities are known, whereas ambiguity aversion concerns dislike of uncertainty about the probabilities themselves. [[Stefansson 2022 - The Economics and Philosophy of Risk (in Heilmann and Reiss 2022, ch. 3)]] is especially useful on this point because it keeps separate risk, uncertainty, and unawareness rather than treating them as interchangeable. It also differs from simpler mistakes in calculation: the point of the Ellsberg problem is not only that people compute badly, but that they may reject the assumption that every uncertain situation can be represented by one determinate subjective probability distribution. In the current vault, ambiguity aversion therefore sits at the boundary between [[Subjective probability]], formal rationality, and the broader family of pages collected under [[Nonstandard beliefs]].

## Evidence and debate

[[Ellsberg 1961]] introduces canonical choice problems in which many people prefer known-risk urns to ambiguous urns in ways that violate the standard Savage-style extension of expected utility. [[Kahneman and Tversky 1982 - Variants of Uncertainty (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 35)]] usefully broadens that context by arguing that uncertainty itself is psychologically heterogeneous and may not always be representable along a single scale of belief. The debate is whether this reveals a behavioral anomaly, a limitation of subjective expected utility, or a sensible response to second-order uncertainty. In the current vault, the page matters because it broadens the rationality debate: departures from formal theory are not only about inconsistent preferences or biased heuristics, but also about whether agents should be expected to behave as if probabilities were always sharp and choice-ready.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many important choices involve ambiguity rather than measurable risk: novel technologies, macroeconomic shocks, geopolitical crises, and public-health emergencies often lack stable known probabilities. For PPE students, ambiguity aversion matters because it links economics, political judgment, and philosophy of uncertainty. Institutions that ignore ambiguity may overstate the precision of their forecasts, while institutions that overreact to it may become excessively cautious. [[Kahneman and Tversky 1982 - Variants of Uncertainty (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 35)]] sharpens that lesson by suggesting that the psychological experience of uncertainty is itself plural, which makes institutional responses harder to standardize. Behavioral public policy therefore has to decide whether ambiguity aversion is something to correct, respect, or strategically accommodate.
