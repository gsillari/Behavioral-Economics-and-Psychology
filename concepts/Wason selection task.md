# Wason selection task

## Core idea

The [[Wason selection task]] is a conditional-reasoning task in which subjects must decide which cards to turn over to test a rule of the form "if P, then Q." The logically correct test is to inspect P and not-Q, because only a case with P and not-Q can falsify the rule. In the classic abstract version, subjects often choose P and Q instead. They look for the named antecedent and a confirming consequent, while failing to look for the falsifying consequent.

In the current vault, the task matters because it sits at the crossroads of [[Confirmation bias]], [[Rationality wars]], [[Evolutionary psychology]], and [[Fast and slow thinking]]. It is not just one more bias experiment. It is a portable diagnostic for asking what counts as good reasoning, what kind of task people think they are solving, and whether poor performance on abstract formal tasks licenses a broad conclusion about human irrationality.

## Key distinctions

The first distinction is between falsification and verification. [[Wason 1968]] treats the main error as failure to search for the potentially falsifying case. This gives the task its link to [[Confirmation bias]], but the connection should be handled carefully: the task is about conditional rule testing, not simply about defending a cherished belief.

The second distinction is between abstract conditional reasoning and content-sensitive reasoning. Abstract letter-number versions usually produce poor performance. Social-contract versions can produce much better performance. That contrast is central to [[Cosmides and Tooby 2000]], which interprets it as evidence for domain-specific cheater-detection machinery.

The third distinction is between logical correctness and adaptive or functional correctness. In some social-contract variants, the cards that identify potential cheaters do not map neatly onto the logically correct P and not-Q answer. Cosmides and Tooby argue that subjects often track the benefit-taken and requirement-not-met structure rather than the content-free logical form.

The fourth distinction is between dual-process explanation and rationality evaluation. A simple dual-process story might treat the wrong response as intuitive and the correct response as reflective. But the selection-task literature complicates that. Social-contract solutions may feel immediate and intuitive while still being functionally apt. Conversely, reflection can fail to repair the abstract task. The task therefore belongs near [[Fast and slow thinking]] without being reducible to the slogan that slow thought is rational and fast thought is biased.

## Evidence and debate

[[Wason 1968]] gives the core experimental finding. Subjects were asked to test conditional rules and repeatedly tended to select matching or confirming cases. Attempts to induce falsification-focused "therapy" did not reliably produce the contrapositive inference. Wason interpreted the pattern as a mental set toward truth, correspondence, and matching, and as a challenge to simple assumptions about adult formal-operational reasoning.

[[Cosmides and Tooby 2000]] gives the major evolutionary reinterpretation. They argue that the task becomes much easier when it is framed as a social contract and the subject must detect a cheater: someone who takes a benefit without meeting the requirement. They treat this not as a generic content effect but as evidence for specialized social-reasoning machinery shaped by the adaptive problem of reciprocal exchange.

The debate is therefore not only whether people are good or bad at logic. It is whether the right explanatory level is formal benchmark failure, pragmatic interpretation, domain-specific adaptation, dual-process architecture, or some combination. That is why the task became a classic object in the wider rationality debate.

## Why it matters here

For the behavioral-economics wiki, the Wason task is useful because it sharpens the bridge from psychology of reasoning to economics-facing rationality debates. It shows how a simple experimental result can support different philosophical conclusions depending on the benchmark. A Kahneman-Tversky-style reading emphasizes systematic bias and failure of formal norms. An evolutionary-psychology reading emphasizes specialized competence in adaptively structured domains. A dual-process reading asks which processes generate, monitor, or correct the response.

The task also helps avoid a common teaching mistake. It is tempting to cite it as a flat proof that people are irrational. The better lesson is more interesting: people may fail an abstract formal task, improve sharply with meaningful social content, and still leave open the question of which standard of rationality is appropriate.

## Open questions

Should the task be taught primarily as evidence for confirmation bias, as a case of pragmatic task interpretation, or as an entry point into the rationality wars? How much of the social-contract effect should be attributed to evolved cheater-detection machinery rather than permission schemas, familiarity, or deontic reasoning? And how should later dual-process theory explain a task in which intuitive processing can look both biased and impressively domain-sensitive?
