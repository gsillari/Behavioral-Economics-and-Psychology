# Asymmetric paternalism

## Core idea

[[Asymmetric paternalism]] is the policy principle that regulations should be designed to help people who are prone to systematic decision errors while imposing little or no cost on people who are already choosing well. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Camerer et al. 2003]], which presents the idea as a disciplined middle position between blanket anti-paternalism and broad, intrusive paternalism. The page now sits next to [[Libertarian paternalism]], which offers a broader defense of welfare-oriented choice architecture.

## Key distinctions

The concept differs from simple paternalism because it does not assume that any detected irrationality justifies coercive correction. It also differs from laissez-faire responses that treat all intervention as an objectionable intrusion on choice. The core distinction is distributive within the target population: a good policy should substantially benefit those likely to err without meaningfully burdening those who are informed, self-controlled, or otherwise acting in line with their own interests. Relative to [[Libertarian paternalism]] and [[Nudge]], asymmetric paternalism is narrower and more demanding. It is not only a defense of steering; it is a test for whether a particular intervention, including a nudge, is modest enough to justify.

## Evidence and debate

[[Camerer et al. 2003]] is the current anchor source, and [[Sunstein and Thaler 2003]] is now an important neighboring source because it frames the broader case for [[Libertarian paternalism]]. Camerer and coauthors do not offer a new experiment; instead they translate behavioral-economics findings into a policy-evaluation criterion. The authors draw on work about bounded rationality, self-control failures, and status quo effects, then argue that some interventions in areas such as retirement savings, consumer protection, and family law can be defended precisely because they are low-cost for rational actors and high-value for those prone to predictable mistakes. [[Thaler and Sunstein 2021 - Chapter 04 - When Do We Need a Nudge?]] is a useful later neighboring unit because it asks a closely related practical question from inside the nudge tradition itself: under which conditions are people most likely to need help, and how can policy architecture help without doing damage? Debate enters immediately, however, because the framework depends on contested judgments about welfare, error, and the competence of regulators, and because some readers may see it as a narrower offshoot of libertarian paternalism rather than a distinct principle. [[Sugden 2018]] sharpens that criticism by arguing that even modest paternalist interventions can still smuggle in an objectionable normative move: the replacement of actual choice with the supposed preferences of an inner rational agent. On that view, asymmetric paternalism constrains intervention, but does not fully escape the benchmark problem.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because it gives [[Behavioral public policy]] a concrete design standard. Rather than asking only whether a behavior departs from an ideal benchmark, asymmetric paternalism asks how institutions should respond to that departure. It is especially relevant to low-burden tools such as defaults, disclosures, framing choices, or opt-out structures that can improve outcomes for some people without forcing large sacrifices on everyone else. In the current vault, it is the best page for distinguishing a general defense of behaviorally informed steering from a more tightly disciplined test for when such steering is justified.

## Open questions

How should policy makers measure the costs imposed on people who are already choosing well? Can asymmetric paternalism remain genuinely modest once regulators begin defining long-run interests on behalf of citizens? Which kinds of interventions best satisfy the principle in practice, and when does the broader language of [[Libertarian paternalism]] become too permissive?
