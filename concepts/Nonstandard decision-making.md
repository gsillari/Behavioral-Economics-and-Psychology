# Nonstandard decision-making

to be REVIEWED

## Core idea

[[Nonstandard decision-making]] refers to departures from the standard assumption that people process given preferences and beliefs in a fully rational way. In the current vault, the anchor sources are [[Rabin 1998]], which gives the branch its broad psychology-and-economics placement, [[DellaVigna 2009]], which groups field evidence under [[Framing effects]], [[Limited attention]], [[Menu effects]], interpersonal influence, and [[Emotions in decision-making]], [[Arkes and Blumer 1985]], which adds the classic case of the [[Sunk cost effect]], [[Thaler 1985]], which adds the broader coding framework of [[Mental accounting]], [[Frederick et al. 2009]], which adds the more specific consumer mechanism of [[Opportunity cost neglect]], and [[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]], which places the whole branch inside a wider review of choice modeling, repeated decisions, and interventions meant to improve decision quality.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Nonstandard preferences]] and [[Nonstandard beliefs]].
The point here is not that people want different things or believe the wrong probabilities, but that they simplify, ignore, frame, or react to decisions in ways that alter choice even when preferences and beliefs are held fixed.
In this sense, it sits closest to the procedural side of [[Heuristics and Biases]].
It is the part of the behavioral field where presentation, salience, attention, and decision architecture do the most work.
[[Reason-based choice]] now belongs in this branch as the clearest Rabin-style mechanism: a decision can change when the menu or frame changes which option has a defensible reason.

## Evidence and debate

[[DellaVigna 2009]] shows that procedural distortions appear in field settings as well as in the lab.
People underweight non-salient information, rely on heuristics when choosing from large menus, respond too strongly to interested advisors or social pressure, and make different decisions under different emotional states or frames.
[[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] matters as background because it gives this branch its earlier judgment-under-uncertainty vocabulary.
[[Arkes and Blumer 1985]] adds another core pattern: people often let irrecoverable past investments shape current choice through the [[Sunk cost effect]], even though those costs are normatively irrelevant to the present decision.
[[Thaler 1985]] adds a wider architecture for the branch by arguing that people do not evaluate outcomes in one fully integrated account. Through [[Mental accounting]], the paper shows how coding, grouping, and local budgeting can shape the experienced value of economically equivalent outcomes. [[Frederick et al. 2009]] adds a narrower but very teachable mechanism: even when choosers can compute a price difference, they often fail to represent the outside goods that the expenditure displaces unless those alternatives are explicitly cued. That makes [[Opportunity cost neglect]] a useful link between [[Limited attention]] and consumer-choice framing.
[[Madrian 2014]] matters because it turns these procedural frictions into policy-relevant intervention margins.
[[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]] adds a later decision-science frame by showing that this branch is not only about one-shot biases. It also includes how choices are modeled, how parameter estimates vary with task design, how description-based and experience-based choices differ, and how institutions can use feedback, training, or redesigned choice environments to improve decision performance.
[[Rabin 1998]] adds a deeper interpretation of this branch by showing that some departures are not simply failures to process preferences and beliefs. In reason-based choice, melioration, and related phenomena, the decision process helps determine what is chosen by supplying reasons, local comparisons, or short-run improvement paths.

The main debate is whether these effects should be understood as general cognitive limits, context-specific shortcuts, or rational economizing on attention and computation.
In the current vault, this page should therefore remain closely tied both to the DellaVigna field-evidence branch and to the policy branch that asks how institutions should react once the main friction is identified.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many interventions in [[Behavioral public policy]] operate precisely by changing the decision environment rather than changing incentives directly. If the main problem is framing, salience, menu complexity, persuasion, or emotion, then policy design and institutional design may work through presentation, timing, reminders, simplification, or defaults. [[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]] widens that point by showing that interventions can also target calibration, diagnostic judgment, and communication quality rather than only consumer-facing choice architecture.

## Open questions

When should nonstandard decision-making be modeled as bounded rationality rather than as irrationality? Which procedural distortions survive experience and repetition in the field? How should policy respond when decision errors are predictable but heterogeneous across populations?
