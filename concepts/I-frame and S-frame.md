# I-frame and S-frame

## Core idea

[[I-frame and S-frame]] names a distinction between two ways of framing public problems and their solutions. The `i-frame` treats the individual as the primary site of both failure and remedy: people smoke too much, save too little, eat badly, or pollute carelessly, so policy should help or steer them toward better choices. The `s-frame` treats the system as primary: rules, incentives, institutions, market structure, norms, and organizational design create the environment in which those behaviors arise. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Chater and Loewenstein 2023]].

The distinction matters because it changes what counts as a plausible behavioral intervention. On the `i-frame`, nudges, reminders, prompts, and other choice-architecture tools naturally move to the foreground. On the `s-frame`, regulation, taxation, market redesign, organizational processes, and broader institutional change become more central.

## Key distinctions

The first distinction is with [[Nudge]]. Nudges are one family of `i-frame`-friendly tools, but the `i-frame`/`s-frame` distinction is broader than the nudge debate. It is about how policy problems are described and where change is sought.

The second distinction is with [[Libertarian paternalism]]. Libertarian paternalism offers one justificatory framework for steering individuals while leaving options open. The `i-frame`/`s-frame` distinction asks a prior question: should the relevant policy response focus on individuals at all, or should it focus on the system in which they act?

The third distinction is with a simple coercion/non-coercion contrast. The `s-frame` is not just a call for harsher or more coercive policy. It is a call to attend to the structure of institutions, markets, and rules rather than assuming that the main policy lever is individual correction.

## Evidence and debate

[[Chater and Loewenstein 2023]] is the clearest statement of the distinction in the current vault. The paper argues that an overemphasis on individual-level solutions has led [[Behavioral public policy]] astray by crowding out system-level reforms and sometimes even serving corporate interests.

[[Loewenstein and Chater 2017]] is an important precursor. It does not yet make the `i-frame`/`s-frame` distinction explicit, but it argues that nudges occupy only one region of a much larger policy space that includes hybrid and more traditional interventions.

[[Hallsworth 2023]] converges on part of the same lesson from a more programmatic angle. Rather than attacking nudges directly, Hallsworth argues that applied behavioral science should become broader in scope, see systems more clearly, and move beyond the image of behavioral science as a specialist tool for isolated tweaks.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because it helps organize one of the main current disputes inside behavioral policy. The issue is no longer just whether nudges work, or whether they are legitimate. It is whether the field has overlearned the lesson that small individual-level interventions are the natural or politically easiest application of behavioral science.

For PPE students, the distinction is especially useful because it reconnects behavioral policy to political economy. It highlights how firms, regulators, lobbyists, and institutional structures shape behavior, and how the framing of a problem can itself shift responsibility away from or toward systemic reform.

## Open questions

When does an `i-frame` intervention genuinely complement system reform, and when does it distract from it? Can behavioral science contribute to `s-frame` redesign without becoming too diffuse or too technocratic? And should the current vault treat the `i-frame`/`s-frame` distinction as a stable concept in its own right, or mainly as a debate-organizing tool for the policy branch?
