# Tragedy of the commons

## Core idea

The [[Tragedy of the commons]] is the failure pattern that can arise when a resource is open to individual use, each user captures the full benefit of additional use, and the cost of overuse is spread across the group. In [[Hardin 1968]], the classic example is a shared pasture: each herder gains by adding another animal, while the damage from overgrazing is distributed across all herders. If all act on the same individually rational incentive, the common pasture is ruined.

The important point is structural. The tragedy does not require irrationality, greed, or ignorance. It can arise because the institutional arrangement gives each actor a reason to impose part of the cost of her action on others. This makes the concept a bridge between [[Free riding]], [[Cooperation]], [[Public goods game|public-goods games]], externalities, and institutional design.

[[Ostrom 2008]] turns this from a universal parable into a conditional theory. Hardin's prediction is strongest when a [[Common-pool resources|common-pool resource]] is governed as open access: users cannot communicate, rights and duties are undefined, monitoring is weak, and no legitimate rule-making system exists. But Ostrom shows that common-pool resources are not automatically open-access resources. They may be governed by private property, state property, common property, or hybrid arrangements. The real question is therefore not whether commons must fail, but which institutional conditions make failure likely.

## What the concept adds to the cooperation branch

The cooperation branch already contains laboratory public-goods problems: people can benefit from a group good while withholding their own contribution. The tragedy of the commons adds an environmental and institutional version of the same family of problems. Instead of asking whether subjects contribute to a group account, it asks how open-access rules shape behavior toward grazing land, fisheries, parks, air, water, and waste sinks.

The concept also sharpens a distinction. A public-goods problem often concerns undercontribution to a beneficial shared project. A commons problem often concerns overuse or degradation of a shared resource. They are mirror images in many settings: people may contribute too little to maintaining a resource and take too much from it.

## From open-access tragedy to common-pool governance

Hardin's phrase "mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon" captures the institutional thrust of the paper. If appeals to conscience are too weak, then the solution may require enforceable social arrangements: property rights, quotas, taxes, usage permits, queues, regulation, monitoring, or other collective rules. In the current wiki, this connects the concept to [[Behavioral public policy]] because the policy question is not simply how to persuade individuals to be better, but how to alter the structure in which their incentives operate.

[[Ostrom 2008]] adds the corrective. Centralized control and privatization are not panaceas; both can fail when rules are corrupt, poorly matched to local conditions, or destructive of existing institutions. Conversely, local users can sometimes create boundaries, rules, monitoring, sanctions, and maintenance duties that make cooperation stable. The second-level dilemma is important: making rules is itself a public good, so self-governance is not automatic. But it is empirically possible, especially where users have secure tenure, low communication costs, relatively aligned interests, and feasible agreement and enforcement mechanisms.

The concept should therefore be read historically. Hardin supplies the founding failure pattern. Ostrom begins the comparative institutional research program: when do open-access incentives dominate, and when can communication, rules, and self-governance change the game?

## Normative caution

Hardin applies the commons argument to population growth and reproductive freedom. That application is historically central to the paper but normatively controversial. For this wiki, the reusable concept is the open-access incentive structure and the problem of institutional design. Hardin's population-control argument should remain visible as part of the source's history, but it should not define the whole concept.
