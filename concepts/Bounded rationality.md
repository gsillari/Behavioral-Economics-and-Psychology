# Bounded rationality

## Core idea

[[Bounded rationality]] is the idea that rational choice must be modeled in a way that is compatible with the information, search capacities, attention, and computational limits that real agents actually have. In the current vault, the anchor source is now [[Simon 1955]], and that matters because the concept is strongest when treated as a foundational revision of the benchmark rather than as a late legal or policy application.

In that original spirit, bounded rationality is not just the claim that people make mistakes. It is the claim that the standard image of fully informed, fully calculating optimization is the wrong starting point for understanding human choice.

[[Petracca 2017]] now makes an important background point explicit. Simon's project was not only a realistic economics of finite minds. It was also built on a specific psychology, namely [[Cognitivism]] as symbolic information processing. That matters because later arguments over [[Situated cognition]] and [[Embodied cognition]] are not external to bounded rationality. They challenge its original cognitive foundation.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Time inconsistency]], where the main problem is failure of self-control over time, and from [[Social preferences]], where the main issue is that people care about fairness, reciprocity, or other-regarding motives. It also differs from [[Heuristics and Biases]], which is a more specific empirical research program organized around systematic departures from probabilistic and statistical norms.

[[Bounded rationality]] is broader and earlier than that research program. It is the umbrella under which limits of search, attention, inference, framing, and computation can be grouped. It also differs from [[Ecological rationality]], which often argues that simple decision rules should be judged by environmental fit rather than by deviation from formal norms. Simon is closer to a realism about finite agents than to a catalog of biases or a full ecological defense of heuristics.

## Evidence and debate

[[Simon 1955]] provides the foundational move. The paper argues that rational choice should be compatible with the alternatives an agent actually considers, the information the agent can actually access, and the computations the agent can actually perform. Search, aspiration levels, satisficing, and dynamic adjustment therefore become central parts of the analysis of rational choice itself.

[[Simon 1956]] adds an important bridge. It shows that realistic rationality depends not only on internal limits but also on the structure of the environment that the organism confronts. That matters because it pushes bounded rationality toward a more relational and environment-sensitive view without fully collapsing it into later [[Ecological rationality]].

[[Simon 1979]] then provides the mature statement of the project. It argues that economics should take actual decision procedures inside organizations seriously and should test its theories against the behavior of finite agents rather than treating procedural realism as optional. That matters because it shows bounded rationality not as a narrow correction to maximization theory but as part of a broader redefinition of what economic explanation should look like.

[[Petracca 2017]] adds a different and now indispensable clarification. The history of bounded rationality is also a history of plural interpretations. Petracca distinguishes a wider `Simon 1` version, associated with the two blades of the scissors and with adaptive fit between agent and environment, from a narrower `Simon 2` version in which agents are mainly limited information processors. His argument is that the narrower version is what mostly travelled into economics. The paper also argues that later bounded-rationality research, including both the heuristics-and-biases and ecological branches, has not seriously absorbed the later cognitive-science turn toward [[Situated cognition]] and [[Embodied cognition]]. In that sense, bounded rationality remains more deeply cognitivist than the vault had previously made explicit.

[[Smith 1991]] adds a different and useful extension of the Simonian branch. The paper does not deny individual cognitive limits. Instead it argues that one should not infer too quickly from those limits to failure at the level of institutions. Experimental markets can generate substantively rational outcomes even when participants do not understand the equilibrium theory describing them. In the current vault, that matters as a reminder that bounded rationality can coexist with institutionally mediated rationality rather than simply replacing rational outcomes with error.

[[Kahneman 2003]] adds a later psychological map of how bounded rationality appears in judgment and choice, linking the concept to intuitive processing, framing, and [[Attribute substitution]]. In that sense, Kahneman helps translate Simon's general benchmark shift into the later language of behavioral economics. [[Laibson and List 2015]] adds a useful didactic compression of that move for the classroom: people try to optimize, but they sometimes fail in predictable ways, and experience helps explain who fails less often. That is not as deep as Simon's foundation, but it is a good teaching restatement of why the benchmark needs revision at all.

From there, the concept radiates outward into several later branches of the vault, especially [[Heuristics and Biases]], [[Limited attention]], [[Framing effects]], [[Behavioral law and economics]], and the broader question of whether institutions should be designed for optimizing agents or for finite, searching, satisficing ones. Those later uses matter, but they should be read as downstream extensions of the Simonian core rather than as part of the concept's foundation.

The main debate is how to interpret the concept. One reading treats bounded rationality as evidence of deviation from formal norms. Another treats it as a more realistic and more humane account of what rational agency can look like under real constraints. Petracca adds a further debate that the vault now needs to keep in view: is bounded rationality still a symbolic-information-processing research program, or should it be rethought in light of post-cognitivist views of mind? Later branches such as [[Ecological rationality]] and the wider [[Rationality wars]] can be read as partly extending, partly contesting, and partly reframing that original Simonian move.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because legal and policy institutions often rely on assumptions about comprehension, foresight, search, and comparison that are stronger than real agents can satisfy. If people are boundedly rational, then disclosures, menus, defaults, warnings, and procedural protections may need to be redesigned. In the current vault, that is why bounded rationality sits so close to [[Behavioral law and economics]] and [[Behavioral public policy]].

It also matters more broadly because it is one of the background concepts against which many later pages in the wiki should be read. Even when a later paper focuses on bias, framing, or welfare, Simon's question remains in the background: what should rationality mean for agents who are not ideal calculators?

## Open questions

When should bounded rationality be modeled as a stable cognitive limitation and when as an adaptive response to complexity? How much of later behavioral economics is genuinely Simonian, and how much represents a different project built on a related dissatisfaction with idealized rationality? Which legal tools respond best to bounded rationality without becoming manipulative? And should the vault treat bounded rationality primarily as a rival standard of rationality or as a family of realistic constraints on any standard?
