# Time inconsistency

## Core idea

[[Time inconsistency]] is the phenomenon in which a person's preferences over future actions change as the future becomes the present. In the current vault, this is the main concept page for the self-control branch. The anchor sources are [[Thaler 1980]], which gives an early economics-facing treatment of precommitment and planner-doer conflict, [[Frederick et al. 2002]], which gives the branch its strongest broad review of discounted utility and intertemporal anomalies, [[O'Donoghue and Rabin 1999]], which gives the branch a sharp formal treatment of present bias, procrastination, preproperation, and sophistication versus naivete, [[Loewenstein 2007 - The Fall and Rise of Psychological Explanations in the Economics of Intertemporal Choice (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 3)]], which gives the branch a richer intellectual history, [[Loewenstein and Prelec 2007 - Anomalies in Intertemporal Choice - Evidence and an Interpretation (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 15)]], which gives the strongest formal anomaly-based reconstruction of the branch, [[Loewenstein 2007 - Out of Control - Visceral Influences on Behavior (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 18)]], which supplies a major motivational mechanism, [[Jolls et al. 1998]], which imports the behavioral-law-and-economics vocabulary of bounded willpower for the same general family of problems, [[DellaVigna 2009]], which surveys field evidence on self-control problems, and [[Madrian 2014]], which shows how these problems affect policy design through ideas such as [[Internalities]].

## Key distinctions

The concept differs from ordinary impatience. A time-consistent impatient person may prefer early consumption to later consumption, but she does not reverse her plan when the decision point arrives. Time inconsistency instead describes dynamic preference reversal, often associated with present bias or quasi-hyperbolic discounting. It also differs from [[Projection bias]], where the problem is a mistaken forecast of future tastes rather than a change in the weighting of present versus future payoffs. And it differs from [[Visceral influences on behavior]], which names one powerful mechanism through which hot states can generate these reversals.

In the current vault, this page also absorbs what had previously been treated separately as bounded willpower. That older term remains useful for reading legal and policy sources such as [[Jolls et al. 1998]], but it does not need a separate full concept page alongside [[Time inconsistency]].

## Evidence and debate

[[Thaler 1980]] matters because it treats precommitment as a rational response to intrapersonal conflict rather than as an oddity. It links self-control problems to saving, education, exercise, addiction, and voluntary constraints on future choice. [[Frederick et al. 2002]] then provides the broad synthetic review the branch needed. It shows that discounted utility faces not one isolated counterexample but a family of anomalies, and it insists that economists distinguish pure time preference from the many other forces that shape intertemporal choice. [[Loewenstein 2007 - The Fall and Rise of Psychological Explanations in the Economics of Intertemporal Choice (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 3)]] deepens that branch by arguing that behavioral work on intertemporal choice is not merely a modern anomaly literature. It is also a recovery of older, psychologically richer ways of thinking about utility and choice over time.

[[O'Donoghue and Rabin 1999]] gives the self-control branch one of its cleanest formal cores. It starts from present bias and then separates two cases that a generic impatience story blurs. With immediate-cost activities, present bias produces procrastination: the person waits when she should act. With immediate-reward activities, present bias can produce preproperation: the person acts when she should wait. The paper also makes sophistication and naivete analytically central. Sophisticated agents anticipate future self-control failure; naive agents overestimate future discipline. That distinction matters because sophistication can mitigate procrastination, but it can also worsen immediate-reward problems by inducing earlier indulgence once the agent anticipates future weakness.

[[Loewenstein and Prelec 2007 - Anomalies in Intertemporal Choice - Evidence and an Interpretation (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 15)]] adds the branch's strongest formal argument. It shows that discounted utility faces patterned anomalies analogous to the ones that pressured expected utility under risk, and it proposes a prospect-theory-like reconstruction of intertemporal choice using reference dependence and a gain-loss value function. That matters because it shows that time inconsistency is not only an empirical embarrassment for the standard model. It is also a site of constructive model-building.

[[Loewenstein 2007 - Out of Control - Visceral Influences on Behavior (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 18)]] then adds something the thinner discounting story lacks: a concrete motivational mechanism. Hot states such as craving, pain, hunger, and strong affect can crowd out longer-run goals and make the gap between plan and action intelligible in psychological rather than purely formal terms. [[Jolls et al. 1998]] treats these same problems as an early reason to revise legal and policy analysis away from the standard model. [[DellaVigna 2009]] makes self-control problems a central part of field-based behavioral economics. The evidence includes health-club contracts, self-imposed deadlines, credit-card borrowing, and retirement savings choices, all of which suggest demand for commitment or predictable divergence between plans and actions. [[Madrian 2014]] then shows how these patterns matter for intervention design once the problem is treated as practically important rather than theoretically marginal.

[[Laibson and List 2015]] is especially useful as a teaching source because it turns this whole branch into one crisp classroom principle: people have self-control problems. The examples in that note are elementary, but that is precisely their value for the current vault. They make the branch easy to introduce without flattening its deeper architecture.

A major debate, made especially clear by [[O'Donoghue and Rabin 1999]], concerns sophistication versus naivete: some people seek commitment because they understand their own inconsistency, while others overestimate future discipline and therefore mispredict their own behavior. A second debate concerns explanation: should time inconsistency be modeled mainly as formal present bias, or should the branch give more weight to psychologically thicker accounts built around conflict, temptation, and hot-state motivation?

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because it gives a behavioral rationale for commitment devices, defaults, reminders, cooling-off periods, and other interventions aimed at helping people follow through on their own plans. In [[Behavioral public policy]], time inconsistency is one of the clearest channels through which current choices can generate [[Internalities]] for the future self. The Loewenstein chapters also make clear that some of these problems are best understood not only as misweighted time but as mismanaged motivation.

## Open questions

How often are commitment devices actually welfare-improving rather than merely paternalistic? When do learning and experience reduce time inconsistency, and when do they fail to do so? How should policy distinguish stable preference change from self-control failure?
