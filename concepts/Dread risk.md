# Dread risk

## Core idea

[[Dread risk]] refers to low-probability, high-consequence events that exert disproportionate psychological force because they are catastrophic, vivid, and feared. In the current vault, the broader conceptual anchor is now [[Slovic 1987]], while [[Gigerenzer 2004]] uses the aftermath of September 11 to show how dread can shift behavior in harmful ways.

## Key distinctions

The concept is closely related to [[Availability heuristic]] and [[Affect heuristic]], but it is not identical to either. Availability emphasizes recall and salience, affect emphasizes evaluative feeling, and dread risk names a class of hazards that reliably amplify both.

## Evidence and debate

[[Slovic 1987]] matters because it helps explain why some hazards are experienced as especially fearsome in the first place: they score highly on dimensions such as catastrophic potential, uncontrollability, and unknownness within [[Risk perception]]. [[Gigerenzer 2004]] then argues that avoidance of a dreaded risk can increase exposure to more familiar dangers. [[Kunreuther 1996]] complements that lesson by showing that catastrophic hazards can also generate unstable preparation and underinsurance before loss occurs. In the current vault, this makes dread risk an important bridge between judgment research and applied risk policy. Debate concerns whether such responses should be read as irrational overreaction, ecologically understandable caution, or a mix of both.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because crisis communication, terrorism, public health, and technological risk often involve exactly these kinds of low-probability catastrophic events. Policy failures can arise not only from underestimating risk, but also from overreacting to the wrong risk.

## Open questions

How should institutions communicate about catastrophic risks without either inflaming or dismissing public fear? When is dread risk a distortion, and when is it a reasonable response to uncertainty and vulnerability?
