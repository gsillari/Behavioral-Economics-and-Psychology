# Equity premium puzzle

## Core idea

[[Equity premium puzzle]] refers to the unusually large historical gap between returns on equities and returns on safer assets, relative to what standard models of representative investors seem able to explain. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Benartzi and Thaler 1995]], which offers [[Myopic loss aversion]] as a behavioral explanation.

## Key distinctions

The puzzle is not simply that stocks are risky. Standard models already allow risky assets to earn more than safe ones. The puzzle is that the observed premium appears too large to reconcile with plausible levels of conventional risk aversion. [[Benartzi and Thaler 1995]] therefore reframes the issue: perhaps the gap reflects how investors psychologically experience frequent losses rather than only how they price long-run risk.

## Evidence and debate

In the current vault, [[Benartzi and Thaler 1995]] is the first direct source on this topic. Its significance is that it treats the puzzle as an opening for behavioral finance rather than only for more elaborate rational asset-pricing models. The main debate is whether the puzzle is best explained by [[Myopic loss aversion]], by alternative behavioral mechanisms, or by changes in the rational benchmark itself.

## Practical or policy relevance

The concept matters because the equity premium puzzle is not just an abstract finance anomaly. It shapes how economists think about household portfolio choice, retirement saving, pension defaults, and the communication of risk. If citizens avoid equities because short-run losses are too salient, then institutions may need to think carefully about reporting frequency, framing, and default exposure to long-run assets.

## Open questions

Is the puzzle best treated as a failure of standard finance, a failure of descriptive assumptions about investors, or both? How much should policy respond if citizens' portfolio choices are shaped by short-run loss salience rather than long-run welfare?
