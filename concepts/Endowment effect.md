# Endowment effect

## Core idea

[[Endowment effect]] is the tendency for people to value a good more once they possess or are entitled to it. In the current vault, the direct anchor source is now [[Kahneman et al. 1990]], with an important earlier conceptual anchor in [[Thaler 1980]], earlier measurement support from [[Knetsch and Sinden 1984]], later review support from [[Horowitz and McConnell 2002]], and an emotion-sensitive extension from [[Lerner et al. 2004]].

The newer market-belief branch is now anchored by [[Achtypi et al. 2021]], which argues that some endowment effects may arise because buyers and sellers use market-price beliefs differently rather than because ownership changes the intrinsic value of the object.

## Key distinctions

The endowment effect is one important route into [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]], but the two are not identical. The endowment effect is the ownership-sensitive valuation change; the asymmetry is the measurement pattern that often results. The concept also sits close to [[Loss aversion]] and [[Reference dependence]] because giving up an endowed good can be experienced as a loss relative to a salient entitlement. But [[Achtypi et al. 2021]] shows why that interpretation should not be automatic: sellers may ask for something close to perceived market price, while buyers may offer less because they want a good deal.
[[Lerner et al. 2004]] now matters because it shows that this valuation pattern can be modulated by incidental emotion, so the effect should not be treated as psychologically rigid.

## Evidence and debate

[[Thaler 1980]] is important because it gives the effect one of its first explicit economics-facing treatments. Thaler's discussion of opportunity costs, out-of-pocket costs, and marketing practices shows how ownership and entitlement can alter valuation before the later experimental branch fully stabilizes the concept. [[Kahneman et al. 1990]] remains the canonical experimental demonstration in the current vault. The paper shows that assigned ownership shifts valuation and reduces trade volume for ordinary consumption goods in ways consistent with loss aversion and reference dependence. [[Knetsch and Sinden 1984]] provides an earlier empirical foothold by showing a large disparity between willingness to pay and compensation demanded under real incentives. [[Horowitz and McConnell 2002]] then surveys the broader literature. [[Lerner et al. 2004]] adds an important complication: incidental emotions can dampen or even reverse the usual valuation gap. [[Van Boven et al. 2000]] adds a different kind of complication: owners and buyers may also systematically misperceive each other's valuations because of egocentric empathy gaps, so bargaining problems reflect not only differing reference points but also failures of perspective-taking. [[Achtypi et al. 2021]] adds a later interpretive challenge. In a meta-analysis and three experiments, buyers and sellers did not differ in beliefs about product quality or market-price distributions. Sellers' WTAs were close to perceived market prices, while buyers' WTPs were lower than what they believed the object should cost. That result makes the endowment effect look less like a pure ownership-induced preference shift and more like a difference in transaction interpretation. [[Slovic 1995]] and [[Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006]] matter here because they place endowment-sensitive valuation inside the larger literature on response-mode and context dependence, making the effect part of the broader challenge to [[Procedure invariance]] and stable revealed preference. [[List 2003]] and [[List 2004]] then add an important field challenge: the endowment effect can weaken substantially with market experience, and prospect-theory-style predictions fit inexperienced traders better than experienced ones. [[Zaklan 2023]] matters for a different reason. It carries the entitlement question into a modern cap-and-trade market and asks whether initial allocation continues to shape outcomes when trade is possible at scale.

Debate concerns whether the effect is best explained by loss aversion, market inexperience, attachment, emotion, egocentric perspective-taking failures, market-price beliefs, good-deal reasoning, or procedural features of elicitation.

[[Skyrms 2014 - Chapter 04 - Correlated Convention]] adds a different but useful neighboring perspective: ownership can function as a role cue in strategic contests. The "bourgeois" strategy - fight as owner, yield as intruder - is a correlated convention in hawk-dove settings. This does not replace the behavioral-economics explanations of WTA-WTP gaps, but it helps explain why ownership and possession may be socially and strategically meaningful rather than psychologically arbitrary.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many institutional settings create entitlements before valuation occurs. That means compensation, legal remedies, and market design can be shaped by ownership effects rather than by stable underlying value alone. The newer emotion result in the current vault also suggests that measured valuation may be sensitive not only to ownership but to transient affective context. [[Achtypi et al. 2021]] adds that measured valuation may also reflect perceived market worth and the desire to avoid a bad deal. The List papers add that institutional learning and market experience may dampen the effect, while [[Zaklan 2023]] shows that in some large trading systems initial entitlement may matter less in aggregate than simpler behavioral stories would suggest.

## Open questions

How much market experience reduces the endowment effect? Which institutional forms create especially strong entitlement-based valuation shifts? When are WTA-WTP gaps driven by ownership psychology, and when are they driven by market-price beliefs and good-deal reasoning?
