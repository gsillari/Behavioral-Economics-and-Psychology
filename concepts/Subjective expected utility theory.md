# Subjective expected utility theory

## Core idea

[[Subjective expected utility theory]] is the classical benchmark for rational choice under uncertainty in which an agent evaluates acts by combining personal probabilities over states of the world with utilities over consequences. In the current vault, the canonical source is now [[Savage 1954]], especially Chapters 2-7, where the framework is built out of preferences over acts, states, events, the sure-thing principle, personal probability, utility, and observation.

This concept matters because it is more specific than generic expected utility. The risk-side benchmark usually starts from lotteries with given probabilities. The Savage benchmark starts from uncertainty, where probabilities are not given objectively but are represented through a person's coherent preferences over acts. That is why this page should now absorb most of the benchmark work previously handled in the vault through loose Savage shorthand.

## Key distinctions

The first distinction is between risk and uncertainty. In the current vault, [[Expected utility theory]] should now be read as an umbrella benchmark containing both a risk-side branch and this uncertainty-side branch. Subjective expected utility is the uncertainty-side branch.

The second distinction is between [[de Finetti 1937]] and Savage. De Finetti is still the deepest benchmark source for coherence and personal probability. But Savage does more than repeat that idea. [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 02 - Preliminary Considerations on Decision in the Face of Uncertainty]] and [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 03 - Personal Probability]] embed personal probability in a theory of acts and decisions, while [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 05 - Utility]] extends the architecture to utility over acts.

The third distinction is structural. The sure-thing principle from [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 02 - Preliminary Considerations on Decision in the Face of Uncertainty]] is not an optional ornament. It is one of the central commitments that lets the theory connect preferences over acts to probabilities over events. The small-world framework from [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 05 - Utility]] and the observation and partition analyses in Chapters 6-7 show that the theory also contains an informational architecture, not just a static utility formula.

## Evidence and debate

[[Savage 1954]] is the foundational source because it supplies the full benchmark architecture. [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 03 - Personal Probability]] builds personal probability out of decision, [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 04 - Critical Comments on Personal Probability]] keeps the personalistic view self-critical, [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 05 - Utility]] adds the utility side, and [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 06 - Observation]] plus [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 07 - Partition Problems]] show how evidence and observation change the structure of choice.

[[Ellsberg 1961]] is the canonical early pressure point on this benchmark. The importance of Ellsberg is not merely that people are cautious in urn problems. It is that they often resist acting as if uncertain situations can always be represented by one precise personal probability function combined with one utility ranking over acts. In the current vault, that challenge is collected under [[Ambiguity aversion]].

This also clarifies the relation to neighboring classical pressures. [[Allais 1953]] mainly attacks the risk-side benchmark through independence. [[Aumann 1962]] relaxes a different rationality assumption through the [[Completeness axiom]]. Ellsberg is different again. He presses directly on the uncertainty-side benchmark that Savage makes explicit.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many later behavioral arguments are arguments about the scope of this benchmark. When economists, philosophers, or policymakers ask whether agents have coherent subjective probabilities, whether ambiguity aversion is irrational, or whether behavior under deep uncertainty departs from the classical model, the relevant benchmark is often not generic expected utility but subjective expected utility in the Savage sense.

It also matters for the philosophy of statistics. [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 06 - Observation]] and [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 07 - Partition Problems]] show why the benchmark reaches beyond choice theory into evidence, observation, admissibility, and statistics proper.
