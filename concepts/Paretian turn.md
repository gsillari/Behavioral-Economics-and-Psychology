# Paretian turn

## Core idea

[[Paretian turn]] is the name used in the current vault for the methodological shift by which economics redefined itself as a science of rational choice rather than as a discipline grounded in psychology. The main anchor is [[Bruni and Sugden 2007]], which uses the phrase to describe the move initiated by Pareto and later consolidated by Hicks, Allen, and Samuelson. On this reading, the turn does not merely improve consumer theory technically. It changes what economics takes itself to be.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from a simple move away from hedonistic utility. [[Hands 2010]] already shows that the history of consumer theory is more selective than a slogan like `psychology out` suggests. [[Paretian turn]] names the stronger claim that economics should become methodologically independent of psychology by grounding itself in rational-choice structure and logical consistency instead.

It also differs from [[Revealed preference theory]]. Revealed preference is one later and sharper way of stabilizing the anti-psychological ambition, especially in Samuelson's operational reconstruction of consumer theory. The [[Paretian turn]] is the broader historical reorientation of the discipline that makes such later moves seem natural.

## Why it matters here

This concept matters because much of the later history of [[Behavioral Economics]] can be read as a reaction to it. If economics defined itself by separating from psychology, then behavioral economics is not just adding new empirical findings. It is reopening an older question about whether the discipline should explain behavior through real cognitive and affective processes or through abstract principles of rational choice. That is why the concept belongs in the methodological backbone of the vault rather than only in the history of ideas.

It also matters because the same issue reappears in newer forms. Bruni and Sugden argue that modern `discovered preference` defenses of rational-choice theory replay Pareto's strategy by treating economics as a separate science of rational action whose domain is protected by narrowing assumptions about learning, repetition, and stable preference revelation.

## Open questions

Was the Paretian turn a scientific advance, a methodological retreat, or both? How much of standard economics still depends on it? And if behavioral economics partly reverses it, does that mean economics should once again be understood as inseparable from psychology?
