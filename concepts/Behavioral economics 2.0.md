# Behavioral economics 2.0

## Core idea

[[Behavioral economics 2.0]] is a proposed research program in which algorithms act as thought partners that help people get closer to what [[Ludwig et al. 2025]] calls reflective equilibrium: the choice they would make under their most considered judgment with the relevant information available at the time. In the current vault, the concept is not treated as an established field label. It is treated as a serious proposal for where part of behavioral economics might go once personalized algorithmic assistance becomes feasible.

The central ambition is to escape a familiar dilemma. Standard revealed-preference reasoning respects heterogeneity but often leaves mistakes untouched. Traditional [[Nudge|nudging]] responds to mistakes but often assumes a common direction of welfare improvement. [[Behavioral economics 2.0]] proposes that algorithmic thought partners may do better on both dimensions: they may reduce error while still allowing interventions to be tailored to the person rather than imposed uniformly across the population.

## Key distinctions

The concept differs from [[Libertarian paternalism]], which usually works by designing choice architecture that uses [[Nudge|nudges]] to move people in a direction policy makers or designers take to be welfare-improving. [[Behavioral economics 2.0]] is closer to a personalized advisory model: the algorithm is supposed to help the person determine what direction is right for that person.

It also differs from [[Behavioral economics of AI]] in general. That neighboring page covers the whole interaction between behavioral economics and AI, including model evaluation and institutional steering. [[Behavioral economics 2.0]] is narrower. It is specifically the proposal to redesign behavioral welfare economics around algorithmic assistance, reflective equilibrium, and agency-preserving personalization.

Finally, it differs from a simple debiasing story. The point is not only to remove mistakes. The point is to improve decisions while respecting heterogeneity in preferences and values.

## Evidence and debate

[[Ludwig et al. 2025]] is currently the anchor source. The paper argues that algorithmic thought partners may explain options, curate information, implement choices, and highlight psychological traps in ways that ordinary [[Nudge|nudges]] cannot. It treats interventions like Save More Tomorrow as an early precursor to this program, because they help people choose their own future defaults rather than simply accepting a one-size-fits-all [[Nudge|nudge]].

In the current vault, this concept should also be read beside [[Sunstein 2025]]. Sunstein's AI-powered choice engines are close in spirit, but the emphasis is different. Sunstein is more squarely inside the existing paternalism debate, asking how AI can personalize welfare-promoting choice architecture while preserving autonomy. [[Ludwig et al. 2025]] is more manifesto-like: it treats algorithmic assistance as the basis for a more general reorientation of behavioral economics itself.

The main debate is whether this proposal really solves the old welfare problem or merely restates it in more technologically sophisticated form. If algorithms are said to help people reach their own considered judgment, someone still has to specify what counts as more considered, how to detect it, and how to know when behavior has genuinely improved rather than merely changed.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because it offers a possible next stage for behaviorally informed policy and decision support. If it works, it could make interventions more individualized, less blunt, and less dependent on assuming that everyone should be nudged in the same direction. It also matters because it repositions AI not only as a threat or as an object of evaluation, but as a proposed welfare-improving assistant.

At the same time, it raises familiar risks in a new form: manipulation, opaque welfare judgments, overreliance on algorithmic authority, and the possibility that systems designed to help people choose better may instead entrench the values or incentives of designers, firms, or regulators.

## Open questions

Is reflective equilibrium in this practical sense measurable enough to guide real institutions? Should [[Behavioral economics 2.0]] be read as an extension of existing behavioral public policy, or as a challenge to its current welfare logic? And will this label become durable in the literature, or remain mainly attached to one influential manifesto?
