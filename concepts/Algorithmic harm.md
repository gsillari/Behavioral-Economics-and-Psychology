# Algorithmic harm

## Core idea

[[Algorithmic harm]] is the idea that algorithms can harm people not only by making inaccurate or unfair predictions, but also by exploiting information deficits, behavioral biases, and other consumer vulnerabilities. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Bar-Gill et al. 2023]], which develops the concept for consumer markets and shows how algorithmic personalization can become exploitative when consumers are imperfectly informed or imperfectly rational.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Algorithmic bias]]. Bias is mainly about patterned unfairness, unequal error burdens, or problematic proxies across groups. Algorithmic harm is broader. A system may harm consumers even without creating the kind of group-based disparity that dominates the fairness-metrics literature.

It also differs from [[Predictive optimization]]. That neighboring page studies the broader legitimacy problems of decision-making systems organized around prediction targets. Algorithmic harm is narrower and more market-facing: it focuses on how firms can use algorithmic tools to tailor prices, products, and offers around behavioral vulnerabilities.

Finally, it differs from a general complaint about personalization. In many settings, personalization can improve fit and welfare. The concept becomes interesting when personalization is used to identify and exploit weakness rather than to help consumers choose well.

## Evidence and debate

[[Bar-Gill et al. 2023]] provides the current vault's anchor statement. The paper distinguishes between algorithmic price discrimination and algorithmic quality discrimination, or product targeting. It argues that in markets populated by informed and rational consumers, these tools can increase efficiency. But in markets populated by imperfectly informed or imperfectly rational consumers, the same tools can magnify harm by identifying people who are inattentive, biased, mistaken, or otherwise vulnerable to manipulation.

In the current vault, this matters because it adds a market-exploitation branch to a literature that had so far been organized mainly by [[Algorithmic bias]], [[Statistical fairness criteria]], [[Target variable problem]], and [[Predictive optimization]]. Those pages focus more on public-sector decision systems, fairness trade-offs, and contested optimization targets. [[Algorithmic harm]] adds a different question: when firms use machine learning to steer consumers toward overpriced, low-value, or behaviorally sticky transactions, what kind of harm is that, and how should law respond?

The policy debate in the current vault turns on disclosure, algorithmic transparency, and design oversight. The paper's key claim is not that all algorithmic personalization is bad, but that behavioral vulnerabilities create a special regulatory problem once they can be identified and exploited at scale.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because more and more consumer markets are mediated by recommendation systems, personalized offers, dynamic pricing, and targeted product design. If firms can detect and exploit limited attention, present bias, mistaken beliefs, or unstable preferences, then consumer protection can no longer rely only on classical models of informed exchange.

In the current vault, the concept therefore sits close to [[Behavioral public policy]] and [[Behavioral law and economics]]. It also matters for the AI branch because it shows that the governance problem is not only whether public institutions should use algorithms, but also how private institutions use them to shape demand, valuation, and choice.

## Open questions

When should welfare-improving personalization be distinguished from exploitation? Which forms of transparency actually reduce harm in complex digital markets? And how should regulators respond when the same algorithmic architecture can both help and exploit, depending on market conditions and consumer vulnerability?
