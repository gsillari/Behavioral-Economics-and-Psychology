# Illusion of control

## Core idea

[[Illusion of control]] is the tendency to behave as if one can influence outcomes that are in fact largely or entirely determined by chance. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Langer 1982 - The Illusion of Control (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 16)]], which shows that chance situations can feel controllable when they contain cues normally associated with skill.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from ordinary confidence in one's abilities because the relevant outcomes are not genuinely controllable in the first place. It also differs from [[Overconfidence]], although the two often overlap: overconfidence concerns excessive belief in one's ability, information, or prospects, whereas illusion of control concerns mistaken belief in causal influence over uncertain outcomes. In the current vault, the page belongs naturally in [[Nonstandard beliefs]] because it is a distorted belief about agency and causation.

## Evidence and debate

The anchor chapter argues that people often treat chance events as if they were controllable, especially when the setting offers choice, familiarity, competition, or active involvement. That matters because it suggests that beliefs about control are not read off directly from the world. They are shaped by the structure and symbolism of the task. In the current vault, the concept also belongs in [[Heuristics and Biases]] because it shows a classic distortion that is not only about probability calculation, but also about causal interpretation and agency.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because beliefs about control influence gambling, investing, planning, and self-management. Institutions that rely on individuals to judge risk may therefore face problems not only of information, but also of mistaken agency. For PPE students, the page matters because it connects psychology of belief to questions of responsibility, luck, and rational action.
