# Reference dependence

## Core idea

[[Reference dependence]] is the idea that people evaluate outcomes relative to a benchmark rather than only in absolute terms. A change can be experienced as a gain or a loss depending on where the reference point is set. In the current vault, this is one of the core structural ideas inside [[Prospect Theory]], beginning with [[Kahneman and Tversky 1979]].

That is why the concept matters so much. It changes the object of evaluation from final states to changes from a salient baseline. Once that move is made, a large part of the behavioral branch begins to cohere: [[Loss aversion]], [[Framing effects]], the [[Endowment effect]], [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]], and [[Loss-framed incentives]] all become easier to understand as variants of the same underlying shift.

## Key distinctions

Three distinctions keep the page clear.

First, [[Reference dependence]] differs from [[Expected utility theory]]. Expected utility usually models preferences over final states. Reference dependence shifts attention to changes from a baseline.

Second, the concept is not identical to [[Loss aversion]]. Reference dependence determines whether an outcome is being treated as a gain or a loss. [[Loss aversion]] then describes the asymmetric weight attached to those coded outcomes. In the current vault, reference dependence is therefore the structure that makes loss aversion possible.

Third, the concept is not the same as [[Framing effects]], even though the two are tightly linked. Reference dependence identifies the baseline relative to which outcomes are evaluated. Framing effects concern how wording, presentation, or institutional design can make one benchmark rather than another salient.

## How the concept works

The cleanest way to read the concept is as a sequence.

1. A benchmark becomes salient.
2. Outcomes are coded as gains or losses relative to that benchmark.
3. Choice and valuation then change because the same objective outcome now has a different psychological meaning.

That benchmark can take several forms in the current vault:
- the status quo
- an expectation
- an entitlement
- a default
- a recent outcome or aspiration level

Once this is clear, a lot of neighboring concepts stop looking separate. They become different consequences of the same baseline-sensitive structure.

## Main branches in the vault

### 1. Prospect theory and risky choice

The first and deepest branch is [[Prospect Theory]]. In the current vault, [[Reference dependence]] is one of the theory’s indispensable moving parts. Without a reference point, there is no principled way to divide outcomes into gains and losses, and without that division the theory loses much of its distinctive structure.

This is also why [[Kahneman and Tversky 1979]] is the core source here. It does not merely mention reference points in passing. It uses them to redefine what the object of evaluation is.

### 2. Framing and representation

The next branch is [[Framing effects]]. If choice depends on the relevant benchmark, then changes in description or representation can shift behavior even when objective outcomes remain the same. In the current vault, this is one reason framing matters so much: it can change which baseline becomes psychologically active.

### 3. Valuation, ownership, and entitlement

The valuation branch shows the concept at work outside classic risky-choice problems. [[Horowitz and McConnell 2002]] is especially important here because it ties reference dependence to [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]] once ownership or entitlement creates a salient benchmark. That same logic also feeds into the [[Endowment effect]], where giving something up is coded relative to possession rather than to neutral final-state comparison.

### 4. Incentives and organizational design

[[Hossain and List 2009]] gives the concept a concrete organizational application. In [[Loss-framed incentives]], a bonus is not just paid or withheld. It is presented as something already possessed and therefore vulnerable to loss. This matters because it shows that reference dependence is not confined to survey or lab tasks; it can be built directly into workplace institutions.

### 5. Field evidence and policy

[[DellaVigna 2009]] matters because it shows that reference dependence is not just a laboratory feature of stylized choice tasks. It appears in field settings and therefore matters for how economists interpret wages, contracts, entitlements, prices, and institutional reform. That is what makes the concept so important for PPE students: it links micro-level decision theory to broader questions of policy design and political backlash.

## Debate and limits

The main debates concern the status and selection of reference points.

How are they formed? How many can operate at once? How stable are they across time or across institutions? And when should an analyst treat a baseline as psychologically real rather than as an interpretive convenience?

This is also the point where the concept can become too loose if used carelessly. If every reaction to change is explained by an unspecified reference point, the theory stops doing real work. The concept is strongest when the baseline is identifiable and the mechanism of gain-loss coding is clear.

## Practical or policy relevance

[[Reference dependence]] matters for any policy that changes what people take to be normal or entitled. Pension reform, benefit redesign, price changes, compensation schemes, and workplace incentives are all experienced through benchmark shifts. Institutions that ignore reference points can therefore misread public reactions even when objective outcomes look favorable on paper.

This is one reason the concept has to remain visible in the PPE framing of the vault. It links decision theory to fairness, entitlement, institutional legitimacy, and political backlash. It helps explain why the same reform can look like relief, fairness, or expropriation depending on the benchmark from which it is viewed.

## Open questions

How are reference points formed in real institutions rather than laboratory tasks? Can multiple reference points compete at once? When do social norms or entitlements set the benchmark more strongly than private expectations? And how much explanatory work should the concept be allowed to do before it becomes too flexible to test?
