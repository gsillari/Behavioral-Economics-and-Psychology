# Menu effects

## Core idea

[[Menu effects]] are systematic distortions that arise when people choose from large or complex sets of options. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[DellaVigna 2009]], which summarizes field evidence on excessive diversification, familiarity bias, salience bias, choice avoidance, and implementation confusion.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Limited attention]], which concerns underprocessing of information, though the two are closely related. It also differs from [[Libertarian paternalism]], which is a normative framework for steering choices, whereas menu effects describe descriptive failures that choice architecture may try to address. DellaVigna's central point is that large menus do not simply reveal preferences more accurately; they often trigger simplifying heuristics.

## Evidence and debate

[[DellaVigna 2009]] organizes the evidence around five suboptimal heuristics: excess diversification or the `1/n` rule, preference for familiar options, preference for salient options, choice avoidance, and confusion in implementing choices. Field settings include retirement-plan allocations, investment decisions, ballot design, and mistaken trading under confusing ticker symbols. Debate concerns whether these patterns reflect bounded rationality, reasonable simplification under complexity, or equilibrium responses to badly designed menus.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many institutions present people with large menus precisely in the name of freedom or flexibility. If menu complexity triggers predictable distortions, then better policy may require simplification, defaults, smarter ordering, or clearer implementation rather than simply more options. For PPE students, this is one of the strongest places where descriptive behavioral findings feed directly into debates about institutional design.
