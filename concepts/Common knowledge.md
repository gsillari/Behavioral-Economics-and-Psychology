# Common knowledge

## Core idea

[[Common knowledge]] is the recursive epistemic condition in which a fact is known, known to be known, known to be known to be known, and so on in the relevant population. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Lewis 1969 - Chapter 02 - Convention Refined]], where common knowledge explains how [[Convention|conventions]] become self-sustaining.

The point is not merely that everyone privately knows the same fact. In a coordination setting, each agent must be able to rely on others also recognizing the fact as shared. That shared recognition is what lets expectations converge.

## Role in convention

Lewis uses common knowledge to refine the theory of convention. A regularity can solve a recurrent coordination problem only if agents know enough about others' conformity, expectations, and conditional preferences. If I conform only when I expect you to conform, and you conform only when you expect me to conform, then the stability of the pattern depends on a higher-order structure of mutual expectation.

This is why common knowledge belongs near [[Coordination games]]. A salient focal point, rule, precedent, or label works only when it can become mutually recognized as a basis for action.

## Limits of common knowledge

[[Van Huyck et al. 1990]] is a useful warning against overloading the concept. In their tacit coordination experiments, subjects have complete information about the payoff table and the strategic setting is made public. Yet the group often fails to coordinate on the payoff-dominant equilibrium and instead converges toward the secure low outcome.

The lesson is that common knowledge of the game is not the same as common expectations about equilibrium selection. Common knowledge can help sustain a convention once a regularity exists, but it does not by itself say which equilibrium agents should expect others to choose when several strict equilibria are available.

[[Camerer 2003 - Chapter 07 - Coordination]] adds the positive side. Common ground can make a [[Focal points|focal point]] usable: a label, precedent, visual feature, assignment, or signal matters when players can expect others to recognize it too. The chapter's psycholinguistic appendix is especially useful here because it treats coordination as a problem of shared meaning, not merely shared information.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, common knowledge matters because many social and political institutions depend on shared expectations. Laws, conventions, money, language, queues, traffic norms, political procedures, and bargaining practices often require not just private belief but public or mutually accessible belief.

It also matters for behavioral economics because it clarifies a non-bias role for salience. In coordination problems, salient cues can be rationally useful because they help generate common expectations.

## Open questions

How strong must the common-knowledge condition be in real institutions? Can approximate, partial, or publicly signaled knowledge do the work that idealized models assign to full common knowledge? When does the absence of common knowledge explain institutional failure better than conflicting preferences do?
