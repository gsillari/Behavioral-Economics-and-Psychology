# Ecological rationality

## Core idea

[[Ecological rationality]] is the view that the quality of a decision rule or social order depends on how well it fits the structure of the environment in which it is used. In the current vault, however, that phrase should no longer be treated as naming one single coherent program. [[Dekker and Remic 2019]] makes clear that the label is used in two importantly different ways: an individual-level heuristic ecology associated with [[Gigerenzer 2008]], [[Gigerenzer and Goldstein 1996]], and [[Todd and Gigerenzer 2007]], and an institutional or system-level ecology associated with [[Smith 2003]]. [[Simon 1956]] remains an important precursor bridge, while [[Hortal 2025]] provides a later philosophical framing of the dispute inside the contemporary rationality wars. [[Berg and Gigerenzer 2010]] matters because it uses the ecological program not just as an alternative benchmark, but as a direct critique of the `as-if` methodology that the paper argues survives inside much behavioral economics.

## Key distinctions

Ecological rationality differs from the formal benchmark approach associated with [[Heuristics and Biases]] because it evaluates judgment by adaptive fit rather than by strict conformity to logic, probability, or expected utility. But the vault now needs a second distinction inside the ecological side itself.

In the Gigerenzer branch, ecological rationality is mainly about individual heuristics and cue use. It is tied to the [[Adaptive toolbox]] and to the question of when simple strategies succeed in real-world environments.

In the Smith branch, ecological rationality is mainly about institutions, rules, and emergent order. It is tied to [[Constructivist rationality]] as a contrast term and to the question of when interaction under suitable norms and institutional settings produces rational outcomes at the system level.

[[Dekker and Remic 2019]] matters because it shows that these are not just two examples of one framework. They are two rival uses of the same label, with different psychological underpinnings and different experimental practices. What they share is mainly a common enemy: the monopoly of one formal benchmark and the dominance of the bias-centered reading of rationality.

## Evidence and debate

The current anchor sources are [[Gigerenzer and Goldstein 1996]], [[Todd and Gigerenzer 2007]], [[Gigerenzer 2008]], [[Smith 2003]], [[Dekker and Remic 2019]], [[Berg and Gigerenzer 2010]], [[Simon 1956]], and [[Hortal 2025]]. [[Gigerenzer and Goldstein 1996]] matters because it gives the ecological-rationality branch a concrete fast-and-frugal model through [[Take-the-Best heuristic]] and one-reason decision making. [[Todd and Gigerenzer 2007]] matters because it states the Gigerenzer program in a compact way centered on mind-world fit, environment structure, and the idea that people can be made smarter by better-matched decision environments. [[Gigerenzer 2008]] provides the broader positive account of that same line: heuristics can work because they fit environments, and the right question is when they do so. [[Smith 2003]] then gives a different ecological program by arguing that ecological rationality can also describe emergent institutional orders, especially markets, whose success cannot be reduced to explicit design or to the optimization capacities of any one agent. [[Simon 1956]] matters here as a bridge source rather than as a full ecological manifesto. It shows that adaptive choice depends on the structure of the environment as well as on the limitations of the organism, which makes it an important precursor to later environment-sensitive accounts. Hortal then uses ecological rationality as a major pole in the broader [[Rationality wars]].

[[Berg and Gigerenzer 2010]] adds a methodological use of ecological rationality that the vault needed to make more explicit. The paper's complaint is not mainly that behavioral economics sometimes forgets that theories like [[Prospect Theory]] are descriptive rather than normative. Its sharper complaint is that behavioral economics often remains committed to Friedman-style `as-if` theorizing: it preserves optimization architectures and adjusts them for fit without uncovering real cognitive processes. Ecological rationality is offered as an alternative precisely because it asks how strategies work in environments, not only how well parameterized models reproduce observed choice.

[[Dekker and Remic 2019]] is the source that should now govern how the vault handles this branch. Its argument is not merely that Smith and Gigerenzer emphasize different levels of one framework. It is that they use "ecological rationality" in two different ways. Gigerenzer's ER1 is an individual-level, Brunswikian, heuristic program focused on cue use, representative design, and real-world tasks. Smith's ER2 is a system-level, distributed-cognition, institutional program focused on interaction, rules, norms, and emergent order. They can still be discussed together because both reject the monopoly of one formal benchmark and both present themselves against the [[Heuristics and Biases]] tradition. But the vault should no longer imply that Smith simply extends Gigerenzer. They are neighboring but distinct ecological programs.

[[Petracca 2017]] adds a useful caution from a different angle. Even when ecological rationality moves beyond Simon's original technical formulations, it may still preserve more of the older [[Cognitivism|cognitivist]] worldview than it admits. Petracca's claim is that post-Simonian bounded-rationality research, including the ecological branch, has not deeply absorbed the challenge of [[Situated cognition]] and [[Embodied cognition]]. In the current vault, that should not be taken as a reason to collapse ecological rationality back into Simon. It should be taken as a reminder that the branch's environment-sensitivity is not automatically the same thing as a full post-cognitivist theory of mind.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters for [[Behavioral public policy]] because the two ecological programs imply different cautions. [[Gigerenzer 2008]] suggests that policy should sometimes improve the fit between individuals, heuristics, and environments rather than treating every shortcut as a defect to be corrected. [[Smith 2003]] suggests that policy should also be careful not to damage ecologically successful institutions and norms in the name of constructivist redesign. Once the vault distinguishes these two ecological programs, it becomes clearer that "ecological rationality" does not yield one single policy lesson.

This branch also matters for [[Debiasing]]. [[Arkes 1991]] already hints that some judgment errors are the costs of otherwise adaptive systems. From the ecological point of view, that means some so-called debiasing should really be understood as environment redesign or fit-improvement rather than as forcing agents toward a single formal benchmark. The ecological critique therefore does not reject all debiasing, but it does demand that the vault distinguish between correcting a genuine malfunction and improving the match between agent, task, and institution.
