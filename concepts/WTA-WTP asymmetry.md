# WTA-WTP asymmetry

## Core idea

[[WTA-WTP asymmetry]] is the tendency for willingness to accept compensation for giving something up to exceed willingness to pay for acquiring the same thing. In the current vault, the direct early anchors are now [[Knetsch and Sinden 1984]] and [[Kahneman et al. 1990]], [[Thaler 1985]] adds a broader consumer-choice framework through [[Mental accounting]], and [[Horowitz and McConnell 2002]] remains the main review source.

## Key distinctions

The concept differs from a simple pricing discrepancy because it concerns valuation under different entitlement positions. It is closely related to [[Endowment effect]] and often interpreted through [[Loss aversion]], but the asymmetry is the measurement pattern itself rather than any single psychological explanation. [[Achtypi et al. 2021]] is useful here because it shows that the same WTA-WTP pattern can also arise from buyers and sellers applying market-price beliefs differently. In the current vault, the asymmetry therefore belongs both in the ownership/loss branch and in the broader family of procedure-sensitive valuation effects emphasized by [[Slovic 1995]] and [[Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006]].

## Evidence and debate

[[Knetsch and Sinden 1984]] matters because it gives the branch an early experimental demonstration of an unexpectedly wide gap under real incentives. [[Kahneman et al. 1990]] matters because it ties the asymmetry more tightly to ownership, reduced trading volume, and the [[Endowment effect]]. [[Thaler 1985]] matters because it gives the asymmetry a larger consumer-choice frame: different valuations can partly reflect the mental account in which a transaction is coded, not only the object traded. [[Van Boven et al. 2000]] adds an important social-cognitive complication by showing that owners and buyers also misjudge each other's valuations, which helps sustain the gap through [[Endowment effect]] as well as through differing entitlement positions. [[Horowitz and McConnell 2002]] then shows that the gap is widespread and systematically larger for less ordinary market goods. [[Achtypi et al. 2021]] adds a market-belief account: sellers' WTAs can track perceived market worth, while buyers' WTPs can sit below perceived market worth because buyers want a good deal. [[Slovic 1995]] and [[Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006]] matter because they place such valuation gaps inside the broader challenge to [[Procedure invariance]]: different elicitation formats can shape not just the number reported, but the preference or valuation that appears to exist. [[List 2003]] and [[List 2004]] add a major qualification by showing that these gaps shrink in populations with substantial market experience. In the current vault, this gives the valuation branch both landmark experiments and a broader theoretical frame. Debate concerns whether the asymmetry is best explained by loss aversion, market inexperience, substitution problems, mental accounting, market-price beliefs, or elicitation procedures.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because policy and law often rely on monetary valuation. Compensation rules, environmental valuation, and cost-benefit analysis can look quite different depending on whether the relevant measure is WTP or WTA.
