# Availability heuristic

## Core idea

[[Availability heuristic]] is the tendency to judge frequency, probability, or importance partly by how easily relevant cases come to mind. In this fresh vault, the direct anchor source is now [[Tversky and Kahneman 1973]], with [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] as the canonical shorter statement inside [[Heuristics and Biases]].

## Key distinctions

Availability differs from [[Representativeness heuristic]] because it depends on recall and salience rather than on resemblance to a prototype. It also differs from [[Anchoring]], where judgment is pulled by an initial numerical or conceptual starting point. Availability becomes especially distortionary when vividness, recency, or media exposure diverge from actual frequency.

## Evidence and debate

[[Tversky and Kahneman 1973]] provides the full original statement of the heuristic and shows how recall, imaginability, and associative structure can bias judgments of frequency and probability.
[[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] then condenses that logic into the broader heuristics-and-biases program.
[[Kahneman and Tversky 1982 - The Simulation Heuristic (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 14)]] extends the branch by distinguishing retrieval from construction: some judgments depend not on recalled instances but on how easily one can mentally generate a plausible scenario or path to an outcome.
The classic logic includes recall effects, imaginability effects, and patterns where vivid cases distort statistical judgment.
The concept later became central to work on risk perception and public attention, but its basic vault role remains simple: it names one of the three core heuristic families in the founding heuristics-and-biases source.
[[Ross and Sicoly 1979]] adds a useful social extension. The paper shows that availability can be egocentrically structured: people recall their own contributions to joint activity more readily than those of others and then assign themselves more responsibility accordingly. That matters because it shows the heuristic working not only in judgments of external frequency but also in interpersonal attribution and memory.
[[Lichtenstein et al. 1978]] is important because it moves the branch into socially meaningful risk judgments by showing that perceived frequencies of causes of death are systematically distorted.
[[Slovic 1987]] then provides the higher-level framework of [[Risk perception]] in which those distortions matter politically and socially, not only statistically.
[[Finucane et al. 2000]] helps extend that later branch by showing that intuitive risk judgment can also be shaped by [[Affect heuristic|affect]], not only by recall and imaginability.
[[Sunstein 2003]] sharpens the policy-facing side of the branch through [[Probability neglect]], where fearful focus on a bad outcome suppresses attention to likelihood itself.
[[Gigerenzer 2004]] then gives the branch a vivid public case: after September 11, a highly salient [[Dread risk]] may have shifted behavior toward driving, with harmful aggregate consequences.
[[Kuran and Sunstein 1999]] extends the branch from individual salience to collective policy response through [[Availability cascades]], where public attention, reputational incentives, and institutional amplification can make some hazards politically dominant.

In the current vault, availability should also be read as part of the wider move from laboratory judgment tasks to public salience and policy attention.
That is one reason it belongs naturally beside the later DellaVigna branch and not only inside the 1974 source cluster.

## Practical or policy relevance

Availability matters for policy because institutions operate in an environment shaped by salience. Public attention, perceived urgency, and support for intervention often respond to memorable cases rather than to baseline incidence. This makes the concept important for risk communication, public health, crime policy, and media-sensitive domains inside [[Behavioral Economics]].
