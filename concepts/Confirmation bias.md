# Confirmation bias

## Core idea

[[Confirmation bias]] is the tendency to seek, interpret, and remember evidence in ways that favor an existing belief, expectation, or hypothesis. In the current vault, the earliest experimental anchor is now [[Wason 1968]], where subjects in the [[Wason selection task]] tend to inspect matching or confirming cases rather than the potentially falsifying case. The main review anchor remains [[Nickerson 1998]], with [[Kunda 1990]] and [[Epley and Gilovich 2016]] providing adjacent mechanism-oriented interpretations through [[Motivated reasoning]].

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from the classic heuristic pages such as [[Representativeness heuristic]], [[Availability heuristic]], and [[Anchoring]], which begin from shortcut-based judgment under uncertainty. Confirmation bias often begins later in the process, once a hypothesis or attitude is already in place and evidence is being searched or weighed. It also differs from [[Motivated reasoning]], which is broader and more explicitly about directional goals. Confirmation bias can be one major expression of motivated reasoning, but the two are not identical. It also differs from [[Myside bias]], which is narrower and more positional: myside bias concerns evaluating arguments from one's own side of a dispute, whereas confirmation bias is the broader family of selective evidence handling.

The [[Wason selection task]] adds one more important distinction. The selection-task error is often described as confirmation bias because subjects choose cards that could verify or match the rule. But the task is also about conditional reasoning, falsification, and the interpretation of "if P, then Q." That is why [[Cosmides and Tooby 2000]] can reinterpret the same paradigm as evidence for domain-specific social reasoning rather than as a simple generic tendency to confirm.

## Evidence and debate

[[Wason 1968]] matters because it gives the concept a compact experimental paradigm. The logically diagnostic card in the abstract task is not-Q, because it can reveal a falsifying P-and-not-Q case. Many subjects instead choose P and Q, as if testing a rule meant looking for the named or confirming cases. [[Nickerson 1998]] matters because it shows how widely the broader pattern appears across hypothesis testing, memory, social judgment, and practical reasoning. The review makes clear that the phenomenon is not one narrow laboratory effect but a family of evidence-handling distortions. [[Kunda 1990]] matters because it supplies a cleaner theoretical account of how goals can shape the use of cognitive processes without turning reasoning into pure irrationality. [[Epley and Gilovich 2016]] matters because it shows how ordinary inferential tools can be recruited selectively in ways that support preferred conclusions. [[Mercier and Sperber 2011]] adds a broader functional backdrop: if reasoning is partly adapted for producing and evaluating arguments, then selective support for favored conclusions is not just an accident in the system but may be connected to one of its core social roles.

[[Cosmides and Tooby 2000]] is important as a warning about overgeneralization. If performance improves dramatically when the same conditional structure is embedded in a social-contract problem, then Wason-style failure cannot simply be read as a global incapacity for conditional reasoning. It becomes part of the [[Rationality wars]]: the same experiment can support a bias-centered interpretation, a pragmatic-content interpretation, or an evolutionary-psychology interpretation depending on the standard being applied.

In the current vault, the concept therefore belongs in the belief branch rather than as an isolated social-psychology curiosity. It helps the vault represent a form of biased inference that is not exhausted by the original three heuristic families.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because policy disputes, expert controversies, ideological polarization, and institutional learning all depend on how evidence is filtered. Confirmation bias can make agents look evidence-responsive while still preventing serious revision of mistaken views. That matters for forecasting, regulation, public debate, and institutional error correction.
