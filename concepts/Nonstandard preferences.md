# Nonstandard preferences

to be REVIEWED

## Core idea

[[Nonstandard preferences]] refers to departures from the standard economic assumption that [[Preference|preferences]] are stable, time-consistent, self-interested, and independent of framing or reference points. In the current vault, the anchor sources are [[Rabin 1998]], which gives the branch its broad psychology-and-economics architecture, [[DellaVigna 2009]], which uses this category to organize field evidence on [[Time inconsistency]], [[Reference dependence]], and [[Social preferences]], [[Guth et al. 1982]], [[Camerer and Thaler 1995]], [[Berg et al. 1995]], and [[Bohnet and Zeckhauser 2004]], which give the social-preference branch its experimental-game spine, [[O'Donoghue and Rabin 1999]], which gives the time-inconsistency branch a precise model of present bias and sophistication versus naivete, [[Benartzi and Thaler 1995]], which shows how reference-dependent loss sensitivity can be used to explain the [[Equity premium puzzle]] through [[Myopic loss aversion]], [[Rabin and Thaler 2001]], which generalizes the loss-aversion-and-narrow-bracketing account to ordinary small-stakes risk aversion, [[Ariely et al. 2003]], which adds a challenge through [[Constructed preferences]] and [[Coherent arbitrariness]], and [[Ariely et al. 2006]], which extends that challenge into experiential goods through [[Preference uncertainty]]. [[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]] gives this branch an additional methodological foundation by clarifying that preference research lacks an accuracy criterion but still depends on consistency standards and on the practical possibility of eliciting meaningful preferences at all.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Nonstandard beliefs]], where the issue is incorrect expectations about the world, and from [[Nonstandard decision-making]], where the issue is the process by which choices are made even when preferences and beliefs are held fixed. In the current vault, the main subtypes are intertemporal instability in [[Time inconsistency]], reference-point dependence in [[Reference dependence]], concern for other people's payoffs and treatment in [[Social preferences]], finance-oriented loss sensitivity in [[Myopic loss aversion]], and the more radical possibility of [[Constructed preferences]], where the issue is not only unusual preference content but the instability or partial formation of preferences themselves.

The game-theory branch gives the social-preference subtype its clearest diagnostic tools. The [[Ultimatum game]] shows costly rejection of unfair treatment, the [[Dictator game]] removes strategic rejection to probe giving and social meaning, and the [[Trust game]] separates reciprocity and betrayal from ordinary risk.

## Evidence and debate

[[DellaVigna 2009]] treats nonstandard preferences as the largest and most developed part of field-based behavioral economics.
The evidence includes self-control problems in savings and consumption, reference dependence in insurance and labor supply, and social-preference patterns in giving and workplace reciprocity.
That is one reason this page should remain one of the most central children of the DellaVigna branch.

[[Rabin 1998]] gives the branch its more general map. Some psychological findings modify utility while leaving rational choice broadly intact: people may care about [[Fairness]], reciprocity, altruism, reference points, losses, self-control, and other motives that the narrow standard model omits. But Rabin also makes clear that not every preference-side challenge is merely a richer utility function. [[Reason-based choice]], melioration, and [[Experienced utility]] press on the stability and welfare interpretation of preferences themselves.

[[Benartzi and Thaler 1995]] extends this picture into finance by arguing that prospect-theoretic loss sensitivity plus short evaluation horizons can shape portfolio choice strongly enough to address the [[Equity premium puzzle]].
[[Rabin and Thaler 2001]] gives the same branch a broader [[Calibration|calibration]] role. It argues that small-stakes risk aversion should not be treated as ordinary concavity over lifetime wealth, because that interpretation generates absurd large-stakes implications. The more plausible nonstandard-preference structure is [[Loss aversion]] plus [[Mental accounting]] or narrow bracketing.
[[O'Donoghue and Rabin 1999]] gives the intertemporal-preference branch its most useful compact architecture. Present bias can produce procrastination for immediate-cost activities and preproperation for immediate-reward activities, while the distinction between sophisticated and naive agents explains why some people seek commitment and others repeatedly mispredict their own future discipline. That makes time inconsistency a structured family of nonstandard preferences, not just a loose observation that people sometimes lack willpower.
[[Loewenstein and Prelec 2007 - Anomalies in Intertemporal Choice - Evidence and an Interpretation (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 15)]] strengthens the intertemporal side by arguing that many apparent differences in discounting are better understood through a richer value structure than through a single stable discount rate.
[[Hsee et al. 2007 - Preference Reversals Between Joint and Separate Evaluations of Options (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 9)]] sharpens the debate on construction by showing how joint and separate evaluation change the weight of attributes through evaluability.
[[Ariely et al. 2003]] then sharpens the debate further by suggesting that preferences may sometimes be partly constructed during elicitation, with arbitrary anchors helping determine valuation levels while preserving stable-looking relative demand.
[[Ariely et al. 2006]] pushes further by suggesting that in some cases people may not even know whether an experience is good or bad after sampling it, which the vault now tracks under [[Preference uncertainty]]. [[Vredenburgh 2022 - The Economic Concept of a Preference (in Heilmann and Reiss 2022, ch. 5)]] helps frame the whole debate at a higher level by arguing that the economic concept of preference is itself more philosophically unstable than many behavioral arguments assume.
[[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]] helps interpret this whole branch by arguing that preference anomalies often emerge when researchers pose abstract, unfamiliar, or objectionable tasks that strain respondents' ability to map values cleanly onto the elicitation format. That does not make the anomalies unimportant. It makes them more theoretically revealing, because they expose both the limits of stable-preference assumptions and the measurement burdens placed on participants.
[[Bernheim 2025]] adds a further normative caution. Even if stable-preference assumptions fail, policy cannot simply assume that analysts have thereby identified a truer welfare benchmark. Preference instability may reveal characterization problems, optimization failures, or deeper ambiguity in value itself, and those possibilities should not be collapsed into one paternalist inference.
[[Sugden 2018 - Chapter 04 - The Inner Rational Agent]] presses the same point from a different angle: behavioral instability does not by itself reveal the preferences of a deeper rational self that policy may promote on the chooser's behalf.

The main debate is therefore not only whether preferences depart from the standard model.
It is also whether those departures reveal stable but nonstandard preference structures or whether, in some domains, they undermine the assumption that preferences are stably there to be revealed at all.
That is exactly why this page needs to remain connected both to the prospect-theory branch and to the constructed-preferences branch.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many behaviorally informed interventions start by asking whether the standard preference model is missing something important. If preferences are time-inconsistent, interventions may address [[Internalities]]. If preferences are reference-dependent, policy communication and default design matter more. If preferences incorporate other-regarding concerns, fundraising, labor contracts, and institutional reciprocity become central policy and market questions. If preferences are partly [[Constructed preferences|constructed]], then welfare analysis becomes harder still, because observed willingness to pay may not straightforwardly express what people value. [[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]] adds an important caution here: some apparent preference instability may reflect not only deep theory failure, but also the difficulty of asking people to state precise trade-offs in domains they do not habitually represent that way. [[Bernheim 2025]] pushes the caution one step further by arguing that the policy question is not just whether preferences are unstable, but whether the instability identifies a genuine mistake rather than a domain in which welfare judgments remain contestable.

## Open questions

Which departures from standard preferences remain robust in high-stakes field settings? When should economists treat these deviations as genuine preference content rather than as mistakes or unstable representations? How should policy respond when different kinds of nonstandard preferences point toward different interventions?
