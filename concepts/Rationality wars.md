# Rationality wars

## Core idea

[[Rationality wars]] refers to the contemporary dispute over what rationality is, how it should be measured, and which standards should guide explanation and policy. In the current vault, the anchor sources now structure the page in complementary ways. [[Gigerenzer 2008]] provides a primary statement of the ecological-rationality side, [[Gigerenzer 2024]] reflects directly on the dispute from within that tradition, [[Smith 2003]] gives the ecological side a parallel statement from experimental economics and institutional emergence, [[Hortal 2025]] emphasizes clashes among epistemological frameworks, and [[Samuels et al. 2002]] argues that some famous disputes are overstated and partly dissolve once standards of evaluation are clarified. [[Sloman 1996]] matters here because it gives the two-systems side an early, explicit computational articulation in terms of associative and rule-based reasoning. [[Evans 2008]] and [[Evans and Stanovich 2013]] then show how the dispute also depends on models of cognitive architecture, [[Keren and Schul 2009]] questions whether two-system theorizing is often evidentially stronger than it really is, and the [[De Neys 2012]] / [[De Neys 2023]] pair shows that some of the debate happens inside dual-process theorizing itself. [[Petracca 2017]] adds a different and now important layer: some rationality disputes are really clashes between cognitive paradigms, especially between [[Cognitivism]] and later views such as [[Situated cognition]] and [[Embodied cognition]]. The newer sources broaden the dispute in four more directions. [[Mercier and Sperber 2011]] asks what reasoning is for and argues for an [[Argumentative theory of reasoning]]. [[Stanovich and West 2000]] argues that the debate should be constrained by individual-differences evidence rather than by unconstrained reinterpretation of classic tasks. [[Stanovich and West 2007]] adds that [[Myside bias]] remains robust even among high-ability reasoners, which prevents rationality from being equated with intelligence alone. [[Bernheim 2025]] pushes the dispute into the normative-policy register by arguing that even when one adopts a rational benchmark, not every deviation should count as a mistake that policy is entitled to correct. [[Berg and Gigerenzer 2010]] adds a methodological challenge inside behavioral economics itself by arguing that much of the field still relies on a Friedman-style `as-if` methodology even while presenting itself as behaviorally more realistic.

## Key distinctions

The most important distinction in the current cluster is between formal or logical approaches to rationality and ecological or context-sensitive approaches such as [[Ecological rationality]]. [[Gigerenzer 2008]] sharpens that distinction by tying ecological rationality to an [[Adaptive toolbox]] of heuristics rather than to a simple defense of error, while [[Gigerenzer 2024]] makes clear how much of the dispute concerned benchmark choice and disciplinary framing. [[Smith 2003]] complicates the ecological side by distinguishing [[Constructivist rationality]] from ecological rationality and by extending the ecological idea from individual heuristics to emergent institutions and market order. [[Dekker and Remic 2019]] pushes the point further: the ecological side is not one unified alternative. It contains at least two different programs, one centered on individual heuristics and one centered on system-level institutional order. [[Berg and Gigerenzer 2010]] adds a different methodological distinction inside behavioral economics itself: even when the field rejects neoclassical descriptive assumptions, it may still preserve a Friedman-style `as-if` methodology by keeping optimization frameworks and adding psychologically labeled parameters. [[Petracca 2017]] adds another distinction the vault now needs to keep visible: some disputes are not only about standards of rationality, but about the ontology of cognition itself. Simonian [[Bounded rationality]] was originally embedded in [[Cognitivism]], whereas later challenges from [[Situated cognition]] and [[Embodied cognition]] question the idea that realistic cognition is basically internal symbolic information processing. Another important distinction, emphasized by [[Samuels et al. 2002]], is between real disagreement and disagreement generated by shifting standards of epistemic evaluation. The dual-process branch adds a further distinction inside cognitive architecture itself: are intuition and reflection best modeled as sharply separate systems, as in [[Sloman 1996]] and later disciplined type-1/type-2 approaches, or as interacting processes that can include [[Logical intuitions]] and conflict sensitivity? [[Mercier and Sperber 2011]] adds a different axis of dispute by shifting the question from standards and architecture to function: perhaps reasoning is best understood as a social device for producing and evaluating arguments. The Stanovich-West branch adds a methodological distinction as well. Some defenses of ordinary reasoning are too cheap if they are not constrained by evidence about who performs better, under what conditions, and on which tasks. [[Stanovich and West 2007]] then sharpens the issue further by showing that some distortions, especially [[Myside bias]], do not disappear just because a thinker has higher cognitive ability. The debate therefore separates descriptive from normative uses of rationality, but also functional from benchmark-based accounts, institutional from individual ecological arguments, intelligence-centered from bias-sensitive accounts, two different meanings of ecological rationality itself, cognition-paradigm disputes from benchmark disputes, and behavioral realism from methodological realism.

## Evidence and debate

[[Gigerenzer 2008]], [[Gigerenzer 2024]], [[Smith 2003]], [[Dekker and Remic 2019]], [[Berg and Gigerenzer 2010]], [[Hortal 2025]], [[Samuels et al. 2002]], [[Sloman 1996]], [[Evans 2008]], [[Evans and Stanovich 2013]], [[Keren and Schul 2009]], [[De Neys 2012]], [[De Neys 2023]], [[Mercier and Sperber 2011]], [[Stanovich and West 2000]], and [[Stanovich and West 2007]] are the current anchor sources. Gigerenzer provides the direct primary case that heuristics can be rational because they fit environments, and his later reflection clarifies how the debate looked from inside that side of the dispute. Smith provides a complementary case from experimental economics in which ecological rationality applies to institutions and emergent market order rather than only to individual heuristics. Hortal frames the conflict between [[Heuristics and Biases]] and ecological or pluralist approaches as a broad philosophical dispute across disciplines. Samuels, Stich, and Bishop focus more narrowly on the clash between [[Heuristics and Biases]] and [[Evolutionary psychology]] and argue that much of the conflict is rhetorical or driven by differing standards.

[[Berg and Gigerenzer 2010]] matters because it adds a methodological accusation that is not captured by the usual benchmark dispute alone. Its claim is not simply that behavioral economics is descriptively richer than neoclassicism or that it is merely descriptive rather than normative. The sharper claim is that behavioral economics often still theorizes in the same Friedman-style way: it preserves optimization architectures, adds parameters such as probability weights or social-preference terms, and then treats improved fit as if it solved the deeper explanatory problem. That makes the rationality wars partly about model form, not only about which benchmark is used to judge outcomes.

[[Petracca 2017]] matters because it shows that the vault's Simon branch cannot be treated as merely an earlier stage of the same conversation. The paper reconstructs the Simon-versus-situated-cognition clash as a genuine paradigm conflict. That gives the rationality wars a deeper historical dimension: some later disputes over heuristics, ecological fit, and psychological realism are built on an unresolved disagreement about whether cognition is fundamentally symbolic, situated, distributed, or embodied.

[[Sloman 1996]] matters because it clarifies why two-systems language became so influential in the first place. Instead of treating dual-process theory as a loose metaphor, Sloman frames the contrast computationally: associative reasoning works through similarity and temporal structure, while rule-based reasoning works through symbolic structures with variables and logical form. That makes the later Evans-Stanovich branch easier to situate historically, and it also explains why critics such as Keren and Schul target strong architectural claims rather than only loose pedagogical shorthand.

After comparing [[Smith 2003]] with the current Gigerenzer sources in light of [[Dekker and Remic 2019]], the best reading in the vault is no longer that the two simply offer layered emphases within one ecological framework. They share a common enemy and a family resemblance, but they are distinct programs. Gigerenzer is focused on individual cognition, cue use, and heuristics in task environments. Smith is focused on emergent order, institutions, and the limits of rational design. The internal structure of the ecological camp is therefore itself part of the rationality wars.

Evans and Stanovich show that some rationality disputes also depend on whether dual-process architecture is treated as a defensible explanatory framework. Keren and Schul add a methodological criticism from inside that same terrain by arguing that evidence for dual systems is often weaker or looser than the rhetoric suggests. De Neys adds a different kind of challenge: some apparent disputes over rationality are really disputes over how to model intuition, reflection, and conflict detection in the first place.

[[Mercier and Sperber 2011]] broadens the debate beyond standards and architecture by arguing that reasoning is primarily argumentative. The surrounding exchange is part of what makes the source valuable here. De Neys pushes back by citing evidence for logical conflict detection even in nonargumentative settings. Evans argues that argumentative function may be primary without exhausting reasoning's role in reflection and prospective thought. Weber and Johnson suggest that query theory shows how people can also "argue with themselves" in the construction of preferences. Mercier and Sperber's reply resists turning these replies into a retreat, but the exchange makes clear that the rationality wars are also partly about whether reasoning is best understood as neutral correction, social argument, or something in between.

The Stanovich-West pair adds a different kind of discipline. [[Stanovich and West 2000]] argues that appeals to alternative construal, wrong norm, or mere performance error need stronger empirical constraint, especially through individual-differences evidence. [[Stanovich and West 2007]] then shows that at least one major bias, [[Myside bias]], remains robust even among cognitively able thinkers. Together, these sources make the page less one-sided: the rationality wars may be deep in some places, overstated in others, and tractable only when the evaluation standard, the function of reasoning, and the evidence about individual variation are all explicit.

## Practical or policy relevance

The concept matters because [[Behavioral public policy]] depends on assumptions about rationality. Whether policy should correct citizens, support adaptive heuristics, or blend multiple approaches depends on which standard is being used. [[Bernheim 2025]] adds that a second question has to be asked after the benchmark question: even if a deviation is real, when does it justify saying that the chooser has made a mistake rather than simply departed from one evaluative ideal? The rationality wars therefore shape not only theory but also the legitimacy of policy interventions.

## Open questions

Can pluralism about rationality be turned into operational guidance rather than staying at a philosophical level? Which disagreements in the rationality wars are genuinely substantive, and which are artifacts of using different standards or targets of evaluation? And does the debate turn partly on what reasoning is for, not only on which norm it should satisfy?
