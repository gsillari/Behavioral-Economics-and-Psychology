# Rationality wars

## Core idea

[[Rationality wars]] refers to the contemporary dispute over what rationality is, how it should be measured, and which standards should guide explanation and policy. In the current vault, the cleanest way to understand the dispute is as five connected fights rather than one giant argument:

1. benchmark disputes about logic, probability, and expected utility
2. ecological disputes about fit, heuristics, and institutions
3. cognitive-architecture disputes about intuition, reflection, and dual-process models
4. methodology disputes about `as-if` explanation and psychological realism
5. normative disputes about when a deviation from a benchmark counts as a policy-relevant mistake

That structure is what makes the page cohere. [[Kahneman and Tversky 1996]], [[Gigerenzer 1996]], [[Gigerenzer 2008]], [[Gigerenzer 2024]], [[Smith 1991]], [[Smith 2003]], [[Hortal 2025]], [[Rich 2016]], and [[Samuels et al. 2002]] help organize the benchmark and ecological sides. [[Sloman 1996]], [[Evans 2008]], [[Evans and Stanovich 2013]], [[Keren and Schul 2009]], [[De Neys 2012]], and [[De Neys 2023]] organize the cognitive-architecture side. [[Petracca 2017]] and [[Berg and Gigerenzer 2010]] make clear that some disputes are really about deeper views of mind and method. [[Mercier and Sperber 2011]], [[Stanovich and West 2000]], [[Stanovich and West 2007]], and [[Bernheim 2025]] then push the debate into function, evidence, and policy.

## Key distinctions

The first distinction is between benchmark-centered and fit-centered rationality. On one side are approaches that evaluate reasoning by conformity to logic, probability, or coherence. On the other side are ecological approaches such as [[Ecological rationality]], where the key question is whether a rule works well in its environment.

The second distinction is inside the ecological camp itself. [[Smith 2003]] and [[Dekker and Remic 2019]] make clear that ecological rationality is not one unified alternative. There is a heuristics-and-cues version associated with [[Gigerenzer 2008]] and an institutional-emergence version associated with Smith. That internal split is part of the wars, not a footnote to them.

The third distinction is between benchmark disputes and cognition-paradigm disputes. [[Petracca 2017]] shows why this matters. Some disagreements are not only about how to score reasoning. They are also about what minds are like. Simonian [[Bounded rationality]] remains partly rooted in [[Cognitivism]], whereas [[Situated cognition]] and [[Embodied cognition]] challenge the idea that realistic cognition is mainly internal symbolic processing.

The fourth distinction is between loose dual-process talk and stronger architectural claims. [[Sloman 1996]] and later type-1/type-2 theorists argue that intuition and reflection correspond to importantly different forms of processing. [[Keren and Schul 2009]] and [[De Neys 2023]] insist that this contrast should not be turned too quickly into a crude two-box model.

The fifth distinction is between methodological and normative uses of rationality. [[Berg and Gigerenzer 2010]] argues that much behavioral economics still preserves Friedman-style `as-if` explanation even while presenting itself as more realistic. [[Bernheim 2025]] adds that even a justified benchmark does not automatically tell policy when a deviation is a mistake rather than simply a departure from one evaluative ideal.

The sixth distinction is between winner-take-all and hybrid interpretations of the wars. [[Rich 2016]] matters here because it argues that the strongest versions of axiomatic and ecological rationality are not best treated as mutually exclusive frameworks. For the purpose of improving reasoning, she argues, each has a distinctive cost-benefit profile, and a hybrid that uses axiomatic criteria to evaluate candidate processes may be better than either pure approach.

## Evidence and debate

The benchmark-and-ecology front is anchored first of all by the 1996 exchange between [[Kahneman and Tversky 1996]] and [[Gigerenzer 1996]], then by [[Gigerenzer 2008]], [[Gigerenzer 2024]], [[Smith 1991]], [[Smith 2003]], [[Dekker and Remic 2019]], [[Hortal 2025]], [[Rich 2016]], and [[Samuels et al. 2002]]. The 1996 exchange matters because it fixes the classic battle lines. Kahneman and Tversky defend the reality of cognitive illusions and deny that benchmark-sensitive errors disappear once tasks are moved into frequency language. Gigerenzer replies that the core problem is not whether one can rescue some results, but whether narrow norms and vague heuristics can ground a satisfactory science of reasoning in the first place. In the current vault, these are the foundational polemical papers of the rationality wars. Later sources refine positions first drawn there rather than replacing them. Gigerenzer 2008 then provides the direct positive case that heuristics can be rational because they fit environments. [[Smith 1991]] is important because it gives the earlier experimental-economics version of the critique: psychology and mainstream economics both overlocate rationality in isolated cognition, while interactive institutions can generate rational outcomes that participants neither understand nor intend. [[Smith 2003]] then gives the later institutional counterpart in the explicit language of ecological rationality and emergent order. [[Dekker and Remic 2019]] matters especially because it prevents the vault from treating these as one seamless program. They are neighboring ecological camps, not merely different emphases. [[Hortal 2025]] broadens that clash into a philosophical dispute over epistemic standards. [[Rich 2016]] sharpens it in a different way by asking what these standards are for. Her claim is not that ecological and axiomatic rationality collapse into one framework, but that each buys different normative advantages. That matters because it shows that some rationality wars are partly fights over evaluative purpose, not only over empirical adequacy. [[Samuels et al. 2002]] then reminds the reader that some apparently deep conflicts dissolve once the standards of evaluation are made explicit.

The cognitive-architecture front is anchored by [[Sloman 1996]], [[Evans 2008]], [[Evans and Stanovich 2013]], [[Keren and Schul 2009]], [[De Neys 2012]], and [[De Neys 2023]]. [[Sloman 1996]] is the key early articulation because it gives dual-process language real computational content: associative and rule-based reasoning are not just two moods of thought but different forms of processing. [[Evans and Stanovich 2013]] then defend a more disciplined type-1/type-2 framework, while [[Keren and Schul 2009]] warn that weak evidence is often treated as if it justified stronger architectural claims than it really does. [[De Neys 2012]] and [[De Neys 2023]] add the most important internal revision: intuitive processing may already register conflict, so the interesting question is not simply whether fast thought errs and slow thought corrects, but how the two interact.

The cognition-paradigm front is anchored by [[Petracca 2017]]. Its importance is larger than one Simon interpretation. The paper shows that some later rationality disputes inherit an older unresolved conflict about what minds are like. If Simonian bounded rationality remains fundamentally [[Cognitivism|cognitivist]], then later appeals to ecological fit or behavioral realism do not automatically count as a break with internalist information-processing models. This gives the rationality wars a deeper historical layer than benchmark disputes alone can capture.

The methodology front is anchored first by [[Gigerenzer 1996]], then more broadly by [[Berg and Gigerenzer 2010]] and [[Smith 1991]]. Gigerenzer's 1996 complaint is methodological before it is anything else: norms are too narrow and the heuristics invoked to explain error are too vague. Berg and Gigerenzer later restate a related dissatisfaction from another angle by arguing that much behavioral economics still explains in a Friedman-style `as-if` way: optimization architectures are preserved, psychologically labeled parameters are added, and better fit is treated as if it settled the explanatory question. Smith adds a different but complementary criticism: both psychologists and economists can misframe the dispute if they assume that the right test of rationality is always the isolated subject facing a described choice problem. Together, these papers show that the rationality wars are partly about model form, benchmark scope, unit of analysis, and explanatory ambition, not only about which benchmark is normatively correct.

The function-and-evidence front is anchored by [[Mercier and Sperber 2011]], [[Stanovich and West 2000]], and [[Stanovich and West 2007]]. [[Mercier and Sperber 2011]] asks whether reasoning is primarily for argument and social evaluation rather than for neutral self-correction. The exchange around that paper matters because it shows that dual-process and rationality disputes are also disputes about what reasoning is for. The Stanovich-West pair adds empirical discipline from another angle: claims that ordinary responses are secretly rational need stronger evidence, and some distortions such as [[Myside bias]] remain robust even among highly capable reasoners. Together, these sources keep the page from collapsing into simple pro- or anti-bias polemics.

## Practical or policy relevance

The concept matters because [[Behavioral public policy]] depends on assumptions about rationality. Whether policy should correct citizens, support adaptive heuristics, or blend multiple approaches depends on which standard is being used. [[Bernheim 2025]] adds that a second question has to be asked after the benchmark question: even if a deviation is real, when does it justify saying that the chooser has made a mistake rather than simply departed from one evaluative ideal? The rationality wars therefore shape not only theory but also the legitimacy of policy interventions.

## Open questions

Can pluralism about rationality be turned into operational guidance rather than staying at a philosophical level? Which disagreements in the rationality wars are genuinely substantive, and which are artifacts of using different standards or targets of evaluation? And does the debate turn partly on what reasoning is for, not only on which norm it should satisfy?
