# Framing effects

## Core idea

[[Framing effects]] occur when different descriptions of substantively similar options produce different choices.
In the present vault, the starting point for this concept is [[Kahneman and Tversky 1979]], where differences in gain and loss framing are closely tied to [[Prospect Theory]] and [[Reference dependence]].
The concept matters because it makes representation itself part of the causal story of choice.

## Key distinctions

Framing effects are about representation, not only about preference intensity. The same outcome can look attractive or unattractive depending on whether it is described as preserving gains, avoiding losses, or accepting risk. That means framing is closely connected to [[Loss aversion]] but is not reducible to it: framing sets the interpretive surface on which gains and losses are perceived.

The concept also differs from simple persuasion. A framing effect does not require false information or overt pressure. It can arise even when the underlying information is substantively equivalent. That is why the concept is so important in rationality debates: it suggests that formally equivalent choices are not always psychologically equivalent choices.

## Evidence and debate

The classic importance of the concept comes from experimental choice problems in which equivalent options yield different decisions under different verbal or structural presentations.
In the first source cluster, the evidence comes from [[Kahneman and Tversky 1979]] and is then presented in especially clear pedagogical form in [[Tversky and Kahneman 1981 (Framing)]], whose gain-loss problems make the reflection pattern vivid even for first-time readers.
[[Kahneman 2003]] then matters because it places framing inside a broader account of intuitive judgment and bounded rationality rather than treating it only as one empirical curiosity.
[[DellaVigna 2009]] broadens the picture by treating framing as one member of a larger family of field-relevant departures from the standard model, which helps show why framing matters outside textbook experiments.
[[Mercer 2005]] and [[Levy 1997]] extend that lesson into politics. They show that framing matters not only for individual risky choice but also for political decision-making, especially when leaders or publics interpret outcomes as losses to be avoided or gains to be protected. [[Shafir et al. 1997]] adds a macroeconomically important variation on the same theme. In [[Money illusion]], equivalent or near-equivalent economic situations can feel different depending on whether they are represented in nominal or real terms.

Debate concerns whether framing reveals unstable preferences, context-sensitive rationality, or the unavoidable dependence of judgment on representation.
In the current vault, that debate matters because framing sits at the intersection of descriptive psychology and normative concern.
If framing changes choice without changing substance, then the interpretation of revealed preference becomes more difficult and the legitimacy of institutional presentation becomes more contested.

## Practical or policy relevance

Framing effects matter whenever institutions communicate risk, eligibility, sanctions, or benefits.
Public messages about health, taxation, safety, and compliance can change behavior without changing the underlying incentives.
That makes framing a central policy concept within [[Behavioral Economics]], but it also raises legitimacy questions about when strategic presentation becomes manipulation.
The politics-facing branch now adds another reason the concept matters: foreign-policy crises, reform debates, and security choices can depend on whether options are represented as preserving gains, avoiding losses, or accepting decline. That is one reason framing belongs not only in policy pages but also in [[Behavioral political economy]].

This is why framing should remain tightly connected to both [[Behavioral public policy]] and [[What Behavioral Economics Thinks Rationality Is]].
It is one of the clearest cases where a descriptive finding immediately becomes a normative and political question.

## Open questions

Which framing effects reflect shallow wording changes and which expose deep differences in how people represent choices? How should institutions communicate clearly without exploiting predictable framing asymmetries?
