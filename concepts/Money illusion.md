# Money illusion

## Core idea

[[Money illusion]] is the tendency to think in nominal rather than fully real terms when evaluating wages, prices, contracts, gains, and losses. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Shafir et al. 1997]], which argues that people often represent economic outcomes in both nominal and real terms but give excessive weight to the nominal representation.

## Key distinctions

The concept differs from a simple calculation error. People need not be incapable of understanding inflation or real value in principle. The problem is that nominal magnitudes often remain psychologically salient even when real comparison is the normatively relevant one.

The concept is also closely related to [[Framing effects]]. A nominal wage cut and a real wage cut brought about by inflation can be economically similar while still feeling very different. That means money illusion is one of the clearest macroeconomic cases in which representation changes evaluation.

## Evidence and debate

[[Shafir et al. 1997]] matters because it gives the branch a clear behavioral account rather than only an economist's complaint about irrationality. The paper argues that nominal and real representations coexist, and that bias emerges because the nominal representation often dominates. In the current vault, that matters because it lets the concept connect to both macroeconomic problems and the wider behavioral literature on framing and evaluation.

The main debate is whether money illusion should be treated as a deep irrationality, a boundedly rational shortcut, or a predictable consequence of how people represent economic outcomes in ordinary life. That matters because different answers support different policy attitudes toward inflation, wage rigidity, and economic communication.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters for [[Behavioral public policy]] because taxation, wage policy, inflation communication, and contract design all depend on whether people respond to nominal and real changes in the same way. It also matters for [[Behavioral political economy]] because macroeconomic perception and political reaction can turn on nominal framing rather than on economically cleaner real comparisons.

## Open questions

How stable is money illusion across high-inflation and low-inflation environments? When do institutions correct it, and when do they strategically exploit it? And should the concept be treated mainly as a framing problem, a macroeconomic perception problem, or both?
