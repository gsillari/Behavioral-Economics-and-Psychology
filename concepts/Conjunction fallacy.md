# Conjunction fallacy

## Core idea

[[Conjunction fallacy]] is the error of judging a conjunction of events to be more probable than one of its components. In the current vault, the main journal-article anchor is [[Tversky and Kahneman 1983]], where the effect is explained through [[Representativeness heuristic]]: a more detailed, more coherent event can feel more likely because it is more representative, even though its formal probability must be lower. [[Tversky and Kahneman 1981]] remains an important earlier branch source in the vault.

## Key distinctions

The concept differs from [[Base-rate neglect]], though the two are related. Base-rate neglect concerns underweighting prior frequency information. Conjunction fallacy concerns the relation between descriptive coherence and probability ordering. In both cases, representational fit can overpower formal reasoning, but the logical structure of the error is different.

It also differs from a mere wording confusion in the way the concept is used in this vault. The point is not simply that people misunderstand a phrase. The stronger claim is that intuitive plausibility can systematically dominate extensional probability reasoning.

## Evidence and debate

[[Tversky and Kahneman 1983]] is the foundational source in the current vault because it explicitly contrasts extensional with intuitive reasoning and links the fallacy directly to representativeness. [[Tversky and Kahneman 1981]] remains useful as an earlier neighboring source in the same branch. [[Kahneman and Frederick 2002]] matters because it gives a later process interpretation through [[Attribute substitution]]: the hard question about probability is replaced by an easier question about resemblance or coherence.

The main debate is whether conjunction errors reveal a deep and general inferential pattern or whether they depend strongly on wording, pragmatics, or task format. In the current vault, the concept should remain tied to the representativeness branch rather than treated as an isolated curiosity.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because public reasoning, forecasting, and political judgment are often organized by narrative coherence. Detailed scenarios can feel more plausible than simpler alternatives even when they are formally less probable. That matters for risk communication, scenario planning, intelligence analysis, and public debate.
