# Betrayal aversion

## Core idea

[[Betrayal aversion]] is the additional reluctance to accept a bad outcome when that outcome would result from another person's betrayal rather than from chance. It is the concept that keeps [[Trust]] from collapsing into ordinary [[Risk attitudes]].

[[Bohnet and Zeckhauser 2004]] is the anchor source. They compare matched trust and risk settings and find that subjects require a higher minimum acceptable probability in the trust condition than in non-social risk conditions with the same material payoffs. The interpretation is that being betrayed carries a cost beyond losing the gamble.

## Why it matters

This concept matters because many economic interactions involve vulnerability to another agent: employment, delegation, contracting, lending, friendship, institutional compliance, and online exchange. If betrayal is psychologically different from bad luck, then trust cannot be modeled only as expected value plus risk aversion.

It also helps connect the social-preference branch to decision theory. Risk attitudes concern preferences over uncertain outcomes. Betrayal aversion concerns the social source of the uncertainty and the meaning of the bad outcome.

## Open questions

How should betrayal aversion be separated from pessimistic beliefs about others, loss aversion, and loss of control? Does betrayal aversion vary across institutions, identities, or histories of interaction? When should policy try to reduce betrayal risk, and when should it build mechanisms that make trust less necessary?
