# Projection bias

## Core idea

[[Projection bias]] is the tendency to expect future preferences to be too similar to current preferences. In the current vault, the anchor source is now [[Loewenstein et al. 2007 - Projection Bias in Predicting Future Utility (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 13)]], which treats the idea as a general mechanism of mispredicting future utility rather than as a loose label for a few field anomalies. [[DellaVigna 2009]] remains important because it shows how the mechanism travels into real economic settings.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Time inconsistency]]. Time inconsistency is about dynamic preference reversal or changing weights on present and future payoffs, while projection bias is about mistaken forecasts of future tastes, needs, or feelings. The two often interact, but they are not the same problem. It also differs from [[Overconfidence]], which is about one's own ability or information rather than about future states of desire. And it differs from [[Emotions in decision-making]], because projection bias is specifically about carrying current states into future prediction rather than about emotion affecting present choice directly.

## Evidence and debate

[[Loewenstein et al. 2007 - Projection Bias in Predicting Future Utility (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 13)]] is the strongest source in the current vault because it makes projection bias explicit and general. People are said to overproject present hunger, fatigue, arousal, mood, weather, and other temporary conditions into the future. That gives the concept explanatory reach far beyond one market setting. The point is not just that people are uncertain about future circumstances. It is that present experience has a privileged and excessive influence on forecasts of future utility.

[[DellaVigna 2009]] then shows why the concept matters for behavioral economics more broadly. The field evidence on seasonal-product purchases, for example, fits the mechanism by suggesting that temporarily cold days can distort beliefs about future need and later regret. Read together, the two sources give the page a cleaner sequence: a direct theoretical statement from Loewenstein and collaborators, followed by field-oriented uptake in the behavioral-economics survey literature.

Debate centers on three issues. First, how sharply can misprediction of future utility be separated from reasonable uncertainty about future states? Second, when does projection bias overlap with self-control problems rather than stand apart from them? Third, which self should matter normatively when present and future evaluations conflict?

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many economic decisions are made under temporary states that should not govern long-run choice. Shopping while hungry, buying goods during unusual weather, or making plans under emotional intensity can all create predictable regret. For policy and institutional design, projection bias matters for cooling-off periods, delayed implementation, return policies, reminder systems, and the timing of decisions. It is one of the clearest places where behavioral economics challenges the assumption that present choice straightforwardly reveals future welfare.

## Open questions

How often can projection bias be separated empirically from rational learning about future conditions? Which interventions best reduce projection bias without constraining choice too heavily? When do firms exploit projection bias, and when do they design around it?
