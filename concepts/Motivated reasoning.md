# Motivated reasoning

## Core idea

[[Motivated reasoning]] is the idea that reasoning can be steered by goals and desires without ceasing to look like reasoning. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Kunda 1990]], which argues that people rely selectively on memories, beliefs, and inferential strategies that support a preferred conclusion, while [[Epley and Gilovich 2016]] offers a compact process account of how this happens. The key point is that directionally biased reasoning is usually constrained by the need to preserve a sense of plausibility and justification.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from a simple claim that people believe whatever they want. The Kunda-Epley-Gilovich branch is stronger and more interesting: people are often motivated, but they are still responsive to what they can make look and feel reasonable. It also differs from many classic heuristic effects in [[Heuristics and Biases]]. Those effects are often framed as shortcut-based departures from formal norms. Motivated reasoning adds a different source of distortion, namely that people may search, interpret, and weight information in goal-consistent ways.

It also differs from crude dual-process talk in which motivated error is always just a case of hot intuition defeating cold reflection. In many cases, the reasoning itself is reflective, articulate, and justificatory. The bias lies in how reflection is guided. That is one reason the page now sits close to [[Argumentative theory of reasoning]], which suggests that reasoning's social function may help explain why justification and persuasion are so central to distorted reflection.

## Evidence and debate

[[Kunda 1990]] provides the foundational theoretical statement by distinguishing accuracy goals from directional goals and arguing that both shape the deployment of cognitive processes. [[Epley and Gilovich 2016]] then makes the mechanism easier to teach and compare with other branches of the vault by emphasizing selective recruitment and interpretation of evidence. [[Mercier and Sperber 2011]] adds a broader functional hypothesis: some of the selective, justificatory character of motivated reasoning may reflect the fact that reasoning is adapted for argument rather than detached contemplation. [[Babcock and Loewenstein 2007 - Explaining the Bargaining Impasse - The Role of Self-Serving Biases (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 8)]] adds a concrete negotiation case in which parties weight the same arguments differently depending on their role while preserving a sense of fairness and objectivity. In the current wiki, this matters because it broadens the beliefs branch beyond calibration errors such as [[Overconfidence]] or [[Law of small numbers]].

The main debate is how sharply motivated reasoning should be distinguished from neighboring ideas such as confirmation bias, [[Self-serving bias]], and partisan belief formation. Another debate concerns normativity: if people remain sensitive to reasons and evidence, but in a tilted way, how irrational are the resulting beliefs?

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many of the most consequential disagreements in politics and public life are not well described as random ignorance. They involve structured, goal-sensitive reasoning about evidence, fairness, risk, and identity. For PPE students, this makes motivated reasoning especially important as a bridge concept: it links psychology of belief to normative questions about rational agency and to political questions about polarization, public reasoning, and institutional trust.
