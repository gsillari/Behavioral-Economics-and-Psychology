# Ultimatum game

## Core idea

The [[Ultimatum game]] is a two-person bargaining experiment in which one player proposes a division of a fixed surplus and the other player either accepts or rejects. If the responder accepts, the proposed split is implemented. If the responder rejects, both players receive nothing.

Its importance comes from the sharp contrast between the narrow game-theoretic prediction and observed behavior. With common knowledge of self-interest, the proposer should offer the smallest positive amount and the responder should accept. [[Guth et al. 1982]] shows that this prediction does not describe actual play: proposers often make substantially fairer offers, and responders often reject low offers.

## What the game shows

The game is not just an anecdote about people being nice. It distinguishes several mechanisms. Proposers may care about fairness, may fear rejection, may want to avoid appearing rude, or may anticipate the responder's willingness to punish. Responders may reject because they dislike unequal outcomes, because they interpret low offers as insulting, or because they want to punish unkind treatment.

[[Camerer and Thaler 1995]] is the best compact guide to this interpretation. Their "manners" framing is useful because it keeps the game social: a low offer is not merely a small number, but an action with a meaning. [[Rabin 1993]] formalizes this kind of thought through reciprocity and perceived kindness. [[Fehr and Schmidt 1999]] formalizes a different route through [[Inequity aversion]].

[[Henrich et al. 2004 - Chapter 02 - Overview and Synthesis]] adds the cross-cultural pressure point. The student-population pattern is not a universal baseline: across the fifteen small-scale societies in [[Henrich et al. 2004]], offers and rejections vary substantially, and group-level features such as market integration and payoffs to cooperation matter more than simple individual predictors. This means the ultimatum game should be read as a diagnostic tool for social preferences and local norms, not as evidence for one universal fairness parameter.

## Open questions

How much ultimatum behavior reflects stable social preferences, and how much reflects social meaning created by the experimental frame? When do market competition, anonymity, or entitlement claims reduce willingness to punish low offers? Is rejection better modeled as inequity aversion, reciprocity, emotion, or norm enforcement?
