# Base-rate neglect

## Core idea

[[Base-rate neglect]] is the tendency to underweight prior statistical information when judging the likelihood that a case belongs to a category or that an outcome will occur. In the current vault, the concept is anchored by [[Kahneman and Tversky 1972]], with [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] as the broader heuristics-and-biases source and [[Tversky and Kahneman 1982 - Evidential Impact of Base Rates (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 10)]] as the clearest blue-book treatment.

The newer base-rate cluster now makes the concept more precise. [[Ajzen 1977]] shows that base rates matter more when they can be interpreted causally. [[Bar-Hillel 1980]] generalizes the point through relevance and specificity: base rates are neglected when they look like low-relevance population facts and used when they seem to bear directly on the target case. [[Fischhoff and Bar-Hillel 1984]] then shows that base-rate effects increase when individuating descriptions are less diagnostically representative.

## Key distinctions

The concept differs from a simple mistake about numbers. Its specific structure is that individuating description, perceived relevance, or coherence crowds out prior frequency information. It is closely tied to [[Representativeness heuristic]], because the case is often judged by resemblance to a prototype rather than by disciplined integration of statistical background information.

It also differs from [[Conjunction fallacy]]. In conjunction problems, descriptive richness can make a narrower event seem more likely than a broader one. In base-rate problems, the central mistake is that background frequencies lose weight relative to case fit.

The new sources also clarify an important qualification. Base-rate neglect is not universal insensitivity to frequencies. Base rates can influence judgment when they are the only information available, when they are framed as causally relevant, when they are made equally relevant to the target case, or when the individuating information is too neutral to guide classification.

## Evidence and debate

[[Kahneman and Tversky 1972]] is important because it gives this pattern an early and explicit home inside the representativeness branch. [[Tversky and Kahneman 1982 - Evidential Impact of Base Rates (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 10)]] then gives the problem its clearest direct blue-book treatment by asking how people combine background frequencies with case-specific description and by showing how weak that integration often is. [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] makes the phenomenon part of the canonical heuristics vocabulary. [[Tversky and Kahneman 1981]] matters because it shows how the same pull toward representational fit also helps explain more dramatic departures from formal probability judgment.

[[Ajzen 1977]] adds the causal-interpretation branch. The paper shows that people may use base rates when those rates can be built into an intuitive causal theory of the outcome, but neglect them when they look noncausal. [[Bar-Hillel 1980]] adds the relevance branch. Its central claim is that people order information by perceived relevance to the target case; more specific, individuating, identifying, or causal information can dominate less specific population statistics even when the latter are normatively required. [[Fischhoff and Bar-Hillel 1984]] adds the diagnosticity branch. It shows that base-rate effects grow as descriptions become less differentially representative of one category rather than another; when representativeness fails to decide, subjects make more use of base rates, often through probability matching rather than Bayesian integration.

In the current vault, base-rate neglect should be read as one of the clearest bridges from [[Representativeness heuristic]] to [[Nonstandard beliefs]]. It is the point where a general mechanism becomes a specific failure of probabilistic reasoning.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because citizens, professionals, and policymakers often reason from vivid cases instead of background rates. Medical diagnosis, criminal profiling, hiring, consumer judgment, and public risk assessment can all be distorted when a compelling case narrative crowds out statistical context. [[Bar-Hillel 1980]] is especially useful here because it explicitly connects the problem to clinical prediction, legal evidence, dangerousness prediction, and medical testing.
