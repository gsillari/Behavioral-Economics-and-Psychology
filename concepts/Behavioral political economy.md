# Behavioral political economy

## Core idea

[[Behavioral political economy]] applies behavioral assumptions to political choice, collective decision-making, and institutional analysis rather than reserving them for markets alone. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Schnellenbach and Schubert 2015]], which argues that voters, politicians, regulators, and bureaucrats should not be treated as behaviorally pristine while market agents are treated as biased.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Behavioral public policy]]. Behavioral public policy asks how institutions should respond to behavioral findings. Behavioral political economy asks how political actors and political processes themselves are behaviorally structured. It also differs from [[Prospect Theory]], which is a model of choice under risk; behavioral political economy is a broader application layer in which prospect-theory-style mechanisms become politically relevant.

## Evidence and debate

[[Schnellenbach and Schubert 2015]] gives the current vault its broadest map of the field by insisting on behavioral symmetry between markets and politics. [[Mercer 2005]] matters because it reviews how prospect theory travels into political science more generally and asks why many political scientists remain reluctant to use it. [[Levy 1997]] matters because it gives the most careful current statement of the international-relations side: prospect theory yields interesting hypotheses for foreign policy, but external validity becomes much harder once collective actors, strategy, and high stakes enter the picture. [[Shafir et al. 1997]] adds a different route into the branch through [[Money illusion]]. It shows that public and political reaction to inflation, wages, and nominal economic change may be behaviorally structured by representation itself, not only by interests or objective real outcomes.

The main debate is whether behavioral findings should mostly motivate better institutional design or whether they should first undermine overly optimistic views of political correction. In the current vault, that makes the concept especially important for PPE students because it complicates any simple story in which biased citizens are corrected by unbiased states.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many normative arguments in behavioral economics rely, implicitly or explicitly, on a contrast between biased choosers and more competent institutions. Behavioral political economy weakens that contrast. It suggests that the political process itself may amplify, redirect, or strategically exploit behavioral patterns. That makes it important both for institutional humility and for more realistic policy design.

## Open questions
