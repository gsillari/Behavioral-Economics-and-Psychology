# Argumentative theory of reasoning

## Core idea

[[Argumentative theory of reasoning]] is the view that reasoning's primary function is social and argumentative: producing and evaluating arguments in contexts of disagreement, justification, and persuasion. In the current vault, the anchor source is [[Mercier and Sperber 2011]]. The main shift introduced by the theory is that reasoning should not first be explained as a neutral truth-seeking device for solitary thinkers. Instead, its characteristic strengths and failures make more sense once argument exchange is treated as its home environment.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from the benchmark-centered parts of the [[Rationality wars]], which ask which norm should be used to evaluate thought. The argumentative theory asks a prior question about function: what reasoning is for. It also differs from standard [[Fast and slow thinking]] accounts. Dual-process theory often describes how reflective reasoning operates. The argumentative theory asks why a capacity for reflective justification and critique would have evolved and why it so often appears self-serving in individual settings. It also sits near [[Motivated reasoning]] and [[Confirmation bias]] without collapsing into them. Those pages track distortions in belief formation. The argumentative theory offers a broader explanation of why such distortions might be built into a faculty that is socially useful.

## Evidence and debate

[[Mercier and Sperber 2011]] provides the direct case for the theory. The BBS exchange around the paper is part of what makes it especially valuable in the vault. [[Wim De Neys]] argues that people show logical conflict detection even outside argumentative settings, so the social-function claim should not be pushed too far. [[Jonathan St. B. T. Evans]] argues that reasoning may have been exapted for private thought and prospective evaluation, not only for outward debate. Weber and Johnson's commentary on query theory suggests that people may also use reasoning intrapersonally by staging internal argument among competing considerations. In response, Mercier and Sperber defend the idea that these roles are secondary or derivative rather than equal competitors to the argumentative function.

In the current vault, that debate matters because it keeps the concept from becoming a slogan. The best use of the page is not to replace all other accounts of reasoning, but to add a functional layer to existing benchmark and process disputes.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many institutions rely on reasoning in adversarial settings: courts, legislatures, markets of ideas, committees, and public debate. If reasoning is partly adapted for argument, then institutional design should not expect neutral private contemplation to be the default case. It should ask when adversarial exchange corrects error and when it merely hardens prior commitments.

## Open questions

How much of reasoning's private problem-solving role should be treated as an exaptation rather than a coequal function? When does argumentative exchange improve collective judgment rather than amplifying bias? And how should PPE students evaluate institutions that depend on adversarial reasoning if the same faculty is both epistemically useful and systematically partisan?
