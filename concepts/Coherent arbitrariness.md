# Coherent arbitrariness

## Core idea

[[Coherent arbitrariness]] is the idea that valuations can be arbitrary in their absolute level yet coherent in their internal ordering. In the current vault, the anchor sources are [[Ariely et al. 2003]], which shows that irrelevant anchors can shape willingness to pay while leaving later relative comparisons stable enough to generate smooth-looking demand curves, and [[Ariely et al. 2006]], which extends the logic into experiential goods. The broader conceptual backdrop now includes [[Slovic 1995]] and [[Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006]], which place such findings inside the larger literature on [[Constructed preferences]] and violations of [[Procedure invariance]].

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from ordinary [[Anchoring]] because the effect is not only a biased estimate around a starting number. The stronger claim is that once an arbitrary valuation anchor is set, later choices can become orderly and internally consistent around it. It also differs from [[Reference dependence]]: reference dependence assumes outcomes are evaluated relative to a benchmark, whereas coherent arbitrariness raises the further possibility that the benchmark itself may be arbitrarily formed in a way that shapes the whole structure of valuation.

## Evidence and debate

[[Ariely et al. 2003]] uses experimental valuation tasks to show that arbitrary numbers can influence willingness to pay for familiar goods and hedonic experiences. The striking result is that these arbitrary starting points do not merely create random noise. They can generate stable-looking demand relations across quantities and qualities. [[Ariely et al. 2006]] extends that pattern by showing that even for experienced activities, early framing about whether one pays to consume them or is paid to endure them can structure later evaluations, connecting coherent arbitrariness to [[Preference uncertainty]]. [[Slovic 1995]] and [[Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006]] matter because they make clear that this is not an isolated anchoring trick but part of a broader problem of context-sensitive valuation and elicitation. Debate therefore centers on how seriously to take the normative implication: if valuations are both coherent and arbitrary, how much confidence should economists have that observed demand reveals true underlying preferences?

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because markets, institutions, and policies often infer welfare from demand behavior. If apparent demand regularities can be built on arbitrary anchors, then willingness to pay becomes a less secure guide to welfare than standard analysis assumes. That matters for pricing, consumer protection, valuation of public goods, compensation rules, and any policy domain that leans heavily on observed choices as evidence of what people value.

## Open questions

How common is coherent arbitrariness in high-stakes market settings rather than experiments? Which institutions amplify arbitrary anchors and which dampen them? Does coherent arbitrariness undermine revealed-preference welfare analysis completely or only in domains with weak prior valuations?
