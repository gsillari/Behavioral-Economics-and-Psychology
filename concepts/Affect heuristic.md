# Affect heuristic

## Core idea

[[Affect heuristic]] is the tendency to use an overall positive or negative feeling as a shortcut when judging risks, benefits, or other uncertain outcomes. In the current vault, the direct mechanism anchor is [[Finucane et al. 2000]], but the broader background now includes [[Slovic 1987]], which gives the risk branch a more foundational account of [[Risk perception]].

## Key distinctions

The concept differs from [[Availability heuristic]] because the key mechanism is not ease of recall by itself, but global affective evaluation. It also differs from [[Representativeness heuristic]] and [[Anchoring]] because the judgment is not primarily driven by resemblance or a starting value. In practice, however, the pages are related: vivid or salient information can shape affect, and affect can then organize later risk judgment.

## Evidence and debate

[[Slovic 1987]] matters here because it shows that risk judgments are organized by broad qualitative dimensions such as dread, controllability, and unknownness rather than by probability alone. [[Finucane et al. 2000]] then gives a tighter mechanism for part of that larger picture by arguing that perceived risk and perceived benefit often move in opposite directions because both are being read off from an overall feeling about the object in question. This matters because it helps explain why controversial technologies, activities, or policies can be judged through a broad evaluative lens rather than through separate calculations of upside and downside. In the current vault, this makes the affect heuristic an important extension of the older [[Heuristics and Biases]] tradition into risk perception and public judgment.

The main debate is how sharply this mechanism should be separated from neighboring ideas about salience, memory, framing, and intuitive evaluation. With the current source set, the page should remain focused and conservative: the affect heuristic is a mechanism for intuitive risk-benefit judgment, especially useful in policy-relevant settings.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because citizens and policymakers often encounter risks through emotionally charged descriptions, symbols, and narratives rather than through neutral statistical tables. That makes the affect heuristic highly relevant to [[Behavioral public policy]], risk regulation, public health messaging, and technological controversy. It helps explain why evidence alone may not settle disputes if the object of judgment already carries a strong positive or negative affective charge.
