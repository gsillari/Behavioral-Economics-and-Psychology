# Nonstandard beliefs

## Core idea

[[Nonstandard beliefs]] is the umbrella concept the current vault uses for departures from the standard assumption that people hold roughly correct, coherent, or well-calibrated beliefs about probabilities, states of the world, causal structure, and their own future behavior. The title is slightly awkward as a concept name, but it remains useful as a field-level organizing label because it captures a whole branch of behavioral work rather than a single mechanism.

In the current vault, the concept matters because behavioral economics does not only revise preferences and decision procedures. It also revises what we should expect from belief formation itself. People may misread frequencies, overweight vivid scenarios, underuse base rates, project present tastes into the future, reason toward preferred conclusions, or react differently when uncertainty cannot be represented by one sharp probability.

## Key distinctions

Three distinctions make the page easier to use.

First, this concept differs from [[Nonstandard preferences]], where the issue is what people want, and from [[Nonstandard decision-making]], where the issue is how choices are processed. Here the main problem is belief formation or uncertainty representation.

Second, not every case in this branch is a simple false belief. Some cases concern miscalibration or incoherence. Others concern the way uncertainty is represented. [[Ellsberg 1961]] matters for exactly this reason: the issue is not just “wrong probability,” but whether people are willing to treat ambiguous situations as if they were representable by the precise personal probabilities required by [[Subjective expected utility theory]].

Third, this is not just a list of biases. It is a meeting point between three layers of the vault:

1. formal benchmarks for probabilistic belief
2. psychological mechanisms of judgment under uncertainty
3. field evidence about the real-world consequences of distorted belief formation

## Benchmark background

[[de Finetti 1937]] and [[Subjective probability]] give the page its deepest benchmark by showing that belief can be personal while still being constrained by coherence. [[Savage 1954]] then matters because it turns that benchmark into part of a theory of action under uncertainty. [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 03 - Personal Probability]] and [[Savage 1954 - Chapter 04 - Critical Comments on Personal Probability]] show that the belief side of rationality is not only about loose confidence talk, but about a disciplined decision framework later collected in the vault under [[Subjective expected utility theory]].

[[Ellsberg 1961]] then complicates the picture. Through [[Ambiguity aversion]], it shows that some departures from standard belief modeling are not neatly cases of miscalculation. They may instead reflect resistance to representing uncertain situations with one crisp, decision-ready personal probability function. In the current vault, that is an important reminder that the belief branch is partly about the limits of the benchmark itself.

[[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]] gives the most useful later organizing frame. It shows that belief research can be evaluated using different standards such as accuracy, calibration, coherence, and practical usefulness, and it stresses that people may understand inferential tasks differently from the way researchers intend them.

## Main families of nonstandard belief

### 1. Representativeness and statistical inference

The first major family comes from the representativeness branch. [[Kahneman and Tversky 1972]] is especially important because it shows how subjective probability judgments can be driven by resemblance rather than by formal statistical reasoning. In the current vault, that branch now includes [[Base-rate neglect]], [[Conjunction fallacy]], and [[Law of small numbers]].

The base-rate branch now has a more precise internal structure. [[Ajzen 1977]] shows that base rates are more likely to be used when they have causal implications for the predicted event. [[Bar-Hillel 1980]] argues that people treat information as relevant when it is specific, identifying, or causal, and that low-relevance base rates can be dominated even when they are normatively probative. [[Fischhoff and Bar-Hillel 1984]] adds that base-rate use rises when individuating descriptions are less diagnostic. These sources make base-rate neglect a problem about the interpretation and perceived relevance of evidence, not just a failure to see numbers.

This family matters because it captures a recurring structure: people use an intuitively compelling cue and then treat it as if it were strong probabilistic evidence. The result is not random noise but a patterned displacement of statistical reasoning by similarity-based judgment.

### 2. Calibration, confidence, and updating

The second family concerns how people calibrate confidence and revise beliefs. [[Overconfidence]] is the clearest case in the field-evidence branch, but it sits beside several related patterns. [[Edwards 1982 - Conservatism in Human Information Processing (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 25)]] matters here because it captures under-updating relative to Bayesian norms. [[Hindsight bias]] matters because it distorts retrospective belief about what was foreseeable. [[Illusion of control]] matters because it attributes agency and predictability where chance is doing the work.

Taken together, these phenomena show that belief problems do not all look the same. Some involve too much certainty, some too little revision, and some retrospective overreading of the evidence once the result is already known.

### 3. Availability, salience, and fear

A third family arises when salient cases, vivid memories, or emotionally charged scenarios distort judgment. [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] is the broad upstream anchor because it makes the heuristics background explicit. [[Ross and Sicoly 1979]] adds a social-attribution variant by showing how cognitively available evidence can distort judgments of contribution and responsibility. [[Sunstein 2003]] adds [[Probability neglect]], where fear can crowd out attention to likelihood.

This family is important because it shows that distorted belief is often not a purely statistical failure. It can arise from memory, vividness, and affective salience.

### 4. Motivated and directional belief formation

The fourth family is the motivated-belief branch. [[Kunda 1990]] argues that beliefs and inferences can be steered by directional goals, while [[Epley and Gilovich 2016]] explains how this often works through selective search and weighting of otherwise ordinary evidence. [[Stanovich and West 2007]] sharpens the point through [[Myside bias]] by showing that some distortions persist even among cognitively capable agents.

This branch matters because it breaks the simple picture in which nonstandard belief is only a matter of weak reasoning skill. Sometimes people reason actively, but in a direction that is already goal-laden.

### 5. Beliefs about the self and the future

[[Projection bias]] is especially important here. It captures the way present tastes and states can distort beliefs about future preferences and future behavior. In the DellaVigna framework, that makes it a bridge between the belief branch and the preference branch: people do not merely want unstable things, they also mispredict what they themselves will later want.

## Field evidence and the DellaVigna umbrella

[[DellaVigna 2009]] is still the best anchor for why this concept belongs in behavioral economics rather than only in judgment-and-decision theory. It organizes field evidence on [[Overconfidence]], [[Law of small numbers]], and [[Projection bias]] and shows that distorted belief formation matters in finance, lotteries, corporate decisions, seasonal consumption, and planning.

That is why the present page should not try to compete with the leaf-level concepts. Its job is to explain what unifies them. The unifying thought is that behavior in the field often depends not just on nonstandard preferences or nonstandard choice procedures, but on systematically distorted expectations about the world, about probabilities, and about one’s own future conduct.

## Debate and limits

The central debate is not whether the individual patterns are real. It is how to interpret them.

Some cases look like clear departures from probabilistic norms. Others may be rational responses to ambiguity, sparse feedback, or researcher-imposed task formats. Still others may reflect motivated cognition rather than defective computation. That is why the page works best when it is read as a meeting point between formal benchmark theory, classic heuristics-and-biases work, and the later methodological caution supplied by [[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]].

In other words, the right question is usually not just “is this belief nonstandard?” but “relative to which benchmark, under which task conditions, and through which mechanism?”

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because policy and market design often depend on what people believe about themselves and the world. If consumers are overconfident, firms and regulators face one set of problems. If they project present tastes into the future, they face another. If fear suppresses attention to probability, risk communication becomes harder. If hindsight bias shapes institutional review, courts and regulators may overstate the foreseeability of bad outcomes after the fact.

The practical value of the page is therefore diagnostic. It helps clarify that “belief problems” are not one thing. Different kinds of nonstandard belief call for different responses: better information, better uncertainty communication, different elicitation methods, stronger feedback, or institutional designs that are robust to predictable belief error.

## Open questions

Which forms of nonstandard belief are most robust once people receive repeated feedback? How much of the evidence reflects stable distortions versus rational responses to ambiguity, salience, or poor task design? And is there a better umbrella label for this branch than `nonstandard beliefs`, or is the current title still the least bad organizing term for the vault?
