# Law of small numbers

## Core idea

[[Law of small numbers]] is the mistaken belief that small samples should look highly representative of the larger process from which they are drawn. In the current vault, the early conceptual anchor is now [[Kahneman and Tversky 1972]], [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] provides the canonical heuristics-and-biases background through [[Representativeness heuristic]], and [[DellaVigna 2009]] shows how the pattern travels into field settings.

## Key distinctions

This concept is closely related to representativeness but has a more specific structure. It generates both gambler's-fallacy reasoning and overinference from short streaks, depending on whether the underlying distribution is seen as known or uncertain. It differs from [[Overconfidence]], which is about beliefs about one's own ability or information, and from [[Projection bias]], which is about future tastes rather than statistical sequences.

## Evidence and debate

[[Kahneman and Tversky 1972]] matters because it gives the small-sample problem an early representativeness-based formulation: people expect samples, even small ones, to reflect the salient structure of the larger process. [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] then makes the pattern part of the canonical heuristics vocabulary. [[DellaVigna 2009]] uses the law of small numbers to organize two field patterns. The first is gambler's-fallacy behavior, such as betting less on lottery numbers that have won recently. The second is overinference or extrapolation, such as inferring too much from short runs of good performance by a mutual-fund manager or recent stock returns. The main debate is whether these patterns truly reflect one common psychological mechanism or instead a mix of misperceived dependence, return-chasing, and strategic responses to changing regimes.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because small-sample inference shows up constantly in markets, politics, and ordinary judgment. People evaluate fund managers, political candidates, and economic trends using short runs of data that may be far less informative than they appear. For PPE students, the concept is a strong example of how a cognitive mechanism from psychology can be imported into field economics.
