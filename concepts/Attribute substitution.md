# Attribute substitution

## Core idea

[[Attribute substitution]] is the process by which people answer a difficult judgment question by unconsciously substituting an easier one. In the current vault, the concept is anchored by [[Kahneman 2003]] and now strengthened directly by [[Kahneman and Frederick 2002]], which uses it to reinterpret the representativeness tradition.

## Key distinctions

The concept differs from a heuristic understood only as a rule of thumb. It is a process account of how intuitive judgment can be generated. It also differs from simple error: the point is not merely that the answer is wrong, but that the mind may have addressed a different question from the one posed.

## Evidence and debate

[[Kahneman 2003]] presents attribute substitution as part of the psychological foundation for behavioral economics. [[Kahneman and Frederick 2002]] matters because it ties the concept directly to [[Representativeness heuristic]], arguing that a hard question about probability is often answered by an easier question about resemblance or coherence. In the current vault, that makes attribute substitution more than a background architectural idea. It becomes a direct interpretive key for understanding classic representativeness findings, including [[Conjunction fallacy]].

The main debate is how far this process should be treated as a general architecture of intuition rather than as a helpful description of some particular cases.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because citizens, experts, and policymakers often face hard evaluative questions under uncertainty. If they substitute easier cues such as affect, salience, or familiarity, then institutional design and communication need to account for that process rather than assuming fully explicit calculation.
