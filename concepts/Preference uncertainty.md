# Preference uncertainty

## Core idea

[[Preference uncertainty]] is the idea that people may not have a settled view of how much they value an experience, even after some exposure to it. In the current vault, the anchor source remains [[Ariely et al. 2006]], but the broader conceptual background now includes [[Slovic 1995]] and [[Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006]], which argue that preferences more generally can be constructed in the act of elicitation rather than simply uncovered.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from [[Constructed preferences]], which is the broader claim that preferences can be formed during elicitation. Preference uncertainty is the more specific claim that people may be unsure of the value of an experience itself. It also differs from ordinary risk or uncertainty about the external world. The issue here is not uncertainty about outcomes, but uncertainty about one's own taste. In that sense, the concept sits between [[Coherent arbitrariness]] and welfare analysis: it concerns the instability of the object that policy and economics often treat as given.

## Evidence and debate

[[Ariely et al. 2006]] is the current anchor source. The paper shows that initial cues about whether an experience is something one pays for or must be paid to endure can shape later evaluations of the same experience. This suggests that some preferences over experiences are not merely latent and waiting to be measured. [[Slovic 1995]] and [[Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006]] matter because they place that result inside a larger program in which elicitation procedure can shape evaluation more generally. [[Lecouteux 2022 - Behavioral Welfare Economics and Consumer Sovereignty (in Heilmann and Reiss 2022, ch. 4)]] helps place the result inside [[Behavioral welfare economics]] more directly: if even experienced goods can leave welfare opaque, then policy can no longer rely on a simple version of [[Consumer sovereignty]]. [[Bernheim 2025]] adds an important policy-facing caution: even when preference uncertainty is real, it does not follow automatically that analysts can identify the correct welfare ranking on the chooser's behalf. [[Sugden 2018 - Chapter 04 - The Inner Rational Agent]] strengthens that caution by arguing that the leap from unsettled experience to recovered true preference is itself a contested normative move. Debate therefore concerns not only how far this generalizes beyond low-stakes or unfamiliar experiences, but also what kind of intervention, if any, such uncertainty could justify.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because many market and policy judgments assume that people know what they like. If preferences over experiences are uncertain, then willingness to pay, take-up, and stated choice become weaker guides to welfare than standard analysis assumes. That matters for consumer protection, cultural policy, education, health behaviors, and any domain where institutions help shape how people first encounter and interpret an experience. [[Bernheim 2025]] is useful here because it sharpens the next question: weaker welfare evidence does not by itself determine how strongly institutions should steer.

## Open questions

Which experiences generate the most preference uncertainty? When does repeated exposure help people discover stable tastes, and when does it simply lock in an arbitrary starting point? If preferences are uncertain, what should count as evidence that a policy improves welfare?
