# Preference reversals

## Core idea

[[Preference reversals]] are cases in which the ordering of options changes across elicitation procedures that are supposed to be normatively equivalent. In the current vault, the anchor sources are [[Slovic 1995]] and [[Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006]], which treat classic choice-versus-pricing reversals as one of the foundational empirical routes into [[Constructed preferences]], now sharpened by [[Hsee et al. 2007 - Preference Reversals Between Joint and Separate Evaluations of Options (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 9)]], which extends the branch to joint versus separate evaluation.

## Key distinctions

This concept differs from ordinary noise or inconsistency because the reversal is systematic rather than random. It also differs from [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]], though the two are related. Preference reversals concern changes in ordinal ranking across procedures, while WTA-WTP asymmetry concerns valuation gaps across buying and selling frames. It also differs from simple framing effects because the key driver here is often not wording alone but the evaluability of attributes across tasks. Both matter because they challenge [[Procedure invariance]].

## Evidence and debate

[[Slovic 1995]] presents preference reversals as a central challenge to the idea that elicitation simply uncovers a stable underlying ordering. [[Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006]] places those reversals inside a broader research program on context, response mode, and preference construction. [[Slovic and Lichtenstein 2006 - Relative Importance of Probabilities and Payoffs in Risk Taking (in Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006, ch. 2)]] matters because it gives the branch an early empirical foothold in the differential weighting of payoffs and probabilities across tasks. [[Grether and Plott 2006 - Economic Theory of Choice and the Preference Reversal Phenomenon (in Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006, ch. 5)]] matters because it brings the challenge directly into economic theory rather than leaving it as a psychological anomaly. [[Tversky et al. 2006 - Contingent Weighting in Judgment and Choice (in Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006, ch. 6)]] then adds a mechanism account: reversals can arise because different tasks induce different attribute weights. [[Hsee et al. 2007 - Preference Reversals Between Joint and Separate Evaluations of Options (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 9)]] adds a more specific later mechanism through the evaluability hypothesis. Some attributes are hard to interpret in isolation but easy to compare side by side, so joint and separate evaluation shifts which features receive weight. Debate therefore concerns not only whether reversals show deep instability in underlying preference, but also whether they reveal differential weighting of multi-attribute options as evaluability changes across tasks.

## Practical or policy relevance

This concept matters because law, regulation, and market analysis often depend on how preferences are elicited. If different procedures produce different orderings, then institutional choices about formats, defaults, and valuation methods can shape not only how preferences are expressed but which preferences appear to exist.

## Open questions

Which kinds of choice tasks are most prone to preference reversal? Do reversals reveal incoherence, or do they reveal that preferences are multi-attribute constructions whose expression depends on the task?
