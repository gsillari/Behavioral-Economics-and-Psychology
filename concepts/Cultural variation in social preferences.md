# Cultural variation in social preferences

## Core idea

[[Cultural variation in social preferences]] is the idea that fairness, reciprocity, sharing, punishment, trust, and cooperation are not expressed in the same way across all societies. The point is not that social preferences are unreal. It is that their content, strength, and experimental expression are shaped by local institutions, livelihoods, market exposure, anonymity, payoffs to cooperation, and everyday social norms.

[[Henrich et al. 2004]] is the anchor source. Its cross-cultural experiments show that the familiar university-student pattern in the [[Ultimatum game]] is not a universal baseline. Some small-scale societies make low offers with few rejections; others make near-equal offers; others show hyper-fair offers and unusual rejection patterns. Public-goods behavior also departs from narrow self-interest, but in patterned and variable ways.

## Why it matters

This concept changes how the wiki should interpret the fairness and ultimatum-game branch. Earlier sources such as [[Guth et al. 1982]] and [[Camerer and Thaler 1995]] show that narrow material self-interest is descriptively inadequate. [[Henrich et al. 2004]] adds that a single universal fairness model may also be inadequate. The behavioral benchmark is not simply homo economicus versus homo reciprocans. The better picture is heterogeneous social preferences embedded in local social worlds.

The concept also clarifies why ethnography matters. The same formal game can cue different analogies: sharing a windfall, bargaining with a stranger, distributing meat, risking reputation, avoiding shame, responding to hostility, or engaging in market-like exchange. Without ethnographic context, experimental-game behavior can be overinterpreted.

[[Skyrms 2014 - Chapter 02 - Fairness and Commitment]] fits this point into an evolutionary story. Skyrms treats ultimatum behavior as norm-sensitive and path-dependent: different cultures may stabilize different frames for what the experimental situation means. That makes cultural variation a constraint on simple evolutionary stories, not a rejection of evolutionary explanation.

[[Camerer 2003 - Chapter 02 - Dictator, Ultimatum, and Trust Games]] is useful here as a synthetic bridge. Camerer presents cross-national ultimatum-game results and the early small-scale-society evidence as showing that offers and rejections are not only preference measurements but culturally meaningful actions. The same game can look like bargaining, sharing, insult, gift exchange, or market exchange depending on local social interpretation.

## Open questions

How much cross-cultural variation reflects different preferences, different beliefs, different norms, or different interpretations of the experimental situation? Can behavioral economics model social preferences without flattening cultural diversity into one parameter? How should field experiments balance comparability with local meaning?
