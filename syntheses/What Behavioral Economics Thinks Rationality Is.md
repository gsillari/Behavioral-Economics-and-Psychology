# What Behavioral Economics Thinks Rationality Is

## Thesis

[[Behavioral Economics]] does not offer a single settled answer to the question of what rationality is.
In the current vault, it is better understood as a field organized around competing benchmarks.
[[Camerer and Loewenstein 2004]] is especially useful here because it presents behavioral economics as a coordinated research program rather than as a mere anti-rationality reaction.
That matters because the rationality debate in the field does not happen at only one level.
It unfolds across anomaly detection, descriptive theory, field evidence, and policy application.

Classical decision theory treats rationality as formal consistency in both preferences and beliefs, with [[de Finetti 1937]] and [[Subjective probability]] helping to clarify the belief side of that benchmark, and [[Ellsberg 1961]] showing that even this belief-side extension becomes unstable once agents confront ambiguity rather than measurable risk. [[Heilmann and Reiss 2022]] adds an important overview layer here by making the philosophy of these benchmark concepts more explicit.
[[Hands 2010]] adds an earlier historical complication on the preference side. The paper argues that economics did not move from psychology to no psychology in one clean step. Instead, the history of consumer choice theory runs through selective rejection of hedonistic introspection, the stabilization of [[Ordinal utility theory]], and then a more explicitly operational and [[Behaviorism|behaviorist]] turn in [[Revealed preference theory]]. [[Bruni and Sugden 2007]] widens that same point into a larger history of the discipline by arguing that the [[Paretian turn]] tried to redefine economics itself as a separate science of rational choice. [[Moscati 2022 - History of Utility Theory (in Heilmann and Reiss 2022, ch. 2)]] helps place that history inside the broader trajectory of [[Utility theory]], which matters because the benchmark later challenged by behavioral economics is already the product of multiple reinterpretations.
[[Simon 1955]] then introduces a different kind of break with the benchmark by arguing that rationality should be modeled for agents with limited information, limited search, and limited computational power rather than for omniscient optimizers.
[[Simon 1979]] later shows how broad that break really is by reconnecting bounded rationality to organizations, administrative procedure, and the scope of economics itself.
[[Petracca 2017]] adds a now-essential clarification for this synthesis. Simon's break was not only a benchmark revision. It was also grounded in [[Cognitivism]] as symbolic information processing. That means the field's rationality debate is not only about norms, heuristics, and welfare. It is also about what kind of mind behavioral economics is assuming when it calls itself psychologically realistic.
The [[Heuristics and Biases]] tradition treats rationality as conformity to probabilistic and statistical norms.
[[Prospect Theory]] keeps the idea of structured choice but relocates explanation in psychologically coded gains and losses.
[[Tversky and Kahneman 1981 (Framing)]] is especially useful here because it makes that shift unusually teachable: the same policy or outcome can be accepted or rejected depending on whether it is represented as saving lives or allowing deaths, which shows how descriptive rationality departs from extensional equivalence.
[[Kahneman 2003]] then helps integrate those strands by making explicit that behavioral economics is a psychological map of bounded rationality, not only a collection of isolated deviations from the benchmark.
[[Sloman 1996]] matters here because it gives the vault an early and influential way of locating the dispute at the level of cognitive architecture: associative and rule-based reasoning may coexist and yield competing responses to the same judgment problem.
[[DellaVigna 2009]] then asks how much of that behavioral departure really survives in the field.
[[Ecological rationality]] argues that good judgment should be evaluated by fit to environments rather than by one universal formal standard, but the current vault should now be more precise that this is not one single view.
The literature collected under [[Rationality wars]] then makes the disagreement explicit: much of behavioral economics is not only about whether people are rational, but about what standard is being used to ask the question. [[Mercier and Sperber 2011]] adds that the dispute is also about what reasoning is for, and the Stanovich-West branch adds that it is about how rationality should be measured rather than merely redescribed away.

## From formal benchmark to behavioral critique

The background benchmark in this vault is [[Expected utility theory]]. In that framework, rational choice under risk is usually understood in terms of coherent preferences over lotteries, evaluated through utility and constrained by axioms such as independence. [[Stefansson 2022 - The Economics and Philosophy of Risk (in Heilmann and Reiss 2022, ch. 3)]] usefully sharpens that benchmark by distinguishing [[Risk attitudes]] from ambiguity-sensitive behavior and by showing how much of the standard model's treatment of risk depends on utility curvature. But choice under uncertainty also presupposes some account of rational belief, and [[de Finetti 1937]] matters because it gives the vault a clearer benchmark on that side through [[Subjective probability]]: beliefs can be personal and still be rationally constrained by coherence. [[Ellsberg 1961]] then matters because it shows that even if the theory allows subjective probabilities, many people do not behave as if uncertain situations can always be reduced to one determinate personal probability distribution. Through [[Ambiguity aversion]], the paper introduces a major challenge to Savage-style decision theory: known risk and unknown probability are psychologically and behaviorally different. [[Aumann 1962]] then matters because it shows that even inside classical decision theory, the preference-side benchmark is less rigid than it first appears. By questioning the necessity of the [[Completeness axiom]], the paper suggests that rationality need not always require a full ranking of every pair of options. That is important because it means the benchmark later challenged by behavioral economics was already internally contestable.

[[Simon 1955]] matters because it changes the critique at a deeper level. The paper does not merely present a new anomaly. It argues that the ideal of global rationality should itself be replaced by a model of choice compatible with the information and computational capacities real agents actually possess. In later vocabulary, this becomes the foundational move behind [[Bounded rationality]]. That shift matters because it makes room for procedural realism before the better-known bias literature arrives. Behavioral economics therefore begins not only by finding violations of formal rationality, but also by asking whether the chooser in the formal model was ever psychologically plausible in the first place.

[[Simon 1979]] then matters because it turns that benchmark revision into a wider statement about the economic sciences. Rationality is not just a matter of what solitary agents should do in idealized choice problems. It is also a matter of how organizations structure search, information, authority, and stopping rules. This makes Simon especially important for the current vault because it ties bounded rationality to firms, institutions, and the real scope of economics, not only to abstract decision theory.

[[Petracca 2017]] then matters because it shows that bounded rationality has its own internal history of psychological interpretation. The paper distinguishes a wider `Simon 1` reading centered on the "scissors" of mind and environment from a narrower `Simon 2` reading centered on agents as limited information processors. It also argues that later economic uses of bounded rationality largely kept the symbolic-information-processing frame intact, even when they became more environment-sensitive. In that sense, some rationality disputes in behavioral economics are cognition-paradigm disputes, not just benchmark disputes.

[[Allais 1953]] then matters because it shows that plausible actual preferences can violate that benchmark. The importance of the [[Allais paradox]] is not only that people sometimes choose badly. It is that the standard theory may fail as a descriptive account of how people in fact choose.

[[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] pushes the challenge further.
Here rationality is no longer just a matter of coherent preference under risk, but also of sound judgment under uncertainty.
People do not reliably reason by statistical rules or probability theory.
They use heuristics such as [[Representativeness heuristic]], [[Availability heuristic]], and [[Anchoring]].
The key claim is not that cognition is random, but that it is patterned.
This gives behavioral economics one of its enduring themes: humans regularly depart from formal standards, and those departures are systematic enough to study.
It also fixes one of the main normative targets of the field, because the source treats probability theory and related statistical norms as the background against which bias becomes visible.

[[Evans 2008]] and [[Evans and Stanovich 2013]] make clear that this debate is also about cognitive architecture. They supply a broad dual-process framework in which intuitive and reflective reasoning are treated as importantly different kinds of processing, with the later Evans-Stanovich paper sharpening the vocabulary into type 1 and type 2 processing. [[De Neys 2012]] and [[De Neys 2023]] then complicate a simple reading of that story. They suggest that biased responders may still register conflict and show [[Logical intuitions]], and that [[Fast and slow thinking]] should not be modeled as a crude contrast between an illogical intuitive system and a fully corrective reflective system. This matters because it weakens the temptation to equate intuitive response with norm blindness. Rationality in behavioral economics is therefore not only a question of whether people get the right answer, but also of whether they detect that a tempting answer conflicts with a better one and of how cognition itself should be modeled.

## Prospect theory and the move from anomaly to model

[[Kahneman and Tversky 1979]] changes the shape of the discussion.
Instead of treating departures from rational choice as a list of anomalies, [[Prospect Theory]] models them.
Outcomes are evaluated relative to reference points, losses loom larger than gains, and framing affects decisions.
The theory also changes how risky prospects themselves are processed, because the psychological impact of probability is no longer treated as a simple mirror of objective probability.
This is a major shift.
Rationality is no longer treated simply as a benchmark against which humans fail.
Behavioral economics now also asks what kind of theory can describe the structure of those failures.

That move does not eliminate the classical benchmark. [[Expected utility theory]] remains the contrast class. But it does mean that behavioral economics starts to develop its own positive account of agency. In this part of the field, rationality is not discarded. It is displaced by a more psychologically realistic picture of how evaluation actually works.

[[Arkes and Blumer 1985]] adds a related but distinct anomaly through the [[Sunk cost effect]]. The paper shows that people often allow irrecoverable past expenditures to shape current choice, even though standard forward-looking rationality says those costs should be ignored. This matters because it broadens the challenge from risky-choice puzzles to persistence and project continuation. Rationality in behavioral economics is therefore not only about how people evaluate uncertain prospects, but also about whether they can detach present choice from psychologically salient past losses.

[[Slovic 1995]] and [[Lichtenstein and Slovic 2006]] add a different kind of challenge. They argue that preferences themselves may often be partly [[Constructed preferences|constructed]] in the moment of elicitation rather than merely distorted around a stable underlying baseline. In the current vault, [[Preference reversals]] and failures of [[Procedure invariance]] are the clearest route into that claim. This matters because it pushes the rationality question beyond error in judgment or deviation from axioms. It raises the possibility that the very object standard economics hopes to read off from choice, stable underlying preference, is sometimes less settled than the model assumes.

[[Ariely et al. 2003]] then gives that challenge a sharper valuation form through [[Coherent arbitrariness]]. The paper shows that willingness to pay can be anchored by irrelevant cues while still producing stable-looking demand relations. That matters because it suggests that behavioral economics is not only finding noise around preference. It is finding path-dependent structure in the formation of value itself.

[[Ariely et al. 2006]] deepens this problem by moving from ordinary goods to experiences. The paper suggests that people may face [[Preference uncertainty]]: they may not have a settled sense of whether an experience is good or bad even after sampling it. That matters because it weakens a common fallback response to constructed-preference arguments, namely that experience will reveal the true underlying taste. In the current vault, the rationality problem is therefore not only that judgments can be biased or that values can be arbitrarily anchored. It is also that the preference being inferred may remain underdetermined for some domains of choice.

[[DellaVigna 2009]] adds an important empirical step to this picture.
The paper surveys field evidence showing that [[Nonstandard preferences]], [[Nonstandard beliefs]], and [[Nonstandard decision-making]] appear across domains such as finance, voting, labor supply, charitable giving, and consumer behavior.
In the expanded vault, that means rationality disputes now connect directly to mechanisms such as [[Time inconsistency]], [[Overconfidence]], and [[Limited attention]], as well as to the institutional question of [[Strategic response to behavioral anomalies]].
That matters because it shifts the debate from "are there lab anomalies?" to "how extensive are these deviations in real institutions and markets?"
It also forces a further question: which mechanisms are field-robust enough to bear theoretical and policy weight, and which remain thinner or more context-bound?
[[Fischhoff and Broomell 2020]] adds a useful later self-description of the field. It presents judgment and decision making as a mature enterprise that links task analysis, descriptive research, and interventions across judgment, preference, and choice. That matters for this synthesis because it shows that rationality disputes do not stay inside one bias literature or one theory of risky choice. They shape how decision problems are analyzed, how performance is measured, and how institutions try to help people decide better.
[[Madrian 2014]] adds a closely related policy step by asking how those empirically important frictions should change the design of public interventions.
The question of rationality in behavioral economics is therefore not only theoretical and normative.
It is also a question about the external validity, practical significance, and policy relevance of behavioral patterns.

## The ecological challenge

[[Gigerenzer 2008]] argues that the heuristics tradition has often used the wrong standard. On this view, rationality is not primarily about approximation to a universal formal model. It is about the fit between simple decision rules and the environments in which they are used. [[Adaptive toolbox]] and [[Ecological rationality]] therefore shift the evaluative question. A heuristic may ignore information and still count as good reasoning if it is well matched to the task environment.

[[Dekker and Remic 2019]] adds an important clarification the vault should now treat as central. The ecological challenge actually comes in two forms. The first is Gigerenzer's individual-level ecology of heuristics, rooted in cue use and task environments. The second is Vernon Smith's institutional ecology of markets, norms, and emergent order. Both challenge the monopoly of one formal benchmark, but they do so through different psychologies, different methods, and different targets of explanation. That means "ecological rationality" should not be used loosely as if it named a single unified alternative to heuristics-and-biases.

This matters because it blocks a simple reading of behavioral economics as the science of human irrationality. Some apparent biases may instead reflect strategies that are adaptive under real-world constraints. The disagreement is not merely empirical. It concerns what rationality should mean in the first place.

## Rationality wars and benchmark pluralism

[[Hortal 2025]], [[Gigerenzer 2024]], [[Rich 2016]], and [[Samuels et al. 2002]] make the meta-level issue explicit. [[Hortal 2025]] argues that disputes about rationality are often disputes across disciplines, methods, and epistemic aims. A single framework should not be allowed to dominate everywhere. [[Gigerenzer 2024]] adds a direct retrospective from one of the central participants in the debate and highlights how much of the conflict turned on benchmark choice and rhetorical framing. [[Rich 2016]] adds a more programmatic way of handling that conflict. Her claim is that axiomatic and ecological rationality should be compared partly by the evaluative jobs they are meant to do. For the purpose of improving agents, each approach has a characteristic cost-benefit profile, and a hybrid that uses axiomatic criteria to evaluate candidate processes may sometimes be better than either pure framework. [[Samuels et al. 2002]] adds a deflationary point: some famous rationality conflicts look deeper than they are because different sides are evaluating different things by different standards.

[[Mercier and Sperber 2011]] adds a different kind of challenge. The paper suggests that reasoning should not first be understood as a neutral device for solitary truth-seeking, but as an argumentative faculty for producing and evaluating reasons in social settings. The commentaries in the same exchange make the challenge especially useful for the current vault. [[Wim De Neys]] argues that there is still evidence for nonargumentative conflict detection and norm sensitivity. [[Jonathan St. B. T. Evans]] argues that reasoning may also have been co-opted for private thought and prospective evaluation. Weber and Johnson suggest that internal query processes can look like argument turned inward. Mercier and Sperber's reply keeps the primary-function claim in place, but the exchange makes clear that the rationality debate is partly about function, not just standard.

The Stanovich-West branch adds a further constraint. [[Stanovich and West 2000]] argues that rationality disputes should be disciplined by individual-differences evidence rather than by unrestricted reinterpretation of errors as harmless or benchmark-relative. [[Stanovich and West 2007]] then shows that [[Myside bias]] is surprisingly independent of cognitive ability. That matters because it blocks a lazy equation between intelligence and rationality and gives the belief branch of behavioral economics a more politically relevant concept of perspective-bound reasoning.

Taken together, these sources suggest that behavioral economics should not be read as having replaced one definition of rationality with another. It has instead produced a more plural and self-conscious landscape. Rationality may mean formal coherence in one context, successful environmental fit in another, descriptive psychological regularity in another, a specific [[Cognitivism|cognitivist]] architecture in another, and something policy-relevant yet again in another.

[[Sunstein and Thaler 2003]], [[Camerer et al. 2003]], and [[Madrian 2014]] show how one side of this benchmark dispute gets translated into policy. Sunstein and Thaler argue that some steering is unavoidable because defaults, framing, and starting points always shape decisions, so the real question is whether that steering is designed well. Their answer is [[Libertarian paternalism]], a framework for welfare-promoting choice architecture that preserves formal freedom of choice. Camerer and coauthors offer a narrower and more demanding follow-up rule. [[Asymmetric paternalism]] aims to help those likely to err while imposing little or no cost on those who are already choosing well. Madrian broadens the toolkit again by asking which behavioral friction is actually at work and by highlighting concepts such as [[Internalities]] when current choices harm the chooser's future self. Together, these papers matter because they show that arguments about rationality in behavioral economics are not merely classificatory. They are used to justify specific institutional designs.

[[Lecouteux 2022 - Behavioral Welfare Economics and Consumer Sovereignty (in Heilmann and Reiss 2022, ch. 4)]] now helps state the normative transition more explicitly. Once the benchmark for rational choice becomes behaviorally unstable, policy cannot simply assume that observed choice still secures [[Consumer sovereignty]]. That is why [[Behavioral welfare economics]] becomes such an important middle layer between rationality theory and public policy. [[Bernheim 2025]] then adds an important restriction from inside that same policy branch. The paper accepts that benchmark failure can matter, but it argues that deviation from an ideal of rationality is not by itself enough to justify either a welfare claim or an intervention. Policy still has to show either a failure of characterization or a failure of optimization relative to the chooser's own evaluative standpoint. In that sense, Bernheim does not reject normative benchmarks. He rejects the too-fast inference from benchmark violation to analyst-certified mistake.

## Extension to AI

[[Camerer 2019]] shows that this problem extends beyond humans in more than one way. Machine learning can be used to search for new behavioral predictors, and AI systems can become part of the environment that shapes, predicts, or exploits human choice. [[Bini et al. 2026]] then shows that the problem also extends to artificial agents themselves. If LLMs can look more human-like in some preference tasks and more classically rational in some belief-based tasks, then rationality is not a one-dimensional score even for nonhuman systems. The behavioral-economics toolkit travels to AI, but it does not become simpler when it gets there. It becomes clearer that different tasks, standards, and purposes produce different judgments of rationality.

## Working synthesis

The best synthesis at this stage is that behavioral economics thinks rationality is a contested family of standards, architectures, and functions, not a single property. The field begins with formal benchmarks for both belief and preference, immediately finds pressure points in those benchmarks through sources such as [[Ellsberg 1961]] and [[Allais 1953]], gains force by showing systematic departures from them, develops descriptive alternatives such as [[Prospect Theory]], accumulates field evidence that those deviations matter outside the lab, encounters ecological critiques that redefine what success should mean, reopens the Simon branch through [[Petracca 2017]] by asking whether bounded rationality still rests on [[Cognitivism]] rather than on [[Situated cognition]] or [[Embodied cognition]], adds functional challenges such as the [[Argumentative theory of reasoning]], produces policy frameworks such as [[Libertarian paternalism]], policy tests such as [[Asymmetric paternalism]], and policy-economics concepts such as [[Internalities]] that operationalize one interventionist use of those findings, and finally becomes explicit that benchmark choice is itself a philosophical and policy question.

The mistake to avoid is moving too quickly from one level to another. A finding that violates a formal norm does not automatically show global irrationality. A successful heuristic does not eliminate the value of formal models. A descriptive theory does not by itself settle a normative question. And a policy intervention cannot pretend to be neutral if it silently chooses one standard of rationality over others, even when it presents itself as merely structuring choices more helpfully.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this synthesis matters because it shows that behavioral economics is not just a list of biases. It is also a debate about normativity, explanation, and institutional design. It matters for economics because what counts as an anomaly depends on the benchmark in use. It matters for philosophy because the field forces questions about reason, agency, normativity, and pluralism. It matters for politics because behavioral public policy depends on judgments about when citizens are making mistakes, when they are using workable heuristics, and when institutions should correct, scaffold, or simply respect those patterns.

## Open questions

Should the vault eventually distinguish more sharply between descriptive rationality, normative rationality, ecological rationality, and policy rationality? How should [[Behavioral public policy]] choose among competing standards without collapsing back into reductionism? And does the AI branch suggest that behavioral economics is becoming a general theory of bounded decision-making agents rather than only a theory of human choice?
