# Behavioral Public Policy and Its Competing Paradigms

## Thesis

[[Behavioral public policy]] is not one unified paradigm. In the current vault, it is better understood as a structured disagreement about four questions: what counts as a mistake, what counts as welfare, what kind of intervention is legitimate, and how confident policy makers should be in the benchmark they are using. The strongest paradigms currently represented are [[Libertarian paternalism]], [[Asymmetric paternalism]], and a more diagnostic, mechanism-matching approach associated especially with [[Madrian 2014]] and concepts such as [[Internalities]]. Alongside them stands a major internal critique, anchored by [[Sugden 2018]], which rejects planner-centered welfare correction and the appeal to an inner rational agent. Over the whole field sits a pluralist critique, anchored by [[Hortal 2025]], which argues that behavioral public policy cannot simply inherit one model of rationality and treat it as philosophically settled.

An important prehistory of this branch is [[Jolls et al. 1998]]. That paper does not yet present the later paradigms in their mature form, but it gives the field an early architecture through [[Behavioral law and economics]] and the three-part vocabulary of [[Bounded rationality]], bounded willpower, and [[Social preferences]]. In the current vault, that self-control branch is gathered mainly under [[Time inconsistency]]. It therefore helps explain why later policy debates are not only about nudges or paternalism; they are also about how realistic models of agency should reshape legal and institutional analysis more generally.

[[Ariely et al. 2003]] adds another pressure point that matters especially for PPE students. If preferences are partly [[Constructed preferences|constructed]] and can display [[Coherent arbitrariness]], then behavioral public policy cannot simply assume that observed willingness to pay or revealed choice transparently identifies welfare. [[Ariely et al. 2006]] sharpens the point by showing that even experienced activities may be subject to [[Preference uncertainty]]. [[Lecouteux 2022 - Behavioral Welfare Economics and Consumer Sovereignty (in Heilmann and Reiss 2022, ch. 4)]] helps make the same issue more explicit at the philosophy-of-economics level by framing it as a dispute over [[Consumer sovereignty]] and the ambitions of [[Behavioral welfare economics]]. This does not by itself settle the paternalism debate, but it does make the welfare benchmark more contested than many choice-preserving frameworks suggest.

[[Bernheim 2025]] adds an important internal challenge to this whole branch. The paper argues that behavioral public policy should call something a mistake only when it can identify a failure of characterization or optimization, not merely because policy analysts reject the chooser's evaluative standpoint. In the current vault, that matters because it turns the welfare debate into a guardrails debate: preference instability may weaken revealed preference without automatically authorizing paternalist correction.

[[Sugden 2018]] adds a stronger and more systematic challenge. Instead of only tightening the conditions under which mistakes may be attributed, Sugden argues that much behavioral policy relies on a fiction: an inner rational agent whose preferences can be treated as normatively superior to the chooser's actual behavior. The book then offers [[Opportunity criterion]] as a rival benchmark.

## First clarification

The first thing to clarify is that [[Libertarian paternalism]] and [[Asymmetric paternalism]] are not best understood as two perfectly parallel theories. [[Libertarian paternalism]] is the broader justificatory framework. It says that because choice architecture is unavoidable, welfare-promoting steering can be legitimate even when options remain open. [[Asymmetric paternalism]] is narrower. It asks whether a particular intervention helps those prone to error while imposing little or no burden on those already choosing well. In that sense, asymmetric paternalism is best read as a stricter design test inside the wider interventionist space opened up by libertarian paternalism.

That distinction matters because otherwise the debate looks flatter than it is. Some disagreements in behavioral public policy are about whether intervention is justified at all. Others are about how tightly it should be constrained once intervention is on the table.

## Paradigm 1: Libertarian paternalism

The central move in [[Sunstein and Thaler 2003]] is to deny that policy makers can avoid shaping choice. Defaults, menus, framing, timing, and presentation already influence outcomes, so the relevant question is not whether institutions steer, but whether they steer well. [[Thaler and Sunstein 2021]] turns that move into a full book-length paradigm statement. Its introduction and Chapter 5 give the clearest pedagogical account of why architecture is unavoidable and why design details matter, while [[Thaler and Sunstein 2021 - Chapter 15 - Much Ado About Nudging]] shows how the authors defend the framework once criticism becomes central. [[Libertarian paternalism]] therefore justifies intervention by combining two values: welfare improvement and preserved formal choice, and [[Nudge]] names the most visible family of tools that paradigm uses. [[Sunstein 2014]] then deepens this paradigm by arguing that active choosing can itself be costly and that some people rationally prefer delegation, default reliance, or simplified choice. [[Sunstein 2025]] extends the same paradigm into AI-powered choice engines, where the promise is more personalized welfare improvement and the risk is more opaque and more manipulable steering. [[Ludwig et al. 2025]] presses on the same fault line from a different angle: ordinary nudges may not respect heterogeneity well enough, so algorithmic thought partners may be needed to help people identify the right direction for themselves.

Its picture of rationality is relatively intervention-friendly. People are often boundedly rational, boundedly self-controlled, or unstable in their preferences, so institutions may legitimately structure decisions in ways that help them do better by their own lights. Its favored tools are low-coercion tools such as defaults, disclosures, framing choices, and other forms of choice architecture. Sunstein's later book makes the case broader still by adding the burden of decision itself as a reason not to romanticize active choice.

The underlying attraction of this paradigm is that it tries to avoid a stark choice between laissez-faire and hard paternalism. The main criticism, however, is that its notion of liberty may be too thin. If institutions strongly steer behavior through defaults and presentation effects, formal opt-out may not be enough to settle the legitimacy question. A second criticism is that the paradigm still needs a view of welfare, and that view may be much less neutral than its rhetoric suggests. The AI extension makes both problems sharper: personalization may improve fit, but it may also make invisible steering more powerful. For that reason, [[Ludwig et al. 2025]] is best read not simply as another application of libertarian paternalism, but as a proposal for a partially new framework gathered in the vault under [[Behavioral economics 2.0]].

## Paradigm 2: Asymmetric paternalism

[[Camerer et al. 2003]] accepts much of the behavioral background but imposes a more demanding policy filter. The key question is not simply whether people err, but whether a policy can help those likely to err while imposing minimal cost on those who are informed, attentive, and already choosing well. [[Asymmetric paternalism]] therefore adds a value that is less central in libertarian paternalism: institutional modesty.

Its picture of rationality is more heterogeneous. The population is not treated as uniformly biased. Some people need help, others do not, and good regulation should respect that variation. Its preferred interventions are therefore those that are low-burden, targeted, and reversible.

The attraction of this paradigm is political and philosophical as much as economic. It offers a way to make behavioral intervention more defensible to skeptics by insisting on a demanding burden test. But its weakness is that the test itself is not neutral. Someone still has to decide who is mistaken, what counts as a burden, and how to measure welfare losses and gains. Asymmetric paternalism is narrower than libertarian paternalism, but it does not escape the benchmark problem. It disciplines intervention; it does not eliminate the need for normative judgment.

This is exactly where [[Bernheim 2025]] becomes useful. It makes explicit that the key unresolved issue is not just burden minimization, but the prior question of what sort of evidence entitles policy makers to say that a chooser has made a mistake at all.

## Paradigm 3: Mechanism-matching policy design

[[Madrian 2014]] shifts the discussion again. Instead of beginning with a large justificatory label, the paper begins with diagnosis. What is the friction? Is the main problem [[Time inconsistency]], [[Limited attention]], [[Reference dependence]], poor information, present bias, or some other mechanism? The policy question then becomes: which intervention best matches that friction?

This is a more technocratic and less slogan-driven paradigm. It is less interested in defending one grand philosophy of steering and more interested in mapping specific behavioral mechanisms to specific tools. In this framework, behavioral policy is not exhausted by defaults or nudges. It includes reminders, planning prompts, commitment devices, psychologically informed incentives, and the use of concepts such as [[Internalities]] to reinterpret market failure when current choices harm the chooser's future self.

The underlying value here is practical effectiveness joined to diagnostic precision. The attraction is obvious: it avoids treating all behavioral problems as the same problem. But the weakness is also clear. A friction-matching approach can sound more neutral than it really is. The moment it moves from diagnosis to welfare analysis, it still has to decide which outcomes count as better, when future selves should be privileged over present selves, and how far policy should go in correcting self-harming behavior.

## Standing critique: Contractarian opportunity-based criticism

[[Sugden 2018]] is best read in the current vault as a standing internal critique rather than as one more interventionist paradigm. The critique has two layers. The negative layer, concentrated in [[Sugden 2018 - Chapter 04 - The Inner Rational Agent]], argues that behavioral policy too often jumps from context-sensitive or unstable choice to a theorized better self whose preferences policy can promote. The positive layer, concentrated in [[Sugden 2018 - Chapter 05 - Opportunity]], argues that institutions should instead be judged by the opportunities they create for mutually beneficial interaction.

This matters because it changes the shape of the dispute. The question is no longer only how much steering is legitimate or how tightly it should be constrained. It is also whether the whole planner-centered welfare framework is the wrong way to respond to behavioral evidence. In that respect, Sugden complements [[Bernheim 2025]] while going further than it. Bernheim imposes guardrails on mistake attribution. Sugden offers a rival normative benchmark.

The attraction of this critique is that it avoids the fiction of purified or latent preferences while still taking behavioral evidence seriously. Its weakness is that opportunity can itself be difficult to operationalize in real regulatory settings, especially where background inequalities or manipulation affect what opportunities are meaningfully available.

## The empirical backdrop

[[DellaVigna 2009]] matters because it gives these paradigms a stronger empirical base. The policy debate is more credible if the underlying mechanisms actually appear in the field rather than only in laboratory tasks. The paper shows that [[Nonstandard preferences]], [[Nonstandard beliefs]], and [[Nonstandard decision-making]] arise across domains such as labor supply, consumer choice, finance, politics, and charitable giving. That gives behavioral public policy more than a conceptual rationale; it gives it a cross-domain evidence base.

But DellaVigna also complicates the picture. The paper highlights [[Strategic response to behavioral anomalies]], meaning that firms, employers, and political actors may exploit predictable mistakes rather than simply correct them. This pushes behavioral public policy away from a narrow "help the chooser" picture and toward a more institutional one. Policy may need to regulate not only biased decisions, but also the environments and strategic actors that amplify them.

## Meta-critique: Is the field too nudge-centered?

[[Loewenstein and Chater 2017]] raises the first important meta-level warning in the current vault. The paper argues that nudges have become too dominant as the public image of behaviorally informed policy, even though the real policy space is much wider and includes conventional economic tools and hybrid interventions.

[[Hallsworth 2023]] responds more constructively than polemically. Its manifesto suggests that the field should move beyond the image of behavioral science as a specialist tool and instead build it into organizations, policy processes, and systems thinking. This is not a rejection of nudging so much as an attempt to put it in proportion.

[[Chater and Loewenstein 2023]] is the strongest and most critical version of this meta-critique. The paper argues that behavioral public policy has often overused the `i-frame`, focusing on individuals and their decision errors, and neglected the `s-frame`, where rules, incentives, institutions, and market structures reside. In the current vault, that criticism is best gathered under [[I-frame and S-frame]]. It does not simply reject behavioral policy, but it does challenge the historical centrality of [[Nudge]] and allied approaches within the field's self-understanding.

## The pluralist challenge

[[Hortal 2025]] introduces the most important philosophical pressure on all three paradigms and on the Sugden critique too. The problem is not only whether interventions work. It is whether behavioral public policy has silently adopted one model of rationality and treated it as authoritative. If the heuristics-and-biases framework, [[Ecological rationality]], and other standards of good judgment are not equivalent, then policy cannot simply move from "observed deviation" to "mistake worth correcting" without argument. [[Bernheim 2025]] complements this critique from inside the policy branch itself by arguing that even once one accepts behavioral findings, the category of mistake still needs narrow and defensible guardrails.

This pluralist challenge does not automatically defeat libertarian paternalism, asymmetric paternalism, or mechanism-matching policy design. But it does change how they should be read. They are not purely technical frameworks. They are frameworks that already embed views about welfare, rationality, error, and legitimacy. The most philosophically self-aware version of behavioral public policy would therefore combine practical intervention design with benchmark humility.

| [[Libertarian paternalism]] | How can institutions steer while preserving choice? | People are often bounded, so steering is legitimate if options remain open. | Welfare plus formal freedom | Strong general justification for choice architecture | Liberty may be reduced to opt-out formalism. |
| [[Asymmetric paternalism]] | Can we help those who err without burdening those who do not? | People differ in competence and vulnerability to error. | Modesty and burden minimization | Offers a disciplined regulatory test | Hidden welfare and burden judgments remain. |
| Mechanism-matching policy design | Which friction is actually blocking good outcomes, and which tool fits it? | Rationality failures are plural and mechanism-specific. | Diagnostic precision and effectiveness | Better fit between problem and intervention | Can hide normative assumptions under technocratic language. |
| Pluralist critique | Which benchmark of rationality is policy using, and why that one? | Rationality is benchmark-dependent and discipline-specific. | Epistemic humility and anti-monism | Exposes hidden assumptions in policy reasoning | Gives less immediate operational guidance. |

## Working conclusion

The best synthesis at this stage is that behavioral public policy is not divided between only two options. It contains at least three operational paradigms, one major internal critique, one forward-looking extension, and one standing philosophical challenge. [[Libertarian paternalism]] supplies the broad case for welfare-promoting choice architecture. [[Asymmetric paternalism]] narrows that case by demanding low burdens on competent choosers. [[Madrian 2014]] adds a more diagnostic framework in which the legitimacy and effectiveness of intervention depend on the specific behavioral friction at issue. [[Sugden 2018]] rejects the move from behavioral instability to latent-preference correction and offers [[Opportunity criterion]] as a rival benchmark. [[Ludwig et al. 2025]] then suggests a more revisionist, AI-mediated extension in which personalized algorithmic thought partners may partially replace one-size-fits-all nudges. [[Hortal 2025]] reminds us that all of these moves depend on contestable benchmarks of rationality.

So the deepest disagreement is not only about policy tools. It is about political morality. How much weight should be placed on formal freedom, substantive welfare, revealed preference, future-self protection, or institutional humility? [[Bernheim 2025]] adds that one more value belongs on this list: restraint in the attribution of mistake. Behavioral public policy matters precisely because it forces those values into the open.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this synthesis matters because it shows that behavioral public policy is not just applied psychology plus a few nudges. It is a live dispute about freedom, welfare, legitimacy, and institutional design. It matters for economics because each paradigm treats welfare failure differently. It matters for philosophy because the whole field turns on contested ideas of rationality and paternalism. It matters for politics because policy makers cannot use behavioral findings without taking a stand, explicitly or implicitly, on what citizens owe to themselves and what institutions may legitimately do in response.

## Open questions

Should the vault eventually split mechanism-matching policy design into its own reusable concept page, or is it better kept as a synthesis-level framing? Can [[Asymmetric paternalism]] be operationalized clearly enough to guide real regulation, or does it remain mostly a rhetorical constraint? Does [[Libertarian paternalism]] become more defensible when paired with stronger transparency requirements? And can a genuinely pluralist version of [[Behavioral public policy]] be action-guiding without collapsing into ordinary technocracy?
