# Robyn M. Dawes

## Who or what it is

[[Robyn M. Dawes]] was an American psychologist and decision researcher whose work matters in this wiki mainly through two branches: formalized judgment and cooperation. In the judgment branch, [[Dawes and Corrigan 1974]], [[Dawes 1979]], and [[Dawes et al. 1989]] develop the case that simple formal procedures can outperform unaided expert judgment when people must combine multiple imperfect cues. In the cooperation branch, [[Dawes and Thaler 1988]] helps frame public-goods contribution as an anomaly for narrow self-interest.

## Why it matters here

Dawes matters because he gives the vault one of its clearest bridges between psychology, methodology, and institutional design. His linear-model work does not merely report that experts make mistakes. It explains why consistency in cue combination can be valuable, why exact weights may matter less than choosing good variables, and why the superiority of formal procedures should be compared with realistic human judgment rather than with an ideal model.

For PPE students, this makes Dawes important for debates about expertise, discretion, prediction, and public decision-making. His work helps connect [[Bounded rationality]] to institutional questions: if people use information inconsistently, then institutions may need procedures that stabilize judgment without pretending that those procedures are normatively complete.

## Key relationships

The nearest predecessor is [[Paul E. Meehl]], whose [[Meehl 1954]] made the clinical-statistical prediction problem canonical. Dawes's contribution is to sharpen the procedural and structural lesson: linear models can work well because many prediction tasks reward consistent monotone cue combination, even when the weights are not optimized.

The later AI and algorithmic-governance branch inherits this comparison but adds new questions about fairness, proxy targets, opacity, and contestability. Dawes is therefore a background figure for [[Clinical versus actuarial judgment]], [[Behavioral law and economics]], and [[Algorithmic Judgment and Debiasing]], even though his central examples predate contemporary machine learning.
