# Robert Sugden

## Who or what it is

[[Robert Sugden]] is a British economist at the University of East Anglia whose work spans decision theory, experimental economics, welfare economics, political philosophy, and the theory of social order. He is best known in the current vault in four roles.
First, with [[Loomes and Sugden 1982]], he is a co-founder of [[Regret theory]] and an important critic of standard expected-utility models.
Second, [[Sugden 1989]] anchors the vault's [[Spontaneous order]] branch by connecting [[Convention]], [[Evolutionary game theory]], Humean justice, and Hayekian institutional emergence.
Third, through [[Sugden 2018]], he becomes one of the clearest internal critics of planner-centered [[Behavioral public policy]]. That later work argues against the idea that unstable choices reveal a hidden rational self whose preferences policy makers may legitimately recover and promote. Instead, Sugden defends an opportunity-based and contractarian view of markets, institutions, and regulation.
Fourth, through [[Bruni and Sugden 2007]], he appears in the vault as a historian and methodologist of economics, arguing that the separation of economics from psychology during the [[Paretian turn]] was a contingent disciplinary choice rather than an obvious scientific necessity.

## Why it matters here

Sugden matters here because he gives the vault several distinct but connected alternatives to standard economic reasoning. In decision theory, he helps represent an alternative to standard expected utility in which regret and rejoicing enter directly into choice under uncertainty. In the spontaneous-order branch, he explains how conventions and property-like expectations can evolve without design and acquire moral force without being guaranteed efficient or just. In the policy branch, he gives the strongest current liberal-contractarian critique of behavioral welfare economics, centered on the rejection of the inner rational agent and the defense of [[Opportunity criterion]]. In the methodology branch, he helps recover a historical line in which economics might have remained more tightly connected to psychology.
