# Richard H. Thaler

## Who or what it is

[[Richard H. Thaler]] (born 1945) is an American economist who helped build behavioral economics from inside economics rather than from its psychological border.
He taught at the University of Rochester and Cornell before joining the University of Chicago Booth School of Business, where his work became central to the field's public identity.
Thaler's early papers on consumer choice, mental accounting, self-control, and the [[Endowment effect]] showed how economic behavior departs from the standard picture of stable, rational optimization.
He helped make anomalies cumulative by connecting scattered findings into a more durable alternative research program.
His work on [[Mental accounting]] and intertemporal choice became especially important for explaining everyday consumer behavior.
With [[Cass R. Sunstein]], he also brought behavioral insights directly into law and public policy through the idea of libertarian paternalism and the book *Nudge*, now represented in the current vault by [[Thaler and Sunstein 2021]].
He received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2017 for his contributions to behavioral economics.
Thaler matters historically because he helped turn behavioral ideas into a recognized branch of mainstream economic analysis.
He is one of the main figures through whom psychological findings became institutional economics, welfare debates, and real policy design.
His career marks the moment when behavioral economics stopped looking like a set of anomalies and started looking like a durable field.

## Why it matters here

The early standalone Thaler spine in the vault now begins with [[Thaler 1980]] and [[Thaler 1985]]. The 1980 paper brings prospect-theory reasoning into consumer choice, develops early economics-facing treatments of the [[Endowment effect]] and the [[Sunk cost effect]], and treats self-control and precommitment as central to real decision-making rather than as side anomalies. [[Thaler 1985]] then deepens the branch by introducing [[Mental accounting]] as a more general framework for how consumers code and evaluate outcomes. Together, these sources show Thaler not only as a collector of anomalies but as a builder of a durable behavioral architecture inside economics.

The later Thaler footprint in the vault is already substantial even before more dedicated Thaler ingests arrive. [[Kahneman et al. 1990]] anchors the endowment-effect branch, [[Benartzi and Thaler 1995]] carries prospect-theory reasoning into finance through [[Myopic loss aversion]], [[Jolls et al. 1998]] helps build the behavioral-law-and-economics branch, [[Sunstein and Thaler 2003]] shapes the modern policy branch through [[Libertarian paternalism]], [[Thaler and Sunstein 2021]] now gives that policy branch its main book-length public statement, and [[Nudge]] now gives the vault a cleaner name for the specific tool family associated with that branch. [[Nesterak 2018]] is a useful later retrospective because it shows how Thaler himself described that legacy a decade on: influential, narrower than behavioral economics as a whole, and increasingly tied to worries about sludge and to a broader policy toolkit that includes AI and design thinking.

[[Dawes and Thaler 1988]] and [[Camerer and Thaler 1995]] now add the early *Journal of Economic Perspectives* anomalies-column branch. The former treats [[Cooperation]] and public-goods contribution as an anomaly for rational selfishness, while the latter treats ultimatum and dictator games as anomalies for narrow self-interest in bargaining. Together they show Thaler's role in turning scattered experimental findings into a cumulative behavioral-economics research program.

[[Rabin and Thaler 2001]] adds a particularly important benchmark-facing Thaler source. It argues that economists should not explain ordinary small-stakes risk aversion by concavity of utility over lifetime wealth, because that explanation fails [[Calibration|calibration]] across stakes. The paper also shows the constructive side of Thaler's program: [[Loss aversion]] and [[Mental accounting]] are not merely colorful anomalies, but candidates for replacing a misleading expected-utility explanation.

## Biographical note

Thaler was awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2017 for contributions to behavioral economics. In the current vault, that recognition matters less as biography than as a marker that what began as descriptive pressure on consumer theory became a durable branch of economics.
