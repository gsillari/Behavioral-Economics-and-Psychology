# Herbert A. Simon

## Who or what it is

[[Herbert A. Simon]] (1916-2001) was an American social scientist whose work crossed public administration, economics, psychology, computer science, and philosophy of science.
He spent the defining part of his career at Carnegie Institute of Technology, later Carnegie Mellon University, where he became one of the institution's signature intellectual figures.
In economics, he is best known for challenging the image of agents as perfect maximizers with unlimited information and computational power.
That challenge gave the field the language of [[Bounded rationality]] and opened the door to satisficing, search, and procedural accounts of choice.
He also transformed organization theory by treating firms and bureaucracies as decision systems operating under constraint.
In psychology and cognitive science, he helped turn problem solving, attention, and search into objects of formal study.
With Allen Newell, he was a founding architect of symbolic artificial intelligence and early models of human reasoning.
Simon received the Turing Award in 1975 and the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1978.
The Nobel citation honored his work on decision making in economic organizations, but his influence reached far beyond organizational economics.
For behavioral economics, Simon remains crucial because he shifted the question from whether people satisfy formal axioms to how finite agents cope with complexity.
Much later work on judgment, heuristics, organizations, and the structure of rationality still unfolds in the space Simon created.
[[Petracca 2017]] makes one more part of that legacy explicit: Simon was not only the founder of bounded rationality, but also a founding figure of [[Cognitivism]], and later clashes over [[Situated cognition]] and [[Embodied cognition]] therefore reach directly into the foundations of his project.
[[Simon 1963]] adds a more explicit methodological layer to that legacy by attacking Friedman's `as-if` rationality directly and by defending a more empirically disciplined notion of approximation.

## Why it matters here

Simon matters here because he changes the background standard against which many later concepts are evaluated. The later vault contains papers on [[Heuristics and Biases]], [[Prospect Theory]], [[Field Evidence in Behavioral Economics]], [[Behavioral law and economics]], and [[Behavioral public policy]], but Simon's work helps explain why the field became willing to model real decision procedures rather than only idealized outcomes. He therefore belongs in the spine of [[Foundations and Rationality]] rather than in a side branch. The 1956 paper also makes him an important precursor to [[Ecological rationality]], even if the later ecological program is not simply identical with Simon's project. [[Simon 1963]] matters because it shows that Simon's challenge was also methodological: he explicitly rejects Friedman's `principle of unreality` and insists that idealization must be defended through continuity of approximation rather than through indifference to false assumptions. The 1979 lecture then ties those earlier moves to organizations, administrative systems, and the scope of economics itself. [[Petracca 2017]] adds that Simon also matters here as a cognition theorist: the vault now needs to read bounded rationality against the backdrop of [[Cognitivism]] and Simon's later resistance to [[Situated cognition]].

## Key relationships

The most important direct link is [[Simon 1955]] to [[Bounded rationality]], with [[Simon 1956]] now serving as a bridge from bounded rationality toward [[Ecological rationality]]. [[Simon 1963]] adds a direct methodological clash with Friedman-style `as-if` reasoning, while [[Simon 1979]] gives the mature organizational and disciplinary statement of the same project. [[Petracca 2017]] adds a different bridge, this time into cognitive methodology: it ties Simon to [[Cognitivism]] and to the later challenge from [[Situated cognition]] and [[Embodied cognition]]. From there, Simon connects to [[Competing Views of Rationality]], because his work offers one of the earliest major alternatives to the classical benchmark. He also connects to [[Behavioral law and economics]], where later writers translate bounded rationality into institutional and legal design, and to the broader field-level syntheses [[Behavioral Economics]] and [[What Behavioral Economics Thinks Rationality Is]].

## Biographical note

Simon was awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1978 for pioneering research on decision-making in economic organizations. That recognition matters in this vault because it underlines how wide his influence became across economics, political science, psychology, management, and later artificial intelligence.
