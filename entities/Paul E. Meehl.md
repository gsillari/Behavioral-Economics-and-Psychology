# Paul E. Meehl

## Who or what it is

[[Paul E. Meehl]] (1920-2003) was an American psychologist and methodologist best known in the current vault for launching the clinical-versus-statistical prediction debate. His central importance here comes from [[Meehl 1954]], which argued that explicit and consistent predictive procedures often outperform informal expert synthesis when they are given comparable information.

## Why it matters here

Meehl matters here because he gives the vault a deep historical anchor for a branch that otherwise risks looking too contemporary and AI-driven. Long before machine learning, fairness metrics, and predictive governance, Meehl had already posed the core comparative question: when should institutions trust informal expertise and when should they trust formalized procedures? That makes him important not only for [[Clinical versus actuarial judgment]], but also for later lines running through [[Behavioral economics of AI]] and [[Behavioral law and economics]].

## Key relationships

The tightest connection is to [[Clinical versus actuarial judgment]], where Meehl should now be treated as the founding source. [[Dawes and Corrigan 1974]], [[Dawes 1979]], and [[Dawes et al. 1989]] are the main later Dawes-line sources that explain, sharpen, and generalize the Meehl lesson. The modern AI branch matters because it inherits the same comparative structure under new technological conditions.
