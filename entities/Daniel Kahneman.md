# Daniel Kahneman

## Who or what it is

[[Daniel Kahneman]] (1934-2024) was an Israeli-American psychologist whose research changed economics, psychology, and public policy.
He trained in psychology at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem and the University of California, Berkeley, and later taught at the Hebrew University, the University of British Columbia, and Princeton University.
Kahneman was not trained as an economist, which is part of why his intervention into economics was so consequential.
With [[Amos Tversky]], he documented systematic departures from formal models of judgment and choice under uncertainty.
Their early work on [[Representativeness heuristic]], [[Availability heuristic]], and [[Anchoring]] became the core of the [[Heuristics and Biases]] program.
They later developed [[Prospect Theory]], the field's most influential descriptive account of risky choice.
Kahneman also helped frame later debates about framing, loss aversion, reference points, and the relation between psychology and welfare.
He received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2002 for integrating psychological research into economic science.
Beyond academic audiences, he became globally known through *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, which gave a wide readership a map of judgment and decision research.
His work helped make behavioral economics legible as a field rather than a disconnected set of anomalies.
Across the main branches of contemporary behavioral research, he remains a central figure linking judgment, choice, and the broader question of what rationality looks like for human beings.

## Why it matters here

Kahneman matters here because the vault now starts with a deeper judgment-under-uncertainty sequence before reaching risky choice. [[Kahneman 2003]] raises the level again by showing how intuitive judgment, [[Bounded rationality]], [[Attribute substitution]], and descriptive choice theory belong to the same larger project. [[Kahneman 2003 (Collaborative research)]] adds a more personal but still highly useful layer: it shows how the collaboration with Tversky emerged from argument, mutual trust, and a shared dissatisfaction with then-dominant models of judgment. [[Kahneman et al. 1990]] adds another major role: Kahneman as a central figure in the valuation-and-entitlement branch of behavioral economics, where [[Endowment effect]] and [[WTA-WTP asymmetry]] connect psychology to bargaining, law, and welfare measurement. His work is therefore tied not only to [[Heuristics and Biases]], [[Representativeness heuristic]], [[Base-rate neglect]], [[Conjunction fallacy]], [[Availability heuristic]], [[Anchoring]], [[Prospect Theory]], [[Loss aversion]], [[Reference dependence]], and [[Framing effects]], but also to the field-level architecture and human history of [[Behavioral Economics]] itself.

## Key relationships

The most important collaboration in this first cluster is with [[Amos Tversky]]. Together they produced [[Kahneman and Tversky 1972]], [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]], [[Tversky and Kahneman 1981]], [[Tversky and Kahneman 1981 (Framing)]], and [[Kahneman and Tversky 1979]], which now function as the anchor sources for the representativeness branch, the wider heuristics program, the framing branch, and the later prospect-theory framework. [[Kahneman and Frederick 2002]] and [[Kahneman 2003]] then bridge those specific contributions to a broader field-defining synthesis. [[Kahneman 2003 (Collaborative research)]] adds something the more formal papers do not: an account of how the partnership itself operated as a mode of joint inquiry. Kahneman is therefore both an entity page and a routing point between the early representativeness lineage, the heuristics program, the later prospect-theory framework, and the more general psychological self-understanding of behavioral economics.

## Anecdotal and biographical notes

[[Kahneman 2003 (Collaborative research)]] is especially useful because it preserves the human texture of the field's early development. The source describes the 1969 Jerusalem seminar discussion that helped launch the Kahneman-Tversky partnership, Kahneman's dissatisfaction with the "conservative Bayesian" interpretation of judgment, and the sense that their collaboration became a form of shared thinking rather than simple coauthorship. Those details are not merely colorful. They help explain why the early papers form such a coherent research sequence.
