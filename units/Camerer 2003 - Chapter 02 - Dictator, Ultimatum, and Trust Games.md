# Camerer 2003 - Chapter 02 - Dictator, Ultimatum, and Trust Games

## Summary

This chapter is Camerer's central treatment of [[Social preferences]]. Its main claim is not merely that people are generous or that standard predictions fail. The sharper claim is that simple games can be used as measurement devices for separating [[Altruism]], fear of rejection, negative reciprocity, positive reciprocity, [[Inequity aversion]], [[Trust]], and trustworthiness.

The chapter begins from the [[Ultimatum game]]. If responders care only about their own money, they should accept any positive offer; if proposers expect this, they should offer the smallest possible amount. The robust experimental pattern is different: proposers commonly offer substantial shares, and responders often reject low offers. Camerer stresses that this is a clean challenge to narrow self-interest because rejecting an offer is strategically simple. The more general lesson is a joint-hypothesis lesson: experiments do not show that [[Game theory]] is dead. They show that game-theoretic analysis paired with money-payoff-only utility is too thin, and that strategic reasoning itself is limited in some settings.

The [[Dictator game]] then removes the responder's veto. This lets Camerer separate strategic fear from positive concern for others. Dictator giving is lower than ultimatum giving, so much ultimatum generosity is strategic fear of rejection. But dictator giving does not disappear, so some positive social concern remains. This is why the dictator game is not a pure measure of altruism, but it is a useful decomposition tool.

The [[Trust game]] adds vulnerability. The first mover can invest resources that increase the second mover's opportunity set; the second mover can then reciprocate or exploit that trust. Camerer uses the game to distinguish trust from trustworthiness and to connect laboratory games to moral hazard, incomplete contracts, organizations, labor relations, and social capital.

## Fairness is not just a label

Camerer's most important interpretive point is that fairness is not merely a label pasted onto behavior after the fact. A label would only rename the observation: people reject low offers because they are "fairness minded." Camerer's argument is stronger. Across many experiments, fairness judgments move in patterned ways when the situation changes. People react differently when a low offer is chosen intentionally rather than produced by chance, when the proposer earned the right to propose, when the responder knows or does not know the size of the pie, when there were fairer unchosen alternatives, when rejection can or cannot hurt the proposer, and when the setting is framed as bargaining rather than market exchange.

This makes fairness explanatory in a disciplined sense. Fairness is a judgment about actions, consequences, entitlement, intentions, and available alternatives, and that judgment changes preferences over actions and allocations. The chapter therefore supports the wiki's treatment of [[Fairness]] as a structured behavioral concept, not as a vague synonym for niceness.

## Inequity aversion and fairness equilibrium

Camerer presents the main theory families without treating any one as the final answer.

[[Altruism]] says that another person's welfare enters utility positively, or that the giver receives satisfaction from the act of giving. This explains some dictator giving and some trust repayment, but it has difficulty explaining costly punishment of unfairness unless the model changes signs or adds further motives.

[[Inequity aversion]] explains fairness behavior by assuming that people dislike unequal outcomes, especially when they are worse off than others and, more weakly, when they are better off. Its strength is tractability: one can adjust the payoffs in a game and then use familiar game-theoretic tools. Its limitation is that it is outcome-based. It struggles when the same payoff distribution is treated differently because of intention, entitlement, chance, or unchosen alternatives.

Fairness-equilibrium models, especially [[Rabin 1993]], explain behavior through reciprocity and perceived kindness. The same allocation can feel kind, neutral, or unkind depending on what the other player could have done and what the player believes the other intended. In this approach, players reward kind treatment and punish unkind treatment, and equilibrium requires beliefs about kindness and response to be mutually consistent. Camerer favors this family psychologically because it captures intention and path dependence, even though inequity-aversion models are easier to use analytically.

## Cultural and structural variation

The chapter also shows why experimental games cannot be read as one universal fairness parameter. Methodological variables such as stakes, repetition, anonymity, and experimenter blindness matter, but they do not eliminate the core patterns. Demographic effects are weaker than often expected; Camerer treats academic major as more reliable than gender, race, or beauty effects. Age matters because children often look more self-interested, suggesting that fairness norms are learned rather than simple mistakes erased by experience.

Culture matters more deeply. Camerer discusses early cross-national ultimatum-game evidence and the wider small-scale-society project associated with Henrich and collaborators. The important lesson is that offers and rejections are culturally meaningful actions. Market integration and local cooperative practices shape how people interpret division, sharing, generosity, and insult.

Structural variables are especially important. Entitlement can lower offers when a proposer earns the proposer role. Limited information makes responders more tolerant of low offers because they cannot tell how unfair the offer is. Competition among proposers or responders can drive outcomes toward extremes. Unchosen alternatives matter because a low offer made when a fair offer was available is judged differently from the same low offer made under constraint.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it transforms fairness and trust from moral vocabulary into experimentally tractable parts of strategic interaction. It matters for economics because it shows how to revise game-theoretic models by enriching utility and beliefs rather than abandoning formal analysis. It matters for philosophy because it makes intentions, entitlement, and treatment central to rational response. It matters for politics and policy because markets, bargaining, labor relations, legal rules, and institutions often depend on whether people can express or suppress social preferences.
