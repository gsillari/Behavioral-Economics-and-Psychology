# Kahneman and Tversky 1982 - On the Study of Statistical Intuitions (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 34)

## Summary

This chapter is a methodological postscript on how to study reasoning errors without caricaturing human judgment. Kahneman and Tversky defend the use of errors, biases, and fallacies as evidence about statistical intuition, but they also insist that not every apparent violation of logic or probability should count as a genuine judgmental error. Misunderstanding, communication failure, and overly charitable reinterpretation are all live problems.

In the current vault, the chapter matters because it gives the heuristics-and-biases branch a more reflective self-understanding. It is one of the clearest internal statements of how the program justifies its method and how it tries to avoid both overclaiming and apologetics.

## Key claims

The first claim is that reasoning errors are a legitimate window into cognitive process, much as perceptual illusions are in the study of perception.

The second claim is that apparent norm violations require interpretive discipline. Researchers should avoid both overly strict readings that convert reasonable answers into errors and overly charitable readings that explain away every contradiction.

The third claim is that the accumulation of statistical and logical errors creates a serious challenge to purely rational models of judgment.

## Evidence and methods

The chapter is reflective rather than experimental. Its importance lies in defending a research strategy: compare intuitive judgments with accepted statistical or logical standards, but remain attentive to communication and interpretation.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it turns a psychological research program into a methodological and philosophical issue. It matters for economics because many behavioral findings depend on whether benchmark violations are interpreted as genuine anomalies. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about normativity, interpretation, and the evidential status of error. It matters for politics because public uses of behavioral findings often depend on how confidently those findings are taken to reveal irrationality.
