# Skyrms 2014 - Chapter 03 - Mutual Aid

## Place in the work

Chapter 3 is the book's central treatment of cooperation and altruism. It explains why correlated interaction is the key to understanding how mutual aid can evolve in strategic settings such as the Prisoner's Dilemma.

## Summary

Skyrms begins with Kropotkin's reply to Hobbesian and social-Darwinist pictures of nature. Mutual struggle is real, but so is mutual aid. The explanatory puzzle is how cooperative or altruistic behavior can be favored by evolutionary dynamics driven by differential reproduction.

The chapter then makes an unusual bridge to decision theory. Savage expected utility evaluates acts using unconditional probabilities of states. Jeffrey expected utility uses probabilities conditional on acts, which creates well-known worries about spurious correlation in rational decision theory. Skyrms's move is to show that the Jeffrey structure is not voodoo in evolutionary game theory when the conditional probabilities represent real correlation in encounters.

Under random pairing, defection is the evolutionarily stable strategy in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. Under correlated pairing, however, cooperators may be more likely to meet cooperators, and the expected fitness calculation changes. Correlation can arise through kinship, partner choice, reciprocal altruism, signaling, local interaction, or group structure. The second edition adds a compact route to Hamilton's rule by treating relatedness as correlation among interactants.

The chapter's strongest conceptual claim is that correlation can make cooperation evolutionarily viable even when it violates principles of individual rational choice in the uncorrelated game. With sufficient self-correlation, strictly efficient strategies can go to fixation, including strategies that would otherwise be dominated.

## Key claims

The equivalence between economic game theory and evolutionary game theory holds only under independence or random pairing. Once correlation enters, the two can part ways.

Correlation mechanisms are central to the evolution of cooperation. Kinship, partner choice, repeated interaction, signaling, local clustering, and group structure are different ways of making like interact with like.

Hamilton's rule can be understood as a special case of the broader correlation framework.

Perfect self-correlation yields a Darwinian analogue of the categorical imperative: act so that if others act likewise, fitness is maximized. In realistic settings, imperfect correlation weakens this into a tendency rather than a guarantee.

## Evidence or method

The chapter's method is formal and conceptual. It uses the Prisoner's Dilemma, replicator dynamics, Maynard Smith's evolutionary stability, Jeffrey decision theory, Hamilton's rule, kin selection, reciprocal altruism, local interaction models, and haystack-style group selection.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter gives a powerful bridge between political theory's problem of the common good, economics' social-dilemma models, and evolutionary explanations of norms. It is especially useful because it does not simply say "people cooperate." It identifies the structural conditions under which cooperation becomes dynamically stable.
