# Kahneman and Tversky 1982 - Intuitive Prediction (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 30)

## Summary

This chapter brings the heuristics program directly into forecasting. Kahneman and Tversky argue that intuitive predictions rely too heavily on singular case information and too lightly on distributional or base-rate information. As a result, predictions are often too extreme, too confident, and insufficiently sensitive to regression toward the mean.

In the current vault, the chapter matters because it gives the prediction branch a classic anchor. It helps connect representativeness and base-rate neglect to applied forecasting rather than leaving them as abstract judgment errors.

## Key claims

The first claim is that prediction problems involve two kinds of information: singular information about the specific case and distributional information about the relevant class of cases.

The second claim is that intuitive prediction overweights the singular side. Forecasters give vivid case details too much influence and neglect the stabilizing role of base rates.

The third claim is that corrective procedures are possible. Better prediction often begins by anchoring on the base rate and then adjusting for whatever genuinely diagnostic singular information remains.

## Evidence and methods

The chapter is conceptual and review-oriented. Its distinctive contribution is to organize forecasting error in terms of the contrast between singular and distributional information and then to derive correction strategies from that distinction.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it turns statistical reasoning into a concrete practical problem. It matters for economics because forecasting is central to markets, policy, and planning. It matters for philosophy because it clarifies what it means for a prediction to be rational under uncertainty. It matters for politics because public forecasting and institutional planning are often distorted by attention to vivid case details at the expense of class-level evidence.
