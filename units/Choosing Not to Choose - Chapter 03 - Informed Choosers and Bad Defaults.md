# Choosing Not to Choose - Chapter 03 - Informed Choosers and Bad Defaults

## Summary

This chapter asks how institutions should choose among default rules. Sunstein's preferred answer is the informed-chooser approach: select the default that most people would choose if they were adequately informed. The chapter matters because it turns the book from a descriptive account of default effects into a normative framework for evaluating good and bad defaults.

## Key claims

The first claim is that some defaults are beneficial and some are harmful, so policy needs a criterion for selecting among them. The second is that the informed-chooser approach can attract support from people with different normative commitments because it does not require settling the deepest disputes about welfare, justice, or autonomy in fully abstract terms. The third is that markets will not always generate good defaults on their own, especially when products are complex and consumers are inattentive.

## Evidence and methods

This unit is normative and framework-oriented, but grounded in behavioral findings about default stickiness and consumer inattention. Its main contribution is a practical criterion for default selection rather than a new behavioral mechanism.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it is one of the clearest places where behavioral evidence is translated into a policy standard. It matters for economics because it confronts market optimism about default design. It matters for philosophy because it tries to secure an incompletely theorized agreement on what makes a default legitimate. It matters for politics because it offers a realistic criterion for administrative and regulatory design.

## Open questions

How stable is the informed-chooser approach when people are divided or poorly informed in different ways? Does the approach preserve enough autonomy, or does it smuggle in a substantive welfare standard under a modest label?
