# Kahneman and Tversky 1982 - The Simulation Heuristic (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 14)

## Summary

This chapter extends the availability branch from memory retrieval to mental construction. Kahneman and Tversky argue that some judgments do not depend mainly on recalling stored instances, but on how easily one can mentally generate a plausible scenario or path to an outcome. That gives the vault a useful bridge between classic availability and later work on framing, plausibility, and process accounts of intuitive reasoning.

In the current vault, the chapter matters because it broadens what counts as "bringing things to mind." It suggests that intuitive probability judgment is shaped not only by remembered examples but also by how readily the mind can simulate possible worlds, alternative paths, or near-miss variations.

## Key claims

The first claim is that recall and construction are distinct psychological operations. Both can feed availability-like judgments, but they answer different sorts of questions and operate under different constraints.

The second claim is that mental simulation yields a sense of the ease with which a model of the situation can produce different outcomes. People can then mistake this ease of generation for evidence of likelihood, normality, or explanatory adequacy.

The third claim is that this mechanism has broader implications than a simple memory bias. It helps explain why some outcomes feel especially plausible, why some alternatives feel painfully close, and why some judgments about prediction or regret are so sensitive to the way a situation is mentally represented.

## Evidence and methods

The chapter is conceptual and example-driven rather than built around a single large empirical sequence. Its value for the vault lies in the process distinction it introduces: availability is not only about what is stored and recalled, but also about what can be mentally generated with relative ease.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it shows how a seemingly technical cognitive distinction can reshape debates about rationality and judgment. It matters for economics because forecasting, regret, and counterfactual comparison often depend on imagined alternatives rather than remembered frequencies. It matters for philosophy because it pushes beyond static belief to the structure of possible-world reasoning. It matters for politics because public interpretation of events often depends on the ease with which actors can simulate blame, preventability, or alternative trajectories.
