# Lewis 1969 - Chapter 01 - Coordination and Convention

## Summary

Chapter 1 builds the bridge from [[Coordination games]] to [[Convention]]. Lewis begins from recurrent coordination problems: situations in which agents have a shared interest in matching one another but more than one matching pattern could work. A convention emerges when one regularity becomes the expected and self-sustaining way of solving such problems.

The chapter is an immediate successor to [[Schelling 1960 - Chapter 04 - Toward a Theory of Interdependent Decision]]. Schelling had shown that focal points, salience, labels, and mutual expectations can select one equilibrium among many. Lewis turns that insight into a more general philosophical account: conventions are stable regularities of action sustained by expectations and by the fact that each person's reason to conform depends on others' expected conformity.

## Key claims

The first claim is that coordination problems are not solved by common interest alone. Agents may all prefer successful coordination to failed coordination, yet still need some way to converge on the same equilibrium.

The second claim is that conventions arise in recurrent coordination problems. Recurrence matters because precedent, expectation, and learned regularity can stabilize one solution among several possible alternatives.

The third claim is that a convention is not merely a repeated behavior. It must be a regularity that agents expect one another to follow and that each has reason to follow if others do.

The fourth claim is that alternatives matter. A conventional regularity could have been otherwise: another regularity could also have solved the coordination problem if it had become established.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it gives a clean game-theoretic path from individual action to social order. It explains how institutions can be sustained without explicit agreement or coercion: not because agents blindly conform, but because mutual expectations make conformity rational.

This is important for economics because many market and institutional patterns depend on stable expectations. It matters for philosophy because it clarifies the difference between habit, agreement, rule, and convention. It matters for politics because many social institutions work through shared expectations before they are codified as law.
