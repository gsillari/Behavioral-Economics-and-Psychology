# Choosing Not to Choose - Chapter 06 - Personalization

## Summary

This chapter extends the default-rule argument into the era of large data sets and individualized design. Sunstein argues that personalized defaults may capture some of the virtues of both active choosing and ordinary defaults: they reduce decision burdens while also fitting outcomes more closely to individual circumstances.

## Key claims

The first claim is that personalized defaults can be more accurate than one-size-fits-all defaults. The second is that personalization may therefore offer a kind of best-of-both-worlds design. The third is that this promise comes with serious concerns about privacy, authenticity, and the narrowing of horizons. The fourth is that technological change is making these issues central to future policy design.

## Evidence and methods

This unit is forward-looking and synthetic. It relies on technological examples and general design logic rather than a single experimental result.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it connects behavioral public policy to data governance, privacy, and algorithmic design. It matters for politics especially because the personalized-default model raises legitimacy questions that go beyond classic nudging.

## Open questions

When do personalized defaults improve autonomy by reducing burdens, and when do they threaten autonomy by overfitting the self? How should institutions balance accuracy against privacy and authenticity concerns?
