# Camerer 2003 - Chapter 07 - Coordination

## Summary

This chapter is Camerer's main treatment of [[Coordination games]] and [[Equilibrium selection]]. Its central lesson is that multiple-equilibrium games cannot be understood by equilibrium existence alone. Once several outcomes are self-enforcing if expected, the hard question becomes which one people actually expect, recognize, and reach.

Camerer treats coordination as a natural home for [[Behavioral game theory]]. Formal game theory identifies possible equilibria; experiments reveal the psychological, historical, communicative, and institutional cues that select among them. The chapter therefore extends the Schelling-Lewis branch of the wiki. [[Schelling 1960]] gives [[Focal points]] their foundational role in tacit coordination. [[Lewis 1969]] turns focal-point reasoning into a theory of [[Convention]]. Camerer adds the experimental inventory: focality, equality, communication, assignment, timing, payoff dominance, risk dominance, loss avoidance, simplicity, level-k reasoning, learning, and path dependence.

The chapter is also important because it does not treat coordination failure as a moral failure or a cooperation failure. People can share an interest in coordinating and still fail. They can also coordinate on an inferior equilibrium because the safer or more salient option becomes expected.

## Matching games and focality

The cleanest evidence comes from matching games, where players receive the same payoff if their choices match and no payoff otherwise. In such games the payoff structure often leaves many equivalent equilibria. The question is what makes one option stand out.

Camerer reviews evidence from Mehta, Starmer, and Sugden showing that players coordinate above chance in apparently simple matching tasks. The important distinction is between private prominence and coordination prominence. An answer can be attractive to one person without being useful for coordination. A focal point works when each person can plausibly think that the other person will also see it as a natural point of convergence.

Visual assignment games sharpen this. Players use principles such as equality, accession, and closeness, but not mechanically. Bacharach and Bernasconi's work shows that focality depends on features such as distinctiveness, rarity, and recognizability. A cue must be noticeable enough to break symmetry but also mutually intelligible enough to be safely used.

This is why [[Focal points]] should not be assimilated to arbitrary salience. In coordination problems, salience can be a rationally relevant part of the situation because it helps create mutually consistent expectations.

## Asymmetry, communication, assignment, and meaning

The chapter then moves from pure matching to asymmetric coordination games such as battle-of-the-sexes cases. These games combine a shared interest in coordinating with conflict about which coordinated outcome should be selected.

Communication has different effects depending on the strategic structure. In battle-of-the-sexes games, one-way communication can help by selecting the speaker's preferred equilibrium, while two-way communication may revive conflict because both players can press for their preferred outcome. In stag-hunt-like games, by contrast, two-way communication can help more because the central problem is assurance rather than distributional conflict.

External assignment also works conditionally. It can select an equilibrium in pure matching games when no rival principle competes with it. But assignment becomes weaker when it conflicts with equality, payoff dominance, or another salient standard. This is one of the chapter's useful empirical lessons: a selection device is not a magic wand. It works only when players can treat it as the selection device.

The sender-receiver material adds a bridge to language. In common-interest games, players can develop homemade signals that reliably separate meanings. When interests diverge, pooling and babbling become more likely. This connects coordination games to [[Convention]], [[Common knowledge]], and the later analysis of language and institutions.

## Market entry and overconfidence

Market-entry games turn coordination into a capacity problem. If too many players enter, payoffs fall; if too few enter, profitable opportunities are missed. Experiments often show noisy early behavior: over-entry when capacity is low, under-entry when capacity is high, and gradual movement toward more stable patterns with experience.

The skill-entry evidence adds a behavioral-economics link. When payoffs depend on relative skill, subjects can predict aggregate entry reasonably well and still enter too often. Camerer and Lovallo interpret this as overconfidence or reference-group neglect: people know many others will enter, but they still overweight their own chance of being among the successful entrants.

This matters because equilibrium selection is not always only about focal cues. Sometimes the selected outcome depends on how players represent the reference group, their own skill, and the distribution of competitors.

## Stag hunt, weak-link, and order-statistic games

The chapter's strongest continuity with [[Van Huyck et al. 1990]] comes through stag-hunt, weak-link, and order-statistic games. These games make the conflict between efficient and secure equilibria vivid.

In stag-hunt games, payoff dominance points toward the high-payoff coordinated outcome, while risk dominance or security points toward the safer outcome. Communication helps when it supplies assurance that others will take the risky efficient action too.

In weak-link games, each player's payoff depends on the minimum effort in the group. Large groups often deteriorate toward inefficient low effort because one low choice pulls everyone down. Fixed pairs do better than large or randomly rematched groups, which shows how group size and matching structure shape strategic uncertainty.

Median-action and broader order-statistic games show path dependence. Early play can anchor later convergence; a first-period median can become a durable precedent. Preplay auctions and entry fees can raise choices, but Camerer notes that this need not prove sophisticated forward induction. Mandatory fees also work, suggesting that loss avoidance and [[Mental accounting]] can become equilibrium-selection principles.

## Selection principles and empirical matches

Camerer's chapter is especially useful because it pairs selection principles with experimental domains instead of presenting them as abstract names.

- [[Focal points]] and common ground fit matching games, visual assignment tasks, and Schelling-style tacit coordination.
- Payoff dominance works when the efficient equilibrium is sufficiently safe or salient, but it often loses force when risk or security concerns are strong.
- Risk dominance and security fit stag-hunt and weak-link environments where players fear being exposed by others' low choices.
- Communication works when it selects a unique equilibrium and creates enough assurance; it is less reliable when communication intensifies distributional conflict.
- External assignment works when it does not collide with stronger principles such as equality or payoff dominance.
- Precedence and path dependence fit median-action games and continental-divide experiments, where small early differences can have lasting consequences.
- Simplicity matters because formally equivalent games can be played differently when one representation is easier to reason through.
- Loss avoidance and mental accounting fit preplay auction and fee experiments, where paying a cost can make low coordination feel like a loss to be avoided.
- Level-1 or diffuse-prior reasoning performs well in Haruvy and Stahl's comparison of selection principles, often outperforming payoff dominance, risk dominance, and maximin.

The right lesson is not that one selection principle wins everywhere. Camerer's conclusion is closer to a disciplined collage: different coordination environments activate different principles, and experiments are needed to learn which principle has behavioral force in which setting.

## Applications: path dependence, market adoption, and culture

The chapter's applications show why coordination belongs in PPE rather than only in microeconomic theory.

Continental-divide experiments show how small initial differences can push groups toward different long-run equilibria with different payoff consequences. Market-adoption experiments show how historical incumbency can slow movement toward a cheaper or more efficient trading venue. Organizational-culture experiments show how groups develop local codes and then overestimate how transparent those codes will be to outsiders after a merger.

These applications connect coordination to institutions. Social institutions can support cooperation, but they can also solve equilibrium-selection problems by making one pattern salient, credible, or publicly expected. Conversely, they can lock people into an inferior pattern when history and expectations settle badly.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it gives an empirical theory of how social order can arise or fail even when incentives are partly aligned. It matters for economics because it shows that equilibrium prediction often requires behavioral and experimental content. It matters for philosophy because it links rationality to salience, common ground, convention, and mutual expectation. It matters for politics because institutions, laws, organizations, markets, and public conventions often work by selecting among multiple possible equilibria, not merely by changing material incentives.
