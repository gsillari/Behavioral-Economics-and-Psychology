# Schelling 1960 - Chapter 04 - Toward a Theory of Interdependent Decision

## Summary

Chapter 4 is Schelling's attempt to enlarge game theory beyond the zero-sum case. Its central claim is that many strategically important situations are not pure conflicts. They mix conflict with mutual dependence, and therefore require the players to coordinate expectations, interpret signals, and find mutually recognizable ways to act together.

The chapter is foundational for [[Coordination games]] because it treats pure coordination as the opposite limiting case from pure conflict. In pure coordination, players have aligned interests but can still fail because they cannot identify the same action, place, time, line, label, or rule. In mixed-motive games, the same coordination problem remains, but it is layered over bargaining, competition, threats, and partial conflict. [[Lewis 1969]] takes this opening and makes it the basis for a theory of [[Convention]].

## Key claims

The first claim is that zero-sum games are a limiting case rather than the natural starting point for all strategy. Pure conflict abstracts away from suggestion, inference, threat, promise, and mutual accommodation. That makes it badly suited to wars, strikes, negotiations, traffic jams, tacit bargains, and many political or institutional settings.

The second claim is that pure coordination is strategically nontrivial. Even where players share the same interest, they must still find a way to make their expectations converge. The chapter's examples include avoiding collision, dancing to unfamiliar music, applauding at a concert, meeting without communication, and drawing matching divisions on a map.

The third claim is that labels and symbolic details matter. The abstract normal-form matrix can hide the very features that make coordination possible. A place, time, boundary, label, convention, or salient feature can become the clue that lets players select one equilibrium rather than another. This is why Schelling's analysis is not merely descriptive psychology added to game theory. He argues that rational players should attend to these details when they help both sides coordinate.

The fourth claim is that coordination is central to mixed-motive interaction. In bargaining, the parties may disagree over distribution, but they also share an interest in reaching one of the outcomes better than no agreement. Coordination of expectations is therefore part of bargaining itself, including explicit bargaining, tacit bargaining, diplomatic maneuvering, and cases where speech is unavailable or strategically constrained.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter is a bridge between formal game theory and social-political life. It explains why strategic interaction often depends on common salience, convention, communication, and mutual perception, not only on preferences and payoffs. That matters for political coordination, protest, deterrence, institutional design, bargaining, segregation, traffic norms, and collective action.

The chapter also matters for behavioral economics because it complicates the idea that deviations from a simple equilibrium prediction must come only from irrationality or social preferences. Sometimes the model has stripped away the labels and signals that rational agents would use. In that sense, Schelling belongs near [[Game theory]] and [[Cooperation]], but he adds a distinct coordination-centered route into social behavior.
