# Tversky and Kahneman 1982 - Evidential Impact of Base Rates (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 10)

## Summary

This chapter is the clearest direct blue-book treatment of base rates as evidence. Tversky and Kahneman ask how people combine general statistical information with case-specific description and show that, in many intuitive predictions, the descriptive case dominates while base-rate data receive little weight.

In the current vault, the chapter matters because it gives [[Base-rate neglect]] a more explicit source anchor than the broader heuristics summaries alone. It helps clarify that the issue is not merely a generic respect for statistics, but a specific evidential failure in combining background frequencies with individuating information.

## Key claims

The first claim is that posterior judgments about particular cases are often driven by representativeness rather than by disciplined integration of base-rate information.

The second claim is that neglect can mean either outright ignoring base rates or grossly underweighting them. The problem is therefore not binary. Intuitive judgment can formally acknowledge relevant statistics while still allowing case fit to dominate.

The third claim is that this pattern helps explain why predictions about individual people, outcomes, or cases can look psychologically compelling while remaining statistically poor. The chapter therefore sharpens the link between [[Representativeness heuristic]] and [[Base-rate neglect]].

## Evidence and methods

The chapter synthesizes experimental work on prediction from descriptions and category frequencies. Its emphasis is evidential and comparative: how much weight do intuitive judges actually assign to base-rate data once rich case information is present?

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it is one of the cleanest demonstrations of a conflict between intuitive plausibility and rational evidence use. It matters for economics because the ability to combine case-specific information with background rates is central to forecasting, diagnosis, and market judgment. It matters for philosophy because it clarifies what kind of error is at issue: not merely false belief, but defective evidential integration. It matters for politics because public judgment often privileges vivid cases over population-level information in exactly this way.
