# Skyrms 2014 - Chapter 02 - Fairness and Commitment

## Place in the work

Chapter 2 moves from symmetric bargaining to the [[Ultimatum game]], where one player appears to have bargaining power and the other can only accept or reject. It is the book's main chapter on fairness, commitment, costly rejection, and the relation between evolution and sequential rationality.

## Summary

The standard backward-induction story says that the proposer should demand almost everything and the responder should accept any positive amount. Skyrms places this story inside a broader discussion of commitment and sequential rationality, using examples such as doomsday deterrence and *Gianni Schicchi* to illustrate why threats that would not be rational to carry out are normally treated as defective.

Ultimatum-game experiments complicate that picture. Proposers often make much fairer offers than the narrow benchmark predicts, and responders often reject low offers at a cost to themselves. Skyrms emphasizes that this is not an anomaly for rational choice unless utility is assumed to equal money. But appeal to fairness norms is not yet an explanation; it raises the further question of how such norms could evolve.

The chapter's evolutionary analysis shows that replicator dynamics need not eliminate non-sequentially-rational strategies such as costly punishment. In simplified ultimatum games, populations can evolve toward polymorphisms containing Fairman-like strategies that demand equal division and reject unfair offers. Recombination and mutation complicate the story but do not guarantee convergence to sequential rationality.

The second edition adds the cross-cultural evidence from Henrich and collaborators. Ultimatum behavior varies substantially across small-scale societies, including low rejection rates, hyper-fair offers, and rejection of overly generous offers. Skyrms interprets this as evidence that culture and framing matter: experimental behavior may depend on which evolved or learned social norm the situation cues.

## Key claims

Sequential rationality is not automatically preserved by evolutionary dynamics.

Costly rejection in ultimatum bargaining can survive under replicator dynamics, even though it is weakly dominated by accepting all positive offers.

Fairness norms should not be treated as explanatory endpoints. They themselves need evolutionary and cultural explanation.

Cross-cultural ultimatum-game variation shows that fairness behavior is path-dependent and norm-sensitive, not simply the expression of one universal fairness parameter.

## Evidence or method

The chapter combines game-theoretic analysis, evolutionary simulations, and experimental evidence. Its empirical anchors are the original ultimatum-game experiments by Guth, Schmittberger, and Schwarze; cross-national ultimatum experiments by Roth and collaborators; and the later anthropological experiments associated with [[Henrich et al. 2004]].

## Concepts touched

The main concepts are [[Ultimatum game]], [[Fairness]], sequential rationality, subgame perfection, weak dominance, replicator dynamics, recombination, mutation, social norms, and [[Cultural variation in social preferences]].

## Relation to other units

Chapter 2 extends the fairness problem from [[Skyrms 2014 - Chapter 01 - Sex and Justice]] to asymmetric bargaining. Its discussion of cultural norms points forward to [[Skyrms 2014 - Chapter 04 - Correlated Convention]], where convention and role-asymmetry become central.

## Why it matters for PPE

This chapter is especially useful for PPE because it shows why fairness in bargaining should be read simultaneously as a behavioral anomaly, a formal game-theoretic problem, a cultural phenomenon, and an institutional norm. It also keeps the critique of rational-choice predictions careful: the issue is not that rational choice is incoherent, but that money-payoff game theory is too thin and evolutionary dynamics may not select the same strategies as sequential rationality.
