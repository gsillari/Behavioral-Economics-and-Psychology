# Loewenstein 2007 - The Fall and Rise of Psychological Explanations in the Economics of Intertemporal Choice (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 3)

## Summary

This chapter is the book's most important historical and methodological intervention. Loewenstein argues that early economists often used explicitly psychological accounts of intertemporal choice, that those accounts were later displaced by more formal and behaviorally thin treatments of utility, and that the modern behavioral literature represents not a strange intrusion into economics but a partial recovery of something economics once contained.

In the current vault, the chapter matters because it gives the time-choice branch a much richer intellectual background. It helps explain why [[Time inconsistency]] is not just a list of anomalies around discounted utility, but part of a larger return of psychology to economics.

## Key claims

The first claim is historical: classical and nineteenth-century economists often explained intertemporal choice through motivation, anticipation, weakness, and psychologically textured views of utility.

The second claim is that twentieth-century economics progressively stripped those psychological elements away. Utility became more formal, preference more behaviorally opaque, and intertemporal analysis more detached from lived motivational conflict.

The third claim is that modern behavioral work on self-control, present bias, and dynamic inconsistency should therefore be read as a rediscovery as well as a critique. It is not just replacing a failed model; it is re-opening a dimension of inquiry that economics had narrowed.

## Evidence and methods

This unit is a historical and conceptual essay rather than an experimental report. Its method is interpretive: it traces how economists have understood utility, discounting, and temporally distributed choice, and it uses that intellectual history to reframe the significance of later behavioral findings.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it turns an empirical literature into an argument about disciplinary self-understanding. It matters for economics because it reframes intertemporal anomalies as evidence about what economics chose to exclude. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about utility, motivation, and the explanatory role of psychology. It matters for politics because many policy debates about savings, addiction, and self-control depend on whether people are modeled as stable planners or psychologically conflicted agents.

## Open questions

How much of behavioral economics is genuinely new, and how much is the recovery of older psychologically richer traditions in economics? Should the current vault treat intertemporal choice mainly as a technical anomaly literature or as a privileged site for the return of psychology to economics?
