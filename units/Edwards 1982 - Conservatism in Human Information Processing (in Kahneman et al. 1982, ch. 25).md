# Edwards 1982 - Conservatism in Human Information Processing (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 25)

## Summary

This chapter gives the vault a classic benchmark-oriented statement of underreaction in belief revision. Edwards compares human updating with Bayesian standards and argues that people typically move their beliefs in the right direction when they receive new evidence, but by too little. The result is conservatism in the technical sense: beliefs change, yet not enough relative to the evidence.

In the current vault, the chapter matters because it gives the belief branch a clearer link to Bayesian rationality and to the long tradition of comparing human judgment with formal updating rules. It strengthens the benchmark side of the heuristics literature without requiring a separate concept branch of its own.

## Key claims

The first claim is that people are often conservative information processors in the sense that they revise probabilities insufficiently in light of new evidence.

The second claim is that this pattern is robust across task formats. People do not ignore evidence entirely, but they tend to stop short of the Bayesian posterior.

The third claim is that the most useful comparison is not with common-sense error in the abstract, but with the ideal update specified by Bayes's theorem.

## Evidence and methods

The chapter draws on experimental belief-revision tasks, including the classic bookbag-and-poker-chip style problems and more complex radar-style inference tasks. Its main value in the vault is methodological: it provides a clean formal contrast between intuitive belief updating and normative probabilistic revision.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it connects behavioral findings directly to the philosophy of rational belief. It matters for economics because expectations, diagnosis, and forecasting depend on how evidence is incorporated over time. It matters for philosophy because it shows one concrete way in which probabilistic rationality becomes operationalized. It matters for politics because policy analysts, judges, and publics alike often update too slowly or too timidly in the face of new evidence.

## Open questions

How should the vault distinguish conservatism in updating from ambiguity sensitivity or simple distrust of evidence? When is underreaction a bias, and when might it reflect sensible caution about noisy signals? And how central should Bayesian updating remain as a benchmark in a PPE-oriented treatment of rational belief?
