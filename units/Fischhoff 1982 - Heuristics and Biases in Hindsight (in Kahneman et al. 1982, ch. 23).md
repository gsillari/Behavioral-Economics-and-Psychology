# Fischhoff 1982 - Heuristics and Biases in Hindsight (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 23)

## Summary

This chapter gives the vault a foundational source on hindsight. Fischhoff argues that once people know an outcome, they systematically exaggerate how foreseeable it was and underestimate the uncertainty that preceded it. In the current vault, this matters because it adds a durable belief distortion that is neither just representativeness nor just overconfidence, but a retrospective restructuring of the past.

Its importance is methodological as well as conceptual. The chapter shows why learning from history, case studies, and past policy failures is harder than it looks: the past does not present itself neutrally once the ending is known.

## Key claims

The first claim is that hindsight does not simply add information to prior judgment. It reshapes the earlier situation by making the realized outcome seem more natural, more probable, and more obviously supported by the evidence than it actually was.

The second claim is that this distortion matters for explanation and evaluation. People who think an event was obvious after the fact may judge past actors too harshly, overstate their own understanding, and fail to appreciate the real uncertainty that had to be navigated.

The third claim is that hindsight weakens learning if it is not actively controlled. If the outcome feels inevitable, then the actual inferential difficulty of the original problem becomes invisible.

## Evidence and methods

The chapter is synthetic and reflective but anchored in experimental work on hindsight judgments. Its main contribution to the vault is to connect a classic bias phenomenon to the problem of historical understanding, evaluation, and methodological humility.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it changes how we read evidence after the fact. It matters for economics because market outcomes, crises, and policy failures are often narrated as if they should have been easy to foresee. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about explanation, responsibility, and historical knowledge. It matters for politics because democratic publics and institutions regularly evaluate past decisions under the illusion that the right path was clearer than it really was.

## Open questions

How should the vault distinguish hindsight bias from ordinary belief revision after receiving new information? When does hindsight mainly distort blame, and when does it mainly distort explanation? And how can policy institutions learn from past failures without letting retrospective inevitability flatten genuine uncertainty?
