# von Neumann and Morgenstern 1944 - Chapter 12 - Extensions of the Concepts of Domination and Solution

## Summary

This chapter revisits the concepts of domination and solution developed earlier in [[von Neumann and Morgenstern 1944]]. Its importance for the current vault is not that it introduces the familiar VNM utility theorem. That role belongs to [[von Neumann and Morgenstern 1944 - Appendix - The Axiomatic Treatment of Utility]]. Chapter XII instead shows that game-theoretic solution concepts are sensitive to the structure of the domination relation, to ordering assumptions, and to the way utility is made usable inside n-person games.

The chapter first generalizes domination by considering relations over a domain and asking what kinds of solution sets can be defined. It then examines complete ordering, partial ordering, acyclicity, strict acyclicity, relative maxima, and uniqueness of solutions. The later sections turn back to utility and explain why the earlier theory had treated utility as numerical and transferable: not because those assumptions are philosophically innocent, but because the machinery of zero-sum and n-person games requires them.

## Key claims

The first claim is that solution concepts depend on the properties of the domination relation. If domination is treated as an ordering-like relation, then existence and uniqueness questions turn on whether the relation is complete, partial, acyclic, or strictly acyclic.

The second claim is that acyclicity matters because cycles in domination can undermine the possibility of a stable solution. For finite domains, acyclicity is closely tied to uniqueness of solution; in broader settings the distinction between acyclicity and strict acyclicity becomes important.

The third claim is that the book's use of numerical utility is a technical necessity for its theory of games. The value of zero-sum two-person games depends on expectation, and expectation requires numerical utilities.

The fourth claim is that n-person game theory requires still stronger assumptions. Characteristic functions and imputations rely not only on numerical utility but also on the possibility of adding and transferring utility across players. The chapter explicitly recognizes that this is a narrow and somewhat dogmatic treatment of utility, adopted for technical reasons.

## Why this unit matters in the vault

This chapter is a good antidote to a lazy reading of classical rationality. It shows that formal rational-choice theory is built out of careful assumptions about order, comparison, domination, utility, and transferability. Those assumptions can be strengthened, weakened, or generalized, and different choices change what counts as a solution.

For the behavioral-economics side of the vault, the most important payoff is indirect. Later behavioral critique often begins by contrasting actual behavior with a benchmark. Chapter XII makes visible that the benchmark itself is not a single primitive idea. It is an engineered structure with moving parts.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this unit matters because social choice, bargaining, and institutional analysis often depend on what counts as a stable or admissible outcome. Chapter XII shows that those questions are not merely empirical. They depend on formal assumptions about comparison, domination, coalition values, and utility transfer.

The unit is also useful for political theory because it shows why multi-person rationality cannot simply be reduced to isolated individual maximization. Once coalitions and domination relations enter, the central question becomes what kinds of social arrangements can be stable under strategic pressure.
