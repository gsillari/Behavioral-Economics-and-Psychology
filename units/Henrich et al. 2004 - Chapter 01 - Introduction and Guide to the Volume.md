# Henrich et al. 2004 - Chapter 01 - Introduction and Guide to the Volume

## Core idea

Chapter 1 introduces the book as a collaborative project between experimental economics and anthropology. Its motivating contrast is between the standard economic assumption of self-interested rational actors and the experimental evidence that people often care about fairness, reciprocity, punishment, sharing, and the processes by which outcomes are generated.

For the current vault, the key point is that the editors do not simply export the [[Ultimatum game]] from the laboratory to the field. They ask whether experimental games can be combined with ethnographic knowledge to study the diversity of human sociality. The chapter therefore frames the book as a methodological and substantive extension of the [[Social preferences]] branch.

## What it adds

The chapter identifies the [[Ultimatum game]] as the canonical example of a laboratory result that challenges narrow self-interest. In standard theory, proposers should offer the smallest positive amount and responders should accept. In actual experiments, low offers are often rejected and proposers typically offer much more.

The book's innovation is to ask whether such findings generalize beyond university subjects in industrial societies. The editors organize the volume around fifteen small-scale societies and a set of games including ultimatum, dictator, public-goods, trust, and third-party punishment games. The chapter also makes clear that the project is not only economic: the field researchers bring ethnographic knowledge about local exchange, sharing, coalition, market exposure, and social norms.

## Open questions

How far can anonymous one-shot games travel across societies without changing meaning? What does it mean to measure a preference if participants interpret the game through local norms of sharing, exchange, or suspicion? When do experiments reveal stable social preferences, and when do they reveal culturally specific construals of the situation?
