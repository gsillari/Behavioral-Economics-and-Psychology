# Camerer and Fehr 2004 - Measuring Social Norms and Preferences Using Experimental Games (in Henrich et al. 2004, ch. 3)

## Core idea

Chapter 3 is the methodological heart of the first-pass ingest. Camerer and Fehr explain how [[Experimental games]] can be used to measure social norms and social preferences. The chapter treats games as both a language for describing strategic situations and a set of controlled measurement devices for altruism, reciprocity, inequity aversion, trust, punishment, and cooperation.

The chapter is important because it prevents the wiki's game pages from becoming a list of named experiments. Each game is a diagnostic instrument: the [[Ultimatum game]] measures rejection of unfair treatment, the [[Dictator game]] helps isolate altruism or sharing absent rejection, the [[Trust game]] measures trust and positive reciprocity, the [[Public goods game]] measures cooperation and free riding, and third-party punishment games measure norm enforcement by people not directly harmed.

## Key distinctions

The first distinction is between game theory as prediction and game theory as taxonomy. Formal game theory can predict play under assumptions about rationality and self-interest. But games can also classify social situations and make social mechanisms experimentally visible without assuming that the self-interest prediction is descriptively correct.

The second distinction is between altruism, reciprocity, and inequality aversion. Altruism is unconditional costly help. Reciprocity is conditional response to kindness or hostility. Inequality aversion is dislike of unequal payoff distributions. The chapter emphasizes that experimental games are useful partly because they help separate these mechanisms.

The third distinction is between the stark baseline and richer field interpretation. Anonymous one-shot play without communication is not meant to be lifelike. It is a control condition. Once that baseline is understood, researchers can add communication, identification, repetition, field context, or strategy-method designs to ask what changes.

## Why it matters for the wiki

This chapter should anchor [[Experimental games]] and strengthen [[Game theory]]. It also gives methodological support to the existing pages on [[Ultimatum game]], [[Dictator game]], [[Trust game]], [[Public goods game]], [[Costly punishment]], [[Reciprocity]], and [[Inequity aversion]].

The chapter also gives a caution that fits the whole vault: experimental games reduce social phenomena to simple structures, but this reduction is a platform for comparison, not a claim that real social life is simple. That is especially important in the cross-cultural setting of [[Henrich et al. 2004]].

## Open questions

Which social motives can be separated cleanly by simple games, and which remain entangled? How should researchers interpret results when participants frame the same material game through different local social meanings? When does a game measure a stable preference, a norm, a belief, or a situational construal?
