# Bar-Hillel 1982 - Studies of Representativeness (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 5)

## Summary

This chapter gives the representativeness branch something it previously lacked in the vault: a sustained internal review of how the mechanism works, why it was criticized, and which kinds of inferential error it helps explain. Maya Bar-Hillel treats representativeness not as a loose slogan but as a psychologically tractable relation between events and categories, then shows how that relation can diverge from formal probability.

In the current vault, this chapter is valuable because it deepens the representativeness branch without merely repeating the better-known overview pieces. It helps connect the early mechanism statement in [[Kahneman and Tversky 1972]] to the broader program in [[Tversky and Kahneman 1974]] by showing more carefully how similarity judgments and probability judgments come apart.

## Key claims

The first claim is that representativeness can be studied independently of probability judgment. The chapter resists the criticism that the concept is too vague to be useful by showing how similarity-based rankings can be elicited and compared with probability judgments.

The second claim is that representativeness creates two broad families of error. It can overweight variables that change perceived similarity without changing probability, and it can underweight variables that matter probabilistically but do not affect perceived fit.

The third claim is that these errors are not confined to one special task. They recur in judgments involving stereotypes, samples, and probabilistic expectations more generally. That makes the chapter especially useful for strengthening the internal architecture of [[Representativeness heuristic]].

## Evidence and methods

The chapter is review-based but grounded in concrete experimental material, including Bar-Hillel's own work on similarity and probability judgments. Its main methodological value is that it clarifies how one might assess representativeness separately from judged likelihood, rather than assuming the two collapse into one another.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it makes a core behavioral concept more precise. It matters for economics because many later belief distortions rest on exactly this kind of substitution of fit for formal inference. It matters for philosophy because it forces a question about what makes a probabilistic judgment rational: resemblance, coherence, or disciplined use of background frequencies. It matters for politics because stereotype-based reasoning, case salience, and sample misreading all depend on this basic inferential tendency.
