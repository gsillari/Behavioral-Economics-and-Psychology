# Choosing Not to Choose - Introduction - Choices

## Summary

The introduction frames the whole book through a series of policy problems in which institutions must decide between active choice and default rules. The core claim is that choice architecture is unavoidable, so the real question is not whether people will be steered, but how. Sunstein presents examples from organ donation, employee benefits, green energy, privacy settings, voting, and predictive shopping to show that the problem is general rather than domain-specific.

## Key claims

The first claim is that defaults and active choice are both forms of choice architecture. The second is that choice architecture is inevitable because social contexts always shape how options are presented and understood. The third is that active choosing has evident attractions for people who value freedom, but that this intuition needs to be tested against the costs of deciding and the possibility that people sometimes prefer delegation or reliance on defaults.

## Evidence and methods

This unit is programmatic rather than empirical. Its value is architectural: it lays out the problem-field that the later chapters investigate in more detail.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this introduction matters because it forces a foundational question for behavioral public policy: is freedom best protected by forcing people to choose, or by giving them good defaults plus the ability to opt out? It also shows how a seemingly technical design issue quickly becomes a dispute about autonomy, welfare, paternalism, and institutional legitimacy.

Is active choice ever neutral, or is it always itself a designed intervention? When does the inevitability of choice architecture justify paternalistic steering, and when does it merely make the problem harder?
