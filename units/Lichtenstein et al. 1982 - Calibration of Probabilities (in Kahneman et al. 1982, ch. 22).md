# Lichtenstein et al. 1982 - Calibration of Probabilities (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 22)

## Summary

This chapter gives the vault a major review anchor for calibration and confidence judgment. Lichtenstein, Fischhoff, and Phillips survey the literature up to 1980 on how well subjective probability judgments line up with eventual truth frequencies and show that confidence is often systematically miscalibrated. In the current vault, this chapter provides much-needed grounding for the overconfidence branch, which previously leaned too heavily on later field applications.

Its importance is that it connects subjective probability to empirical adequacy. The chapter does not ask only whether people hold beliefs, but whether expressed confidence tracks truth in a calibrated way across repeated judgments. That makes it a strong bridge between formal benchmarks and behavioral departures.

## Key claims

The first claim is that calibration is a distinct dimension of judgment quality. It is not identical to internal coherence or topic knowledge, even though it is related to both.

The second claim is that confidence judgments are often miscalibrated, especially when people express more certainty than subsequent truth frequencies justify. That gives the overconfidence branch a clearer conceptual basis than treating overconfidence only as self-enhancing belief.

The third claim is that calibration performance depends on task structure, feedback, response format, and training conditions. The chapter is therefore valuable not only as a diagnosis of error but also as a review of what makes judgment quality improve or deteriorate.

## Evidence and methods

This unit is review-based. It synthesizes experimental work on probability elicitation, confidence judgments, and the comparison between stated probabilities and realized outcomes. Its main methodological contribution to the vault is to make calibration a durable evaluative standard rather than a loose synonym for confidence.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it sharpens what it means to say that beliefs are good or bad. It matters for economics because forecasting, expert judgment, and probabilistic communication all depend on more than having a view; they depend on how well confidence tracks reality. It matters for philosophy because it distinguishes coherence, belief, and accuracy in a way that is often blurred. It matters for politics because institutions routinely rely on expert probabilities in areas such as risk, regulation, and public forecasting, where miscalibration can be costly.

## Open questions

How should the vault distinguish overconfidence in self-assessment from miscalibration in probabilistic judgment? Which kinds of feedback or elicitation formats genuinely improve calibration, and which only shift reported confidence? And how far should calibration be treated as a central benchmark in PPE-facing discussions of rational belief?
