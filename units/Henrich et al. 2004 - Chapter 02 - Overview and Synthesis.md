# Henrich et al. 2004 - Chapter 02 - Overview and Synthesis

## Core idea

Chapter 2 is the book's main cross-cultural synthesis. It argues that behavior in experimental games varies substantially across the fifteen small-scale societies studied, and that this variation is not well explained by a universal selfishness axiom or by a single universal replacement such as generic altruism or reciprocity.

The chapter is central for [[Cultural variation in social preferences]]. It preserves two claims at once: people often violate narrow self-interest in [[Ultimatum game]], [[Dictator game]], and [[Public goods game|public-goods]] settings, but the pattern of violation differs across societies and appears connected to local social and economic life.

## Main findings

The chapter reports that ultimatum-game behavior varies widely across groups. Some groups make low offers with few rejections, some make near-equal offers, and some make hyper-fair offers that may themselves be rejected. This means the classic student-population pattern is not the universal human baseline.

The chapter also reports that public-goods contributions conflict with the selfishness axiom across the societies studied, though again with variation across groups. The most important aggregate predictors are not stable individual traits such as sex, age, or wealth. Instead, group-level features appear more important, especially market integration and the local payoffs to cooperation.

The interpretive lesson is subtle. The chapter does not say that culture simply replaces economics. Rather, it argues that local institutions, livelihoods, and social interaction patterns shape how people construe experimental situations and what norms or preferences are activated. The same formal game may cue different everyday analogies in different societies.

## Why it matters for the wiki

This chapter deepens the existing [[Fairness]] and [[Social preferences]] architecture. The earlier behavioral-game sources show that material self-interest is too thin. This chapter adds that a single fairness parameter is also too thin. Fairness and reciprocity are real, but they are culturally and institutionally patterned.

It also matters for [[Game theory]] because it clarifies the difference between the formal game and the behavioral game. The experimenter controls the material payoffs, anonymity, and rules, but participants may still interpret the situation through local categories of sharing, suspicion, status, coalition, or market exchange.

## Open questions

Are cross-cultural differences in game play best understood as different stable preferences, different expectations about others, different learned norms, or different construals of the same experimental situation? How should behavioral economics model social preferences without replacing homo economicus with an equally over-simple homo reciprocans?
