# Lewis 1969 - Chapter 03 - Convention Contrasted

## Summary

Chapter 3 clarifies [[Convention]] by distinguishing it from nearby social phenomena. Lewis contrasts convention with agreement, social contract, norms, rules, conformative behavior, and imitation. The chapter is important because it prevents the concept from becoming a catch-all for any stable social pattern.

The main lesson is that conventions are neither reducible to explicit agreements nor identical with moral norms or formal rules. They are self-sustaining solutions to recurrent coordination problems. Agreements, rules, norms, and imitation may help create or support conventions, but they are not the same thing as convention.

## Key claims

The first claim is that convention does not require explicit agreement. Agreement can create expectations, but conventions can also arise through precedent, salience, and repeated successful coordination.

The second claim is that convention differs from social contract. A social-contract story may explain political obligation or institutional legitimacy, but Lewis's convention analysis is narrower and more strategic: it explains how regularities solve coordination problems.

The third claim is that convention differs from norms and rules. Norms may involve approval, disapproval, obligation, or sanctions; rules may be formulated explicitly. A convention can exist without being morally obligatory or legally codified, so long as the relevant expectation and incentive structure is in place.

The fourth claim is that convention differs from mere conformative behavior and imitation. People may copy others for many reasons. Convention requires that conformity be tied to the role of mutual expectation in solving a coordination problem.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it makes convention usable for institutional analysis. If every stable pattern is called a convention, the concept loses explanatory power. Lewis gives a sharper criterion: look for a recurrent coordination problem, alternative possible regularities, mutual expectations, and conditional reasons to conform.

This is why the chapter is a precursor to game-theoretic analyses of social institutions. It shows how institutions can be analyzed as equilibrium-like patterns without reducing them to explicit contracts, moral commands, or centralized enforcement.
