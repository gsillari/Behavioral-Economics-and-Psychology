# Hsee et al. 2007 - Preference Reversals Between Joint and Separate Evaluations of Options (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 9)

## Summary

This chapter extends the preference-reversal branch beyond the classic choice-versus-pricing setting. Hsee, Loewenstein, Blount, and Bazerman review reversals between joint and separate evaluation and explain them through the evaluability of attributes. Some features of an option are hard to assess in isolation but become easy to assess in comparison, which means that the same option can be ranked differently depending on how it is presented.

In the current vault, this chapter matters because it gives [[Preference reversals]] and [[Constructed preferences]] a more explicit mechanism. The relevant issue is not only that preferences move across procedures, but that different tasks change which attributes can be evaluated at all.

## Key claims

The first claim is that joint and separate evaluation can generate systematic preference reversals. An option that seems superior in isolation can lose when compared side by side with another option, or vice versa.

The second claim is that these reversals are often driven by evaluability. Attributes that are difficult to interpret on their own receive too little weight in separate evaluation and much more weight in joint evaluation.

The third claim is that these reversals are not confined to one unusual domain. They generalize across personnel decisions, consumer evaluation, disputes, and other multi-attribute choices.

## Evidence and methods

The chapter is a review and theoretical synthesis. It draws together multiple experimental paradigms and organizes them under the evaluability hypothesis. Its main contribution to the vault is therefore explanatory rather than archival: it shows why response mode and presentation mode can alter preference without assuming random noise.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it sharpens the challenge to simple revealed-preference reasoning. It matters for economics because it shows that preference depends partly on how alternatives are rendered comparable. It matters for philosophy because it complicates the assumption that normatively equivalent procedures should uncover the same underlying ranking. And it matters for politics and policy because public institutions often choose whether people evaluate options one at a time, comparatively, or through aggregated metrics.

## Open questions

When do joint evaluations improve judgment by making relevant trade-offs visible, and when do they distort judgment by overemphasizing easily comparable but low-experience attributes? Should the current vault treat evaluability as a mechanism inside constructed preferences or eventually as a standalone concept?
