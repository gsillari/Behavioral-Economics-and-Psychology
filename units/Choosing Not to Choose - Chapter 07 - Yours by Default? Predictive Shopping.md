# Choosing Not to Choose - Chapter 07 - Yours by Default? Predictive Shopping

## Summary

This chapter explores a stronger future-facing possibility: predictive shopping systems that know what people want before they choose it themselves. Sunstein uses this case to test the Hayekian argument for liberty by asking what happens when planners or platforms have near-perfect information about preferences.

## Key claims

The first claim is that active choosing in markets is often defended because planners lack the information needed to know what people want. The second is that sufficiently strong data systems could weaken that argument in some domains by reducing both decision costs and error costs. The third is that such systems might be attractive especially where choosing is burdensome and not pleasurable. The fourth is that even if predictive shopping is welfare-improving in some cases, it raises difficult questions about autonomy, legitimacy, and market design.

## Evidence and methods

This unit is partly thought experiment and partly market trend analysis. It uses examples from contemporary subscription and recommendation systems to explore a more automated future of consumer choice.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it links behavioral public policy to digital markets and the future of economic agency. It matters for economics because it revisits the informational argument for market choice. It matters for politics and philosophy because it asks whether autonomy still requires active choice when systems can predict preferences with high accuracy.

## Open questions

Would predictive shopping genuinely reduce error, or would it mostly shift power from choosers to platforms? At what point does personalized convenience become objectionable automation?
