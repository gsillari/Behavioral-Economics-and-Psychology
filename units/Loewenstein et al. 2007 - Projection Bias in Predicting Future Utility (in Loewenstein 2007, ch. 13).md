# Loewenstein et al. 2007 - Projection Bias in Predicting Future Utility (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 13)

## Summary

This chapter is the current vault's strongest direct source on [[Projection bias]]. Loewenstein, O'Donoghue, and Rabin argue that people systematically exaggerate the degree to which their future tastes will resemble their current tastes. That mistake matters because many economic decisions involve choosing now for later selves whose preferences will not match the chooser's present state.

In the current vault, this chapter upgrades projection bias from a DellaVigna survey label into a real explanatory mechanism with its own model, examples, and policy implications.

## Key claims

The first claim is that people project current tastes, needs, and feelings into the future more than they should. Present hunger, fatigue, arousal, weather, or mood can therefore distort predictions of future utility.

The second claim is that projection bias matters economically. It can produce mistaken durable-goods purchases, excessive early consumption, under-saving, and flawed decisions about long-run commitments.

The third claim is that the bias is not confined to small consumer mistakes. The authors explicitly push the idea toward larger domains such as marriage, divorce, fertility, suicide, and end-of-life choices.

## Evidence and methods

The chapter combines formal modeling with evidence from multiple domains rather than relying on a single experiment. Its contribution is to turn a familiar intuition about mispredicting future desire into a more general account of dynamic choice error.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it complicates the idea that present choice cleanly represents future welfare. It matters for economics because intertemporal decisions often depend on forecasts of future utility rather than only on discounting. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about which self should count in welfare analysis. It matters for politics and policy because cooling-off periods, delayed implementation, commitment devices, and consumer protections can all be understood partly as responses to projection bias.

## Open questions

How sharply can projection bias be separated from ordinary uncertainty about future conditions? When do people mispredict future utility, and when do they accurately predict it but give present preferences normative priority anyway?
