# Choosing Not to Choose - Chapter 05 - Choice-Requiring Paternalism

## Summary

This chapter contains one of the book's most distinctive claims: requiring active choice is often itself a form of paternalism. Sunstein argues that because some people prefer not to choose, institutions that force them to decide may be overriding their preferences rather than respecting them. The chapter also introduces the important distinction between hard requirements to choose and softer forms such as simplified active choosing.

## Key claims

The first claim is that paternalism and active choosing are not simple opposites. The second is that people may rationally prefer not to choose for many reasons, including limited bandwidth, fear of error, lack of information, and aversion to responsibility. The third is that mandatory active choosing can therefore count as nonlibertarian paternalism. The fourth is that simplified active choosing, where people can elect to rely on a default, may sometimes be the more attractive form.

## Evidence and methods

This unit is conceptual and normative, but it draws on behavioral ideas about present bias, unrealistic optimism, regret, responsibility, and bandwidth limitations. Its contribution is to reframe how policy debates about active choice should be understood.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it turns a familiar political intuition on its head. It matters for economics because it shows that decision costs are not a side issue. It matters for philosophy because it complicates the relation between freedom, autonomy, and responsibility. It matters for politics because many institutional reforms present active choice as anti-paternalist when it may actually be coercive in a subtler way.

## Open questions

When should policy treat nonchoice as a genuine preference and when as procrastination or confusion? Is simplified active choosing a stable compromise, or does it reproduce the same legitimacy problems in a milder form?
