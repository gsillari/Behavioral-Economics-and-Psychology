# Loewenstein et al. 2007 - Risk as Feelings (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 19)

## Summary

This chapter gives the current vault a direct statement of the idea that risky choice is often governed by emotion at the moment of decision rather than by detached calculation of probabilities and utilities. Loewenstein, Weber, Hsee, and Welch argue that cognitive evaluations of risk and affective reactions to risk can diverge sharply, and that the latter often drives behavior.

In the current vault, this matters because it strengthens the bridge between classic [[Risk perception]] work and the emotion side of behavioral economics.

## Key claims

The first claim is that most formal theories of choice under risk are cognitive and consequentialist. They treat behavior as the result of evaluating outcomes and weighting them by probability.

The second claim is that actual risky choice often depends on affective responses such as fear, dread, vivid imagery, anticipation, and physiological arousal. Those feelings may track probability only loosely.

The third claim is that risk attitudes should therefore be expected to vary across contexts more than consequentialist models imply. The same person can respond differently to risks depending on vividness, timing, and emotional framing.

## Evidence and methods

The chapter is a broad theoretical synthesis that draws on psychology, physiology, and decision research. Its main value for the vault is not one isolated empirical effect but a coherent alternative lens through which to read risky choice.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it pushes risk beyond statistics. It matters for economics because it offers a challenge to models that assume stable risk attitudes across domains. It matters for philosophy because it bears on the normative status of fear, dread, and emotion in judgment. It matters for politics and public policy because many controversies over technological, medical, or catastrophic risks are driven as much by feeling as by formal probability.

## Open questions

When should institutions treat emotional reactions to risk as distortions, and when should they treat them as morally and politically relevant information? How should risk analysis and risk communication change if feeling is part of risk rather than a contamination of it?
