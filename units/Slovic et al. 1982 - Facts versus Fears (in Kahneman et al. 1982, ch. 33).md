# Slovic et al. 1982 - Facts versus Fears (in Kahneman et al. 1982, ch. 33)

## Summary

This chapter gives the risk branch a major early anchor inside the blue book. Slovic, Fischhoff, and Lichtenstein argue that the assessment of risk is not a simple matter of reading off objective danger. Both experts and publics rely on judgment, but they often attend to different dimensions of a hazard, and those differences can have major implications for policy conflict and institutional legitimacy.

In the current vault, the chapter matters because it extends the risk branch beyond a single overview source. It helps connect availability, affect, dread, and probability neglect to the wider question of why perceived risk and expert assessment so often diverge.

## Key claims

The first claim is that risk assessment always contains a subjective component. Even technical analysis involves judgment about models, assumptions, evidence, and relevance.

The second claim is that laypeople are not simply making random mistakes. Their judgments track qualitative dimensions of hazards that formal expert assessment treats differently, including dread, catastrophe, controllability, and familiarity.

The third claim is that these judgmental patterns matter politically. If the public does not understand or trust expert claims, then risk management becomes a problem of communication, legitimacy, and institutional design rather than of calculation alone.

## Evidence and methods

The chapter is review-based and policy-facing. It synthesizes work on judgmental heuristics, availability, and psychometric research on hazards, then shows how those findings matter for social conflict over risk.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it makes risk perception a PPE topic in the full sense. It matters for economics because regulation and precaution depend on judged risk, not only actuarial estimates. It matters for philosophy because it challenges the idea that expert assessment cleanly settles what counts as a serious hazard. It matters for politics because public controversy over technology, health, environment, and security often turns on exactly the facts-versus-fears distinction named in the chapter.

## Open questions

When should public fear be treated as a distortion, and when as a legitimate signal about trust, vulnerability, or moral salience? How should institutions communicate risk when expert and public framings of the hazard are structured differently from the start?
