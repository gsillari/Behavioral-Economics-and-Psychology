# Loewenstein and Prelec 2007 - Anomalies in Intertemporal Choice - Evidence and an Interpretation (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 15)

## Summary

This chapter is the current vault's strongest formal statement of the idea that discounted-utility theory faces anomalies analogous to those that undermined expected utility in risky choice. Loewenstein and Prelec enumerate those anomalies and propose an intertemporal model that imports prospect-theoretic ideas such as reference dependence and a gain-loss value function into choice over time.

In the current vault, the chapter matters because it deepens [[Time inconsistency]] beyond a loose family of self-control effects. It shows that intertemporal choice can be behaviorally reconstructed in a systematic way rather than only criticized piecemeal.

## Key claims

The first claim is that discounted utility fails in patterned ways. People display present-oriented reversals, magnitude effects, sequence effects, and framing-sensitive intertemporal choices that a simple constant discount rate cannot explain.

The second claim is that many of these anomalies are parallel to expected-utility anomalies. That parallel matters because it suggests that the same broad behavioral rethinking that produced [[Prospect Theory]] is also needed for intertemporal choice.

The third claim is that at least some apparent variation in discounting can be reinterpreted as a feature of the value function and of reference-dependent evaluation rather than as a stable personal discount rate.

## Evidence and methods

This unit combines an anomaly review with a formal interpretive model. It is not just an inventory of strange findings. Its importance lies in showing how a prospect-theory-style architecture can travel from risky choice to temporally distributed outcomes.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it challenges one of the cleanest simplifying assumptions in economics: that time preference can be summarized by a stable discount parameter. It matters for economics because it links intertemporal anomalies to the broader behavioral turn away from thin utility models. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about temporal welfare, anticipation, and reference points. And it matters for politics because policy around savings, addiction, education, and commitment devices depends on how we understand choice over time.

## Open questions

How much of intertemporal inconsistency should be modeled as changing discounting, and how much as reference dependence or framing over time? Which parts of the Loewenstein-Prelec reconstruction deserve to be treated as central to behavioral economics rather than as an elegant side branch?
