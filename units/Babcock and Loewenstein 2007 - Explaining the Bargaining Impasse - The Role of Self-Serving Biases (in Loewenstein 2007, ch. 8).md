# Babcock and Loewenstein 2007 - Explaining the Bargaining Impasse - The Role of Self-Serving Biases (in Loewenstein 2007, ch. 8)

## Summary

This chapter gives the vault a strong behavioral account of why disputes fail to settle even when settlement is available. Babcock and Loewenstein argue that negotiators develop self-serving views of what is fair, reasonable, or likely to happen, and that these biased expectations can create bargaining impasse.

In the current vault, the chapter matters because it gives [[Self-serving bias]] a concrete and institutionally important form. It shows that behavioral distortion in negotiation is not only about greed or spite. It is also about sincerely held but role-dependent judgments of fairness.

## Key claims

The first claim is that disputants do not merely bargain strategically over a shared understanding of the facts. They often perceive the same case materials in systematically different ways depending on their role.

The second claim is that these biased judgments matter economically. The larger the gap between what each side thinks is fair or predicts a judge will do, the more likely bargaining is to break down.

The third claim is that self-serving bias is not just a laboratory curiosity. The chapter includes both experimental and field evidence and shows that some debiasing interventions can materially reduce impasse.

## Evidence and methods

The chapter combines laboratory negotiation experiments with field evidence from teacher contract negotiations. Its most important mechanism claim is that people weight evidence in a role-dependent way: plaintiffs and defendants, or unions and school boards, find different arguments compelling while preserving a sense of objectivity.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it sits at the intersection of psychology, law, and political economy. It matters for economics because it shows that negotiation failures can come from biased judgment, not only from incentives or asymmetric information. It matters for philosophy because it pressures the idea that fairness judgments are neutral moral assessments. And it matters for politics and public policy because labor disputes, mediation, and legal conflict often depend on exactly these mechanisms.

## Open questions

When should self-serving bias be treated as a special case of motivated reasoning, and when does it deserve to stand on its own? Which debiasing tools help in negotiation without simply advantaging the less reflective party?
