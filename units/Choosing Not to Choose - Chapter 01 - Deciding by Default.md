# Choosing Not to Choose - Chapter 01 - Deciding by Default

## Summary

This chapter is the book's behavioral core. Sunstein argues that default rules are often decisive because people tend not to alter them even when switching is easy. He uses cases such as double-sided printing, taxi-tip screens, retirement enrollment, and other familiar settings to show that defaults can outperform education and standard incentives in changing behavior.

## Key claims

The first claim is that default rules are sticky. The second is that this stickiness can arise from inertia, endorsement, loss aversion, and the way defaults define the status quo. The third is that default effects are not merely curiosities; they are often large enough to have real environmental, financial, and welfare consequences. The fourth is that institutions can use defaults more effectively than some traditional tools, but that this power makes the design of defaults normatively important.

## Evidence and methods

This unit is synthetic and example-driven. It assembles empirical findings across domains to explain why defaults matter and why their effects persist over time.

## Why it matters for PPE

For PPE students, this chapter matters because it gives the strongest practical case in the book for why default rules deserve serious attention. It matters for economics because it shows that low-cost design changes can alter behavior dramatically. It matters for philosophy because it raises questions about endorsement, manipulation, and the status quo. It matters for politics because it provides the empirical backbone for default-based public policy.
